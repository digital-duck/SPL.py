### 木 (24.1s)

## 木

**Picture this**: You're in a forest and spot one tree. Now imagine two trees standing together. What if you could write "forest" by putting your tree-picture twice? That's exactly how Chinese writing works!

**The story**: The character 木 means "tree" or "wood." It looks like a tree—a trunk with branches spreading out. Ancient Chinese writers noticed something clever: they could build new meaning by repeating things. Want to write "forest"? Stack two 木s:

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \text{林 (forest)}$$

Similarly, when a person (人) rests by a tree, you get 休—the meaning is painted right into the picture.

**The twist**: But 木 has a secret second job! In 沐 (to bathe), 木 is there only for its **sound**, not its meaning. The same character wearing different hats—like an actor playing different roles. In 林, 木 tells you *what* the word means. In 沐, it tells you how the word *sounds*.

**Now you try**: Why do you think copying 木 twice makes "forest"? Can you think of other things you'd count by repeating?

### 女 (27.7s)

## 女

Picture this: thousands of years ago, someone had to draw the word "woman." There were no alphabets—just pictures. They drew a figure kneeling, arms folded. This pose felt true: in the world of family and home, a woman resting, ready, present.

That kneeling figure became magic. Later writers took 女 and mixed it with other pictures to build new words—all about family, all about home.

Here's the connection. Watch what happens when you combine pieces:

$$\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = \underbrace{好}_{good}$$

A mother with her child is goodness itself. That's not random; it's how the ancient writer *saw the world*.

Look around and you'll spot 女 hiding in family words: 妹 (younger sister), 姐 (older sister), 妇 (wife). Each one whispers the same truth—*woman is the root of family*.

**Now you try:** If 女 means "woman" and you see it inside a word, what does that tell you about what the word might mean? Try guessing before looking it up.

### 人 (16.5s)

## 人

**Picture this.** Imagine watching someone walk toward you from far away. As they get closer, you can see their head at the top, their two legs below—but from that distance, you can't see their arms or face in detail. All you see is the shape: head on top, two legs moving below. That's exactly what the character 人 looks like! Ancient Chinese writers looked at real people and drew the simplest shape: a silhouette of a walking figure.

**Why does it feel right?** Because this character does one job brilliantly: it represents the idea of "person" in a way anyone can instantly recognize. It doesn't need to show a face or hands—just the essential shape that says "someone is here, and they're moving."

**How it builds other ideas.** Once you know 人 means "person," you can start spotting it inside bigger characters. When 人 appears in a character, it often hints at something about people, their actions, or their qualities. The character becomes a building block—a puzzle piece that helps you guess what a bigger character means. Add 人 to different pieces, and you can build words connected to people: resting, trust, goodness.

**Now you try.** Look at the other characters on this page and find ones that contain 人 hidden inside them. What do those characters mean? Does the "person" idea still make sense in each one?

### 子 (21.9s)

## 子

**Picture this:** You're wrapping a newborn baby in a blanket. What do you see? A tiny head poking out, with cloth bundled warmly around the rest of the body.

Long ago, when Chinese writers created their characters, they looked at this exact image and drew it. The character 子 shows a baby's head (the top part) and the swaddled body wrapped below it.

**Why this shape:** When you see 子, you can almost feel the smallness and warmth. The character *looks* like what it means — a child bundled safe and snug. That's the power of these old pictures: they help you remember instantly.

But 子 is clever in another way too. When you add 子 to another word, something magical happens: things become smaller, cuter, or more lovable. In English, we do this with "-ling" or "-let" (duckling, piglet). In Chinese, 子 carries that same feeling of *precious-small-ness*.

**Now you try:** What animals or objects would feel different if they were smaller? How would your drawing change to show something "mini" or "baby"?

### 宀 (32.6s)

## 宀

**Picture this:** You're playing outside when dark clouds roll in. You run inside and feel safe under the roof. That feeling of being sheltered and protected—that's exactly what 宀 means.

Look at the shape: 宀. It looks like a roof or canopy, doesn't it? Two walls holding up a ceiling, creating a safe space underneath.

**The big idea:** In Chinese writing, 宀 is a visual clue. Whenever you see it in a character, that character usually has something to do with homes, houses, rooms, or safety. It's like a label that says: "This word involves shelter or dwelling."

**How it works:** The character for "home" is 家. Look at it closely—you can see 宀 right on top like a roof! Below it are strokes representing family. So home really means family under shelter.

Other characters with 宀 work the same way: they mean rooms, buildings, peaceful places, or treasured things (precious things kept safely inside).

The radical is showing you a picture: whatever sits under this canopy is protected, settled, or belongs somewhere.

**Now you try:** Hunt for 宀 in other characters you know. When you spot it, what do you think that character might mean?

### 马 (24.5s)

## 马

**One Picture, Many Uses**

The character 马 is a picture of a horse. But here's the magic: it also sounds like "ma"—and that sound is so handy that it appears in tons of other characters.

When ancient Chinese writers needed to write 妈 (mother), they didn't invent a completely new picture. They borrowed the 马 and tucked it in, because 妈 is pronounced "ma." Then they did the same thing for 吗 (the question word)—also "ma." In both cases, the horse picture isn't really about horses anymore; it's a *sound-marker* saying "say ma here."

This is how phonetic borrowing works. A picture that starts out meaning one thing (horse) gets reused to help you pronounce another thing (mother, question particle). The same 马 shape helps signal the same sound across many different characters—almost like a stamp that says "*ma-sound goes here.*"

**Now you try:** Find 妈 and 吗 in a sentence. Do they sound the same? What other characters might hide 马 inside?

### 从 (12.2s)

## 从

**Picture this:** You're walking through a crowded market. Your little sibling holds your hand, staying right behind you, following your footsteps. You move forward—they move forward. You turn left—they turn left. They're not walking beside you as a friend; they're watching you and coming after.

**Why it makes sense:** The character 从 literally draws this picture. Two people in a line. One person in front, one person behind. The one behind isn't their own leader—they're following. One person, then another person moving after them, creates the whole idea of *following*.

**How it's built:**

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = 从$$

The second person comes *after* the first. It's not side-by-side friendship; it's a direction, a sequence. The character shows dependency: you go where the first person goes. That's what makes it "follow."

**Now you try:** Think of times you follow in real life. Following a recipe. Following a friend's dance. Following a teacher's directions. In each case, you're watching someone (or something) and doing what they do. Can you see how that's exactly what the character shows?

### 口 (42.5s)

## 口

**Picture this.** Your mouth is an opening. Through it, words come out when you speak. Food goes in when you eat. Questions burst out when you're curious. That opening—that gateway where inside meets outside—is what makes a mouth a mouth. In Chinese writing, ancient people drew this opening as a simple shape: a square or rectangle. That shape is the character 口 (mouth).

**Here's the intuition.** 口 looks like a frame or box because it shows an opening. When you see 口, think of a doorway or a window—a place where something can move from one side to the other. The shape is simple so you remember it easily.

**How it works.** The character 口 stands by itself to mean "mouth." It also appears inside larger characters, where it keeps that sense of an opening, a space, or a place where communication happens.

**Now you try.** If 口 means "an opening where things move in and out," what other openings in your world fit that description? A door, a window, a cave entrance—could they all be drawn as 口?

### 日 (41.8s)

## 日

**Picture this:** You're outside on a sunny day. If you needed to draw the sun quickly, what would you draw? A circle, maybe with dots or lines—something round and bright. That's exactly what ancient Chinese writers did. They wanted a symbol for "sun," so they drew what they saw: a glowing disk.

**Making it real:** To show that the sun glows, they added a mark inside the circle. But over time, as Chinese writing became organized and neat—with characters fitting into square grids—that round picture had to change. The circle became four straight lines. The brightness mark stayed inside. The result? 日—a sun disk, now squared.

**The composition:**
$$\underbrace{\text{sun disk}}_{\text{circle}} + \underbrace{\text{glow}}_{\text{mark}} = \text{日}$$

**Why this matters:** 日 means both "sun" and "day." Whenever you see this character inside other words, brightness and time are nearby.

**Now you try:** Look at 日 closely. Can you see the sun trapped in a square, with its brightness marked inside? Try creating your own picture-symbol for something bright—a star, a flame, a light bulb. What shape would you choose?

### 月 (22.1s)

## 月

Picture this: On a clear night, look up. The moon appears as a gentle crescent, thin and curved. Ancient Chinese observers saw this shape and called it 月 (yuè). Because the moon waxes and wanes, it became a natural symbol for cycles and change—something alive and rhythmic, not static.

But here's where it gets clever. The human body also cycles: it breathes, it grows, it moves. When Chinese writers needed a label to mark that a character meant something about bodies, bones, blood, or flesh, they grabbed the moon's shape. The same 月 that lights the night sky became the *flesh radical*—a visual stamp that whispers, "This word lives in the body."

Think of 月 like a name tag. On its own, it means the moon. But add it to another piece, and it means "body stuff." This one shape, two jobs—that's the genius of how Chinese builds complexity from simplicity.

Look around characters like 肉 (meat), 脚 (foot), or 肯 (willing—notice the body label inside?). See the moon shape hiding there? That crescent isn't random. It's the writer's way of saying: *This connects to something organic, something that grows and changes.*

**Now you try:** Next time you encounter a character with 月 in it, pause and ask: "Could this relate to bodies or things alive?"

### 林 (13.3s)

## 林

**Picture this:** You're walking through your neighborhood and you see a single tall tree. It's beautiful—one tree standing alone. But then you turn the corner and see three trees growing close together, creating a small grove where the branches intertwine and shadows dance. That grove feels different from one lonely tree. It feels like a *place* rather than just an object.

The ancient Chinese people noticed this too. One tree is 木 (mù). But when you put *two trees together* in writing, you get 林 (lín)—*woods*. Two trees make a group. It's that simple: repetition creates something new.

This is the LEGO rule of characters. You take the same piece twice and snap them together. Suddenly the meaning intensifies. One tree is a tree. Two trees become a *forest*—a whole new concept born from doubling.

**Here's the pattern:**

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \underbrace{林}{woods}$$

**Now you try:** What do you think would happen if you stacked *three* trees together? Does the meaning get even stronger?

### 水 (15.3s)

## 水

Picture a flowing stream on a hillside. Watch how it moves—sometimes fast, sometimes slow, but always seeking the lowest ground. Now imagine trying to draw this with just a few strokes of a brush. How would you show "water" in the simplest way possible?

The ancient Chinese artists had a brilliant idea: they drew three drops of water. Look at the character 水—it has a vertical line with three little bumps on the sides, like droplets falling. This shape captures something essential about water: it flows, it moves, it's always changing. A single drop isn't much, but three drops remind us that water gathers and moves together.

Here's the clever part. This simple three-drop shape appears *so often* in characters related to water that it became a marker—a visual clue. When you see 氵 (three drops) on the left side of any character, your brain knows: this word has something to do with liquids. River, ocean, ice, sweat, tears—they all use this water radical.

**Now you try:** Look around and find three liquid words. Can you guess which ones might have 氵 hidden inside them?

### 休 (10.4s)

## 休

Picture sitting outside on a hot day. You're tired. Then you spot a big tree with cool shade underneath. You lean against the trunk and feel the weight lift from your body. That moment—when tired becomes calm, and movement becomes stillness—that's the word we're exploring today.

In Chinese writing, the ancient people watched this exact scene happen again and again. Someone exhausted, a tree waiting. They asked: what picture captures the feeling of rest? The answer: put a person next to a tree, and you see rest happening.

The character 休 is built from two simpler pictures that snap together:

$$\underbrace{\text{人}}_{person} + \underbrace{\text{木}}_{tree} = \text{休}$$

When you see 休, you're looking at that same scene—someone finding strength by leaning, someone becoming whole again through stillness.

**Now you try:** Think of a place where *you* feel rested. Does it have a natural spot to lean or rest? What two simple pictures would snap together to show that feeling?

### 众 (11.8s)

## 众

Picture this: You're at recess, and you're playing alone. Then one friend joins you. Now you're a pair. Then another friend comes over. Suddenly, something changes—it's no longer just you plus friends; it's a *group*. You're part of something bigger.

The Chinese character 众 (zhòng) captures this exact feeling. It means "crowd" or "a gathering of people." And here's the clever bit: the character is made by stacking three people together.

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = \underbrace{众}_{crowd}$$

Why three? In Chinese, the number three often means "many" or "several"—it's the idea of *multiplicity*. One person is alone. Two people might be a pair or friends. But three people? That's when the magic happens. That's when you stop seeing individuals and start seeing a *crowd*—a unified group with its own energy and voice.

**Now you try:** Think about a time when you felt like you were part of a crowd. Did it feel different from being with just one or two people? What changed in how you acted or felt?

### 吗 (11.8s)

## 吗

**Picture this:** In English, if you want to ask a question, you have to rearrange words. "You like ice cream" becomes "Do you like ice cream?" But what if there were a tiny magic word you could just stick on the end to turn any sentence into a question instantly? Chinese has exactly that.

**How it works:** The character 吗 is a question particle—a word whose only job is to turn a statement into a question. Take any sentence, plop 吗 at the end, and boom: it's a question now. "你喜欢冰淇淋。" (You like ice cream.) becomes "你喜欢冰淇淋吗?" (Do you like ice cream?) The sentence doesn't change. Only the meaning changes.

**Why this shape?** Look at how 吗 is built:

$$\underbrace{口}_{\text{mouth/speech}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{ma sound}} = 吗$$

The 口 radical means "something spoken"—it's your signal that this is about speech and questions. The 马 gives the sound. Together, they whisper: "This sound marks a question you ask out loud."

**Spot the pattern:** Notice how 吗, 妈 (mother), and 马 (horse) all sound like "ma"—but each one looks different because each one means something different. That tiny 口 mouth in 吗 is the only difference between a question-maker and a regular sound.

**Now you try:** Can you think of a statement and imagine adding 吗 to it to make it a question?

### 好 (10.4s)

## 好

**Picture this**: What makes life feel truly good? If you had to draw one picture to answer that question, what would it be?

In ancient China, thousands of years ago, people thought deeply about this. They watched families and communities and asked themselves: what is the heart of wellbeing? When they looked for an answer, they saw something they kept seeing again and again—a woman caring for her child. Not wealth, not fame, not fancy things. Just that bond. A mother and child together, safe and connected. *That* was the picture of good.

So they made it into a character:

$$\underbrace{\text{女}}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{\text{子}}_{\text{child}} = \text{好 (good)}$$

For three thousand years, Chinese people have been writing this character. Every time they write it, they're keeping alive an ancient truth: good isn't about having everything. Good is about care, connection, and the people you hold close.

**Now you try**: Think of a moment when you felt completely happy. Was someone you love with you? What does that tell you about why this character looks the way it does?
