## Measurement

Measurement is the quantitative determination of a physical property, expressed as a numerical value paired with a unit. Every measurement takes one of two forms: a **direct measurement**, in which an instrument compares the property to a standard (using a ruler to find length, a balance to find mass), or a **derived measurement**, in which the value is calculated from other measured quantities using a known relationship. Density, for instance, is never read directly off a device — it is computed from measured mass and volume via $\rho = m/V$.

**Worked example.** Suppose you measure the mass of a metal block as $m = 47.6\ \text{g}$ using a balance, and its dimensions as $2.0\ \text{cm} \times 3.0\ \text{cm} \times 4.0\ \text{cm}$ using calipers. The volume is a derived quantity:

$$
V = 2.0 \times 3.0 \times 4.0 = 24\ \text{cm}^3
$$

The density is then a second derived quantity, built from the first:

$$
\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{47.6\ \text{g}}{24\ \text{cm}^3} \approx 1.98\ \text{g/cm}^3
$$

Comparing this to a reference table, you'd identify the metal as magnesium ($\rho \approx 1.74$–$1.98\ \text{g/cm}^3$ depending on alloy), illustrating how a chain of measurements supports an inference.

**Problem-solving application.** A crucial practical skill is tracking how uncertainty propagates through derived measurements. If each caliper reading carries an uncertainty of $\pm 0.1\ \text{cm}$, that uncertainty does not vanish when the dimensions are multiplied — it compounds. For a quantity $Q$ computed as a product or quotient of measured values, the relative uncertainties add:

$$
\frac{\delta Q}{Q} \approx \frac{\delta x}{x} + \frac{\delta y}{y} + \frac{\delta z}{z}
$$

Here each dimension has a relative uncertainty around $0.1/2.0 = 5\%$ to $0.1/4.0 = 2.5\%$, so the volume's relative uncertainty is roughly their sum — about $10\%$ — meaning the reported density should be written as $1.98 \pm 0.20\ \text{g/cm}^3$, not as a bare number. This is the discipline measurement demands: a value without a stated precision is scientifically incomplete, because it hides whether an apparent difference between two measurements is real or merely instrument noise. Recognizing when a derived result is trustworthy — and when it merely reflects accumulated measurement error — is a skill that recurs across every quantitative science.

## Observation

Observation is the deliberate act of noticing, measuring, and recording phenomena in the natural world. It is the foundational empirical act of science: before a hypothesis can be formed, tested, or discarded, something must first be seen, counted, timed, or otherwise registered as data. What separates scientific observation from casual noticing is systematicity — the observer specifies in advance what will be measured, how, and under what conditions, so that the record can be checked, repeated, and compared against other records.

Consider a simple worked example. A biology student wants to know whether a species of moth is more active at dusk than at midday. She sets up a fixed observation protocol: count moths landing on a lit trap every 15 minutes, for two hours at dusk and two hours at midday, on five separate days. The resulting counts are raw observational data — no interpretation yet, just numbers tied to a time and a condition. Only after this data exists can she ask whether the pattern is real or due to chance, which is a separate act of inference (statistics, later in this course) built on top of observation.

The problem-solving skill worth practicing here is designing an observation protocol that avoids two common failure modes: selection bias, where the method of observing systematically favors certain outcomes over others (e.g., only checking the trap when moths happen to be visible), and confounding, where an uncontrolled variable changes alongside the one being studied (e.g., dusk observations happening on cooler, calmer evenings than midday ones, so "time of day" and "temperature" are tangled together). A well-designed observation protocol fixes as many conditions as possible — same trap, same light, same recorder, same weather range — so that when a pattern shows up in the data, it can plausibly be attributed to the variable of interest rather than to how the data was collected.

Try this: suppose you are asked to observe whether traffic noise affects how long birds sing near a city park. Before recording a single measurement, write down exactly what you will count, how often, at which locations, and what you will hold constant. That written protocol — not the data itself — is the real test of whether your observation will support a meaningful conclusion later.

## Scientific Law

A scientific law is a concise statement, often written as a mathematical equation, that describes a pattern nature reliably follows. Unlike a hypothesis, a law is not a single guess to be tested once; unlike a theory, it does not explain *why* the pattern exists. A law simply and precisely describes *what happens*, distilled from repeated observation and measurement across many trials. Newton's second law, $F = ma$, doesn't explain why mass resists acceleration — that deeper explanation (inertia, spacetime, quantum fields) is left to theory. The law's job is narrower and more useful: given a force and a mass, predict the acceleration, every time, in any lab.

**Worked example.** Consider Boyle's Law: for a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely related:

$$
P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2
$$

A syringe holds 60 mL of air at 1.0 atm. If you push the plunger until the volume drops to 20 mL, what is the new pressure? Since $P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2$, we get $(1.0)(60) = P_2(20)$, so $P_2 = 3.0$ atm. The law converts a physical action (compressing gas) into a precise numerical prediction, without needing to explain gas behavior at the molecular level (that explanation — kinetic molecular theory — is a separate theory built later to justify the law).

**Problem-solving application.** Laws function as reusable computational tools: once validated, you plug in measured quantities and solve for the unknown. This is why laws are indispensable in engineering and experimental science — they let you predict outcomes *before* building or testing anything. Suppose an engineer designs a pressurized tank rated to survive up to 5.0 atm and needs to know the maximum safe volume when filling from a source at 2.0 atm and 15 L. Rearranging Boyle's Law: $V_2 = \dfrac{P_1 V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{(2.0)(15)}{5.0} = 6.0$ L. Any fill below 6.0 L keeps pressure under the safety limit. Note the boundary condition baked into the law's derivation: it holds only at constant temperature. Applying a law outside the conditions under which it was measured — extrapolating Boyle's Law to a heated tank, for instance — produces confidently wrong answers, a common source of real engineering error.
