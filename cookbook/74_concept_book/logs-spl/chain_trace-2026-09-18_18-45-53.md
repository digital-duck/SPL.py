### probability_amplitude (0.0s)

## Probability Amplitude

A quantum system does not carry a probability for each possible configuration the way a die carries a $\frac{1}{6}$ chance for each face. Instead, it carries a **probability amplitude** — a complex number $\psi$ assigned to each configuration (a position, a spin orientation, a path through a double slit). The amplitude itself is not observable; what connects it to experiment is **Born's rule**:

$$P = |\psi|^2 = \psi^* \psi$$

Squaring the modulus converts a complex number into a non-negative real number that behaves like a probability, and when you sum $|\psi|^2$ over all mutually exclusive outcomes, the total equals 1 (the amplitudes are "normalized").

**Worked example.** Suppose a particle can reach a detector via two paths, with amplitudes $\psi_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\psi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} e^{i\theta}$. If only path 1 is open, $P_1 = |\psi_1|^2 = \frac{1}{2}$. If only path 2 is open, $P_2 = |\psi_2|^2 = \frac{1}{2}$. Classically, with both paths open, you'd expect $P_1 + P_2 = 1$. Quantum mechanically, you must add the *amplitudes* first, then square:

$$P = |\psi_1 + \psi_2|^2 = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + 2\,\text{Re}(\psi_1^* \psi_2) = 1 + \cos\theta$$

The cross term $2\,\text{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2)$ is the interference term, and it exists only because $\psi$ carries a phase — an imaginary part. If amplitudes were forced to be real and non-negative, this term would vanish and quantum mechanics would collapse into ordinary probability theory. The imaginary part is not a bookkeeping convenience; it is the mathematical carrier of relative phase, and relative phase is what makes interference constructive ($\theta = 0$, $P=2$) or destructive ($\theta = \pi$, $P=0$) at a given detector location.

**Problem-solving application.** To predict an experimental outcome: (1) identify every indistinguishable path or configuration leading to that outcome, (2) assign each a complex amplitude, (3) sum the amplitudes if the paths are indistinguishable (interference applies), or sum the probabilities if a measurement has distinguished them (interference is destroyed), and (4) apply Born's rule at the end, never before. This ordering — amplitudes first, modulus-squared last — is the single most common point of error when translating a quantum scenario into a calculation.

### quantum_state (0.0s)

## Quantum State

The complete description of a quantum system is a vector $|\psi\rangle$ in a complex vector space called Hilbert space. This vector — the state vector — encodes everything that can, even in principle, be known about the system. Unlike a classical particle, whose state is a definite position and momentum, a quantum state is generally a superposition: a linear combination of possible outcomes, written

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i |i\rangle$$

where the $|i\rangle$ are basis states (e.g., possible measurement outcomes) and the complex coefficients $c_i$ are probability amplitudes. The state is normalized, $\sum_i |c_i|^2 = 1$, since the outcome probabilities $|c_i|^2$ must sum to 1. Before measurement, the system does not "secretly" have one definite value hidden from us — it genuinely exists in the superposition itself.

**Worked example.** Consider a qubit — the quantum analog of a bit — with basis states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. The state

$$|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle$$

is a valid quantum state because $\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 = 1$. Measuring this qubit yields $0$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ and $1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ — not because the qubit was secretly one or the other, but because measurement forces the superposition to "collapse" into one basis outcome, with the amplitudes fixing the odds.

**Problem-solving application.** Given a proposed state $|\phi\rangle = a|0\rangle + b|1\rangle$ with $a = 0.6$ and $b = 0.9$, is it physically valid? Check normalization: $a^2 + b^2 = 0.36 + 0.81 = 1.17 \neq 1$. It fails — this is not a legitimate quantum state, and no measurement probabilities could be computed from it as written. To fix it, rescale by dividing both coefficients by $\sqrt{1.17}$, restoring $\sum |c_i|^2 = 1$. This normalization check is the first diagnostic step in any problem involving quantum states: before computing measurement probabilities, expectation values, or how a state evolves under an operator, confirm the vector is properly normalized, since every physical prediction is built on top of it.

### observable (0.0s)

## Observable

An observable is a physical quantity that can, in principle, be measured on a quantum system — position, momentum, energy, spin, angular momentum. In classical mechanics an observable is just a function of the system's state that returns a number. In quantum mechanics an observable is represented by a **Hermitian operator** $\hat{A}$ acting on the system's state vector $|\psi\rangle$ in Hilbert space. Hermiticity ($\hat{A} = \hat{A}^\dagger$) is not a formal nicety — it is what guarantees that the operator's eigenvalues are real numbers, since a measurement outcome (a meter reading, a detector click) must be a real number, never a complex one.

The eigenvalue equation for an observable is

$$\hat{A}|a_i\rangle = a_i|a_i\rangle$$

where $|a_i\rangle$ is an eigenstate of $\hat{A}$ and $a_i$ is the corresponding eigenvalue. The postulate of quantum measurement states that the *only* possible results of measuring $\hat{A}$ are its eigenvalues $a_i$, and that immediately after a measurement yielding $a_i$, the system's state collapses into the eigenstate $|a_i\rangle$.

**Worked example.** The energy observable is the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$. For a particle in an infinite square well of width $L$, solving $\hat{H}|\psi_n\rangle = E_n|\psi_n\rangle$ gives the discrete eigenvalues

$$E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Any energy measurement on this particle must return one of these $E_n$ values — never something in between. This is the direct link between the mathematical spectrum of $\hat{H}$ and the physically observed quantization of energy.

**Problem-solving application.** Given an operator, the practical task is to find its eigenvalues, since those are the measurable predictions. For the spin-$z$ observable $\hat{S}_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_z$ with $\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$, solving $\det(\hat{S}_z - \lambda I) = 0$ gives $\lambda = \pm\hbar/2$: the two possible outcomes of a spin measurement, matching the "spin-up/spin-down" results seen experimentally. This procedure — diagonalize the Hermitian operator, read off the eigenvalues as possible outcomes — is the general method for predicting what any observable can yield.

### planck_constant (0.0s)

## Planck Constant

Every quantum mechanical calculation contains a number roughly equal to $6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J·s}$. This is Planck's constant, $h$, and it is not merely a conversion factor — it is the fundamental unit of "action" (energy multiplied by time) at which classical, continuous descriptions of nature break down and discrete, quantized ones take over.

Planck introduced $h$ in 1900 to solve the blackbody radiation problem: classical physics predicted that a hot object should radiate infinite energy at short wavelengths (the "ultraviolet catastrophe"), which is absurd and contradicted every measurement. Planck found that if electromagnetic energy is emitted only in discrete packets, or quanta, of size

$$
E = h\nu
$$

where $\nu$ is the radiation's frequency, the theoretical spectrum matches experiment exactly. This equation is the first quantization condition, and it recurs throughout quantum theory in different guises: the photoelectric effect ($E = h\nu$ for photon energy), the Bohr model's quantized angular momentum ($L = n\hbar$, with $\hbar = h/2\pi$), and de Broglie's matter-wave relation ($p = h/\lambda$).

**Worked example.** Compare the photon energy of red light ($\nu \approx 4.3 \times 10^{14}\text{ Hz}$) to visible violet light ($\nu \approx 7.5 \times 10^{14}\text{ Hz}$):

$$
E_{\text{red}} = h\nu_{\text{red}} = (6.626\times10^{-34})(4.3\times10^{14}) \approx 2.85\times10^{-19}\text{ J} \approx 1.78\text{ eV}
$$

$$
E_{\text{violet}} = h\nu_{\text{violet}} \approx 3.10\text{ eV}
$$

This directly explains why violet light, not red, ejects electrons from many metals in the photoelectric effect: energy per photon scales with frequency, not intensity, and $h$ is the proportionality constant linking the two domains.

**Problem-solving application.** The smallness of $h$ explains why quantum effects are invisible at everyday scales: for a macroscopic object, the associated de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ is astronomically small compared to the object's size, so wave-like quantization is undetectable. Given a $0.145\text{ kg}$ baseball at $40\text{ m/s}$, $\lambda = h/(mv) \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-34}\text{ m}$ — far smaller than any atom, confirming why classical mechanics suffices for ordinary objects but quantum mechanics is essential at atomic scales.

### wave_function (0.0s)

## Wave Function

The wave function $\psi(x,t)$ is the position-space representation of a quantum state — a complex-valued function that encodes everything that can be known about a particle's location and its evolution in time. It is built from two ideas already in hand: the state itself is a vector in an abstract Hilbert space, and $\psi(x,t)$ is what results when that vector is projected onto the position basis, giving a probability amplitude at each point $x$. The physically meaningful quantity is not $\psi$ directly but its squared modulus, $|\psi(x,t)|^2$, which is a probability *density*: the probability of finding the particle in a small interval $[x, x+dx]$ at time $t$ is $|\psi(x,t)|^2\,dx$. Because the particle must be found *somewhere*, $\psi$ is normalized so that

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x,t)|^2\, dx = 1.$$

**Worked example.** Consider the (unnormalized) Gaussian wave packet $\tilde\psi(x) = e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$. To normalize it, compute $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}\,dx = \sigma\sqrt{\pi}$, using the standard Gaussian integral. The normalization constant is therefore $A = (\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}$, giving

$$\psi(x) = \left(\pi\sigma^2\right)^{-1/4} e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}.$$

Here $|\psi(x)|^2$ is itself a Gaussian of standard deviation $\sigma$, so $\sigma$ directly sets the spatial uncertainty in the particle's position — a first hint of the uncertainty principle, since a narrow $\psi(x)$ (small $\sigma$) corresponds to a broad spread in momentum once $\psi$ is Fourier-transformed to momentum space.

**Problem-solving application.** The normalization integral is the one tool this section adds, and it does double duty. Fixing the overall scale of $\psi$ is just the special case where the integration region is the entire line; the same integral, restricted to a finite interval, answers the more common question "what is the probability the particle is found here?" For the normalized Gaussian above, the probability of finding the particle in $(0, L)$ is $P(0 < x < L) = \int_0^L |\psi(x)|^2\,dx$ — no new machinery, just a different limit of integration on the same $|\psi|^2$. This pattern — normalize once, then integrate $|\psi|^2$ over whatever region the question asks about — is the core skill to master before moving on to how operators extract other physical quantities (momentum, energy) from $\psi$ in later sections.

### schrodinger_equation (0.0s)

## Schrodinger Equation

The Schrödinger equation, $i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi\rangle$, is the law of motion for quantum states. Given the wave function $|\psi\rangle$ at any instant, this equation tells you exactly what it becomes an instant later. It is linear (a sum of solutions is a solution) and first-order in time (only $\partial|\psi\rangle/\partial t$ appears, not $\partial^2|\psi\rangle/\partial t^2$), so a single initial condition $|\psi(0)\rangle$ determines the entire future trajectory of the state — deterministically, as long as no measurement intervenes. The operator $\hat{H}$, the Hamiltonian, represents total energy; it is what generates time evolution, just as force generates acceleration in Newtonian mechanics.

**Worked example.** Consider a system with a time-independent Hamiltonian and an initial state that happens to be an eigenstate of $\hat{H}$: $\hat{H}|\psi\rangle = E|\psi\rangle$, with $E$ the energy eigenvalue. Substituting into the Schrödinger equation gives $i\hbar \frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = E|\psi(t)\rangle$, a simple first-order differential equation whose solution is
$$|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iEt/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle.$$
The state only picks up a phase factor — its physical content (probabilities of measurement outcomes, computed from $|\langle \phi|\psi\rangle|^2$) never changes. This is why energy eigenstates are called stationary states: probability distributions frozen in time even though the state vector itself is rotating in phase.

**Problem-solving application.** For a general initial state, decompose $|\psi(0)\rangle$ into the Hamiltonian's eigenbasis: $|\psi(0)\rangle = \sum_n c_n |n\rangle$, where $\hat{H}|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$. Because the equation is linear, each component evolves independently by its own phase factor, giving
$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n\, e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle.$$
This is the standard technique for solving the time-dependent equation: diagonalize $\hat{H}$, expand the initial state in that basis, attach a phase $e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}$ to each term, and reassemble. It converts a differential equation into pure bookkeeping — the reason eigenvalue problems dominate the practical side of quantum mechanics.

### energy_quantization (0.0s)

## Energy Quantization

When a particle is confined — bound by a potential well rather than free to roam — the Schrödinger equation only admits solutions for certain discrete energy values. This is energy quantization: the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian operator $\hat{H}$ form a discrete spectrum rather than a continuum. It is not an extra rule bolted onto quantum mechanics; it falls directly out of requiring the wavefunction to satisfy boundary conditions.

**Why boundaries force discreteness.** The time-independent Schrödinger equation $\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$ is a differential equation. For a free particle, any $E \geq 0$ yields a valid, normalizable-enough solution — energy is continuous. But once you confine the particle (say, $\psi = 0$ at two walls), you are asking for solutions that satisfy that equation *and* vanish at both boundaries simultaneously. Most values of $E$ produce solutions that blow up or fail to meet the second boundary. Only a special, discrete set of $E$ values thread the needle. This is exactly analogous to a guitar string fixed at both ends: only certain wavelengths fit, producing a discrete set of allowed harmonics — energy quantization is the quantum-mechanical version of standing-wave resonance.

**Worked example: particle in a box.** For a particle confined to $0 \le x \le L$ with infinite walls, solving $\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$ subject to $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$ gives sinusoidal solutions $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$, valid only for positive integers $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Substituting back yields the allowed energies:

$$E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$$

The quantum number $n$ labels the "rungs" of this energy ladder. Note two consequences: the ground state ($n=1$) has energy strictly greater than zero — there is no state of zero energy, a direct result of the uncertainty principle forbidding a perfectly localized, motionless particle — and the spacing between adjacent levels, $E_{n+1} - E_n$, grows with $n$, so the ladder rungs spread apart rather than staying evenly spaced.

**Problem-solving application.** Given $L$ and $m$, you can compute $E_n$ directly, or invert the formula to find which $n$ corresponds to a measured energy, or find the photon energy $\Delta E = E_{n_f} - E_{n_i}$ emitted when the particle drops between levels — the same logic used to predict spectral lines in atoms, where a Coulomb potential replaces the box walls but discreteness survives for the same boundary-condition reason.

### quantum_tunneling (12.4s)

## Quantum Tunneling

A particle described by a wave function does not simply stop where classical physics says it must. If a particle with total energy $E$ encounters a potential barrier of height $V$ and width $L$, classical mechanics forbids it from ever appearing on the far side when $E < V$: it lacks the kinetic energy to climb over. Quantum mechanics disagrees. Solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation inside the barrier gives a wave function that does not vanish there — it decays exponentially, but it is nonzero throughout, and reconnects to an oscillating (nonzero-amplitude) wave function on the other side. That residual amplitude means there is a genuine, calculable probability of finding the particle beyond the barrier: tunneling.

**Worked example.** Inside the barrier the wave function satisfies $\psi(x) \propto e^{-\kappa x}$, where the decay constant is
$$
\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V-E)}}{\hbar}.
$$
For a barrier of width $L$, the transmission probability (fraction of particles that tunnel through) is approximately
$$
T \approx e^{-2\kappa L}.
$$
Notice the exponential sensitivity: doubling $L$, or increasing $V - E$, drives $T$ toward zero rapidly, while thin or low barriers still let a measurable fraction through. This is why tunneling is significant at atomic and nuclear length scales ($L \sim 10^{-10}$ to $10^{-14}$ m) but utterly negligible for a baseball approaching a wall.

**Problem-solving application.** This formula is the working tool for three phenomena you'll meet across physics: alpha decay, where a helium nucleus tunnels out of the strong-force potential well confining it — Geiger and Nuttall's empirical half-life law is really $T$'s exponential dependence in disguise; stellar fusion, where two protons in the Sun's core have far too little kinetic energy to overcome their mutual Coulomb repulsion classically, yet tunnel together often enough to power the Sun (the Gamow factor is exactly $e^{-2\kappa L}$ applied to that Coulomb barrier); and tunnel diodes, engineered semiconductor junctions thin enough that electrons tunnel across on demand, producing fast switching used in electronics. In each case, the same calculation — estimate $\kappa$ from the barrier's height and the particle's energy deficit, then exponentiate — tells you whether the "impossible" process is rare or routine.

### semiconductor_devices (12.7s)

## Semiconductor Devices

A pure crystal like silicon has a valence band (filled with electrons) separated from a conduction band (mostly empty) by a band gap of about 1.1 eV — too large for room-temperature thermal energy ($k_BT \approx 0.026$ eV) to routinely bridge, but small enough that engineering can control conduction precisely. **Doping** — substituting a small fraction of atoms with different valence — creates the two building blocks of every semiconductor device. Phosphorus (5 valence electrons) donates an extra electron to silicon's lattice, producing n-type material with mobile negative carriers. Boron (3 valence electrons) leaves a missing bond, a "hole" that behaves as a mobile positive carrier, producing p-type material.

**Worked example — the p-n junction.** Join a p-type region to an n-type region. Electrons diffuse from n to p, holes diffuse from p to n, and where they meet they recombine, leaving behind fixed dopant ions. This creates a depletion region with no free carriers and a built-in electric field pointing from n to p. That field opposes further diffusion, and the junction reaches equilibrium. Apply a forward voltage (positive terminal to the p-side): the external field cancels the built-in field, carriers flow, and current passes easily. Apply a reverse voltage: the depletion region widens and current is blocked (aside from a tiny leakage current). This asymmetry is what makes a diode — the junction rectifies, converting AC to DC.

**Application — the transistor.** Sandwich three doped regions to form n-p-n or p-n-p layers: this is a bipolar junction transistor (BJT), or in modern chips, a MOSFET, where a gate electrode's voltage creates or removes a thin conducting channel between source and drain terminals via the field effect. A small signal at the gate controls a much larger current between source and drain — amplification and switching in one device. Because the gate is separated from the channel by an insulating oxide layer only a few nanometers thick, electrons can quantum-mechanically tunnel through it if the layer is made too thin, which is exactly the leakage mechanism engineers must budget for as transistors shrink below 5 nm. A billion of these switches, each exploiting band structure, doping, and tunneling limits, are wired together on a single chip to execute the logic of every computer — quantum mechanics is not an abstraction here but the literal mechanism running your laptop.
