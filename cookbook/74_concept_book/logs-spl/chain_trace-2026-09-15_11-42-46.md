### 宀 (8.5s)

## 宀

Picture a peaked roof stretched over a small space — that's exactly what 宀 draws: two slanted strokes meeting at a point, with a short stroke crossing them like eaves. On its own, 宀 isn't a word you'd ever say out loud. It's a radical, a building-block shape that shows up *inside* other characters, always sitting on top, always doing the same job: marking that something below is covered, protected, or settled in one place.

Once you start noticing 宀, you'll see it everywhere characters describe home life. Take 家 (jiā, "home" or "family"). Strip away the roof and you're left with 豕 (shǐ), an old picture of a pig — because in ancient farming households, a home was a roofed place where livestock lived alongside people.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{豕}_{\text{pig}} = 家 \; (\text{home, family})
$$

The same pattern holds in 安 (ān, "peaceful, safe") — roof over 女 (a woman/person), suggesting safety found *under shelter*. And in 室 (shì, "room") — roof over 至 (arrival), a space you arrive at and settle into.

The rule to remember: whenever you see 宀 perched at the top of a character, expect the character's meaning to involve a house, a room, or a state of being sheltered — even if the meaning has drifted to something abstract like "peace" or "contentment."

**Practice:** The character 宁 is built from 宀 (roof) plus a stroke suggesting something resting quietly inside. Given what 宀 usually signals, guess what general *category* of meaning 宁 might belong to before looking it up — then check if you're right.

### 女 (9.9s)

## 女

You already know 女 as a picture — a kneeling figure, one of the oldest ways early Chinese writing captured "woman" or "female." That picture didn't stay locked in its own box, though. Once a shape like this existed, ancient scribes reused it as a building block for other characters, especially ones about family, relationships, and people connected by birth or marriage.

This is where 女 becomes more than a single word — it becomes a *semantic anchor*, a piece of meaning that shows up inside other characters to signal "this is about a woman, a relationship, or family." When you see 女 tucked into a more complex character, it's a clue pointing toward that meaning, even before you know the whole character.

For example, "mother" builds directly on this anchor:

\[
\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sound cue}} = 妈 \ (\text{mother})
\]

Here, 女 supplies the meaning ("something to do with a woman/family role"), while the added piece often nudges the pronunciation. This split — one part for meaning, one part for sound — is one of the most common patterns in Chinese character-building, and 女 is one of the clearest examples of a meaning-anchor you'll meet again and again.

**Try it yourself:** Below are three characters that all contain 女: 姐 (older sister), 妹 (younger sister), and 好 (good). Based on what you just learned, which one do you think *doesn't* fit the "family relationship" pattern as directly as the other two — and why might it still include 女?

### 子 (8.3s)

## 子

You already know 子 as "child" — you've seen it in words like 孩子 (child) or maybe 儿子 (son). But look closer at the character itself: it started as a picture of a swaddled infant, with a big round head and two little arms, wrapped tight in a blanket. That's the whole image compressed into a few strokes.

Here's where 子 gets more interesting: it doesn't only mean "child" as a standalone word. It also works as a **suffix** — a small piece added to the end of other characters to turn them into nouns, especially names for everyday objects. Think of it like the English "-er" in "teach" + "-er" = "teacher," except 子 doesn't tell you *what kind* of noun, just that it *is* one.

$$
\underbrace{桌}_{table} + \underbrace{子}_{noun\ suffix} = 桌子 \ (table)
$$

$$
\underbrace{椅}_{chair} + \underbrace{子}_{noun\ suffix} = 椅子 \ (chair)
$$

Notice the pattern: 桌 and 椅 already carry meaning on their own, but adding 子 makes them feel complete and natural as everyday nouns — the way a bare stem word sometimes needs an ending to sound "finished" in a sentence. This is 子's second life: the picture of a baby became a grammatical tool, riding along on other characters to shape them into names for things you can point at.

### 字 (25.7s)

## 字

You already know that a 字 (zì) is a Chinese character — one written "word" in the script. But look closer at the character for "character" itself, and it tells you its own secret origin story.

字 is built from two pieces stacked together: 宀, a roof, sitting over 子, a child (a character you likely already know).

$$\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = \underbrace{字}_{character}$$

Why a child under a roof? Because that's exactly how the ancient Chinese thought about new characters — they weren't invented out of nowhere. They were "born" from existing pieces and then "raised" inside a structure, the way a child is born into a family and sheltered by a home. In this picture, a radical is the parent-piece, and the new character it helps form is the child-piece, growing up under the roof of that meaning-family.

This is such a central idea in the script that it named the whole system: characters that grow other characters are studied as **ZiNets** — literally "字-networks" — webs of parent characters branching into child characters, generation after generation.

Once you notice this pattern, you'll start seeing family trees everywhere in Chinese writing: one root character quietly fathering a dozen descendants, each one borrowing a piece of the parent's shape or sound.

**Practice:** 宀 (roof) shows up in other characters too, not just 字. Think of one more character you know that has 宀 sitting on top of it, and draw its own composition equation like the one above, labeling each piece with its meaning or role.

### 安 (14.9s)

## 安

You already know 安 (ān) as "peace" — the feeling of things being settled and safe. But look closer at how the character is built, and that feeling starts to make visual sense.

The character stacks two pieces: 宀 (a roof — picture a simple triangle shape covering something) and 女 (woman). Put them together, and you get a picture of a woman sheltered under a roof — someone protected, indoors, out of danger. That image *is* the meaning. Ancient Chinese writers didn't explain peace with an abstract symbol; they built it out of a scene you could picture.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = \underbrace{安}_{\text{peace, settled, at home}}$$

This is worth noticing as a pattern: many Chinese characters aren't random shapes to memorize — they're small stories, built from simpler pieces that each carry meaning. Learning to recognize these building-block pieces, called *radicals*, is one of the fastest ways to guess at an unfamiliar character's meaning before you even look it up. Once you know 宀 as "roof," you'll start spotting it in other characters about houses, family, and shelter.

You can also see 安 at work in real words: 平安 (píng ān, "safe and sound") and 安全 (ān quán, "safety, security") both carry this same "sheltered, secure" feeling forward.

**Practice:** Look at the character 字 (zì, "character/word"), which is also built from 宀 plus another piece: 子 (child). Using the same logic as 安, write out the decomposition equation for 字, and guess in one sentence why "roof + child" might mean "word" or "character." (Hint: think about what happens to a child under a roof — at home, being taught.)

### cultural_etymology (14.7s)

## Cultural Etymology

Every Chinese character is a tiny archive. Long before dictionaries, scribes carved characters into bone and cast them into bronze — and many of those ancient shapes survive almost unchanged in the characters you write today. Reading a character's history is less like studying vocabulary and more like excavating a site: the oldest layers are still visible if you know where to dig.

Take 安 ("peace, safety"). Its oldest forms show a roof (宀) with a woman (女) beneath it — a home where a person is sheltered. The character doesn't just mean "peace"; it pictures the Bronze-Age idea of what peace *was*: a roof over your head, protection, a settled household. You can write the logic as a build-up chain:

$$
女 \xrightarrow{+\text{roof }(宀)} 安
$$

The same roof shape (宀) shows up again and again across the writing system, always carrying a hint of "shelter" or "home." Take 字 itself — the word for "character." Its etymology describes the writing system's own logic: a roof (宀) over a child (子), meaning something "born" or "produced" under shelter, extended to mean characters "born" from combining simpler roots. In other words, 字 is a character that explains, in its own structure, how characters are made:

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof, shelter}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \underbrace{字}_{\text{character, born of simpler parts}}
$$

This is why etymology in Chinese feels like archaeology you can actually read — no shovel required, just attention to the shapes.

**Practice:** The character 好 ("good") combines 女 (woman) and 子 (child). Based on what you now know about how ancient scribes built meaning from combined images, write one sentence guessing why "woman + child" might have come to mean "good."
