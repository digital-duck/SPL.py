## Neuron

A neuron is a eukaryotic cell specialized for rapid electrical and chemical signaling, forming the fundamental unit of the nervous system. Its architecture divides labor across three regions: dendrites, branching extensions that receive incoming signals from other neurons; the soma (cell body), which houses the nucleus and organelles and integrates those incoming signals; and the axon, a long projection that carries an outgoing signal away from the soma toward target cells. Like other eukaryotic cells, the neuron relies on standard organelles — mitochondria for ATP production, ribosomes and endoplasmic reticulum for protein synthesis — but it deploys them toward a distinctive purpose: sustaining a cell whose membrane can rapidly change its electrical state and whose shape can span, in some human motor neurons, over a meter.

Consider a sensory neuron in your fingertip that detects a hot stove. Heat-sensitive receptors on the dendrites convert (transduce) thermal energy into small electrical changes. If enough dendrites are stimulated at once, the soma integrates these inputs and triggers an action potential — a brief, self-propagating reversal of the membrane's electrical charge — which races down the axon. At the axon's end, this electrical signal triggers the release of chemical messengers (neurotransmitters) into a synapse, the junction with the next neuron, propagating the signal onward until it reaches the spinal cord and brain, producing the reflex to pull your hand back.

This structure directly solves a problem that plain diffusion cannot: getting a signal across long distances quickly and reliably. A molecule diffusing passively through cytoplasm would take minutes to travel even a few millimeters — far too slow for reflexes or thought. By concentrating charged-ion channels along the axon membrane, the neuron converts distance into an actively regenerated electrical pulse that travels at up to 120 meters per second in myelinated axons, rather than relying on molecules physically drifting from one end of the cell to the other. When diagnosing nervous-system dysfunction — for instance, why a reflex arc is delayed — the practical strategy is to localize which region is compromised: impaired dendritic reception, disrupted soma integration (as in some neurodegenerative diseases), or slowed axonal conduction (as in demyelinating conditions like multiple sclerosis), since each maps to a distinct structural stage of the signal's path.

## Electrical Synapse

At an electrical synapse, two neurons sit close enough that their membranes are bridged by **gap junctions** — clusters of channel proteins called connexons, each formed from six subunit proteins (connexins) arranged around a central pore. When a connexon in one cell's membrane aligns with a connexon in the neighboring cell's membrane, they dock to form a continuous aqueous channel connecting the two cytoplasms directly. This channel is wide enough to pass not only ions but also small signaling molecules (under roughly 1 kDa) such as ATP, IP₃, and small peptides — a feature chemical synapses, which rely on vesicle release across a synaptic cleft, cannot replicate.

The defining functional consequence is that current generated in one neuron flows straight into the next with no synaptic delay: transmission is essentially instantaneous, since it requires no neurotransmitter release, diffusion, or receptor binding. This makes electrical synapses ideal wherever a circuit needs multiple neurons to fire in tight synchrony — for example, coordinating the rhythmic contractions of cardiac muscle cells, or synchronizing interneurons that generate oscillatory activity in the brain. Most electrical synapses are also **bidirectional**: current can pass in either direction through the same channel, unlike the strict one-way flow of a chemical synapse from presynaptic to postsynaptic cell.

Consider a simple modeling problem: two neurons are coupled by a gap junction with conductance $g_j$. If neuron 1 is depolarized to a membrane potential $V_1$ while neuron 2 rests at $V_2$, the current flowing through the junction is given by Ohm's law applied to the channel:

$$
I_j = g_j (V_1 - V_2)
$$

This equation captures the essence of electrical coupling: the transmitted current scales linearly with the voltage difference between the two cells and with the junction's conductance, which itself grows with the number of open connexon channels. Suppose an experimenter records $V_1 = -40$ mV and $V_2 = -70$ mV with intracellular electrodes and measures a junctional current $I_j$ of 3 nA. Rearranging the equation gives $g_j = I_j / (V_1 - V_2) = 3\text{ nA} / 30\text{ mV} = 100$ nS — a direct estimate of how strongly the two cells are electrically coupled. Comparing this value across different pairs of neurons, or before and after a pharmacological treatment that closes connexons, lets researchers quantify changes in coupling strength within synchronized networks such as retinal amacrine cells or cortical interneurons.
