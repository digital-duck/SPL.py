## Neuron

A neuron is a eukaryotic cell specialized for rapid electrical and chemical signaling, forming the fundamental unit of the nervous system. Its architecture divides labor across three regions: dendrites, branching extensions that receive incoming signals from other neurons; the soma (cell body), which houses the nucleus and organelles and integrates those incoming signals; and the axon, a long projection that carries an outgoing signal away from the soma toward target cells. Like other eukaryotic cells, the neuron relies on standard organelles — mitochondria for ATP production, ribosomes and endoplasmic reticulum for protein synthesis — but it deploys them toward a distinctive purpose: sustaining a cell whose membrane can rapidly change its electrical state and whose shape can span, in some human motor neurons, over a meter.

Consider a sensory neuron in your fingertip that detects a hot stove. Heat-sensitive receptors on the dendrites convert (transduce) thermal energy into small electrical changes. If enough dendrites are stimulated at once, the soma integrates these inputs and triggers an action potential — a brief, self-propagating reversal of the membrane's electrical charge — which races down the axon. At the axon's end, this electrical signal triggers the release of chemical messengers (neurotransmitters) into a synapse, the junction with the next neuron, propagating the signal onward until it reaches the spinal cord and brain, producing the reflex to pull your hand back.

This structure directly solves a problem that plain diffusion cannot: getting a signal across long distances quickly and reliably. A molecule diffusing passively through cytoplasm would take minutes to travel even a few millimeters — far too slow for reflexes or thought. By concentrating charged-ion channels along the axon membrane, the neuron converts distance into an actively regenerated electrical pulse that travels at up to 120 meters per second in myelinated axons, rather than relying on molecules physically drifting from one end of the cell to the other. When diagnosing nervous-system dysfunction — for instance, why a reflex arc is delayed — the practical strategy is to localize which region is compromised: impaired dendritic reception, disrupted soma integration (as in some neurodegenerative diseases), or slowed axonal conduction (as in demyelinating conditions like multiple sclerosis), since each maps to a distinct structural stage of the signal's path.

## Neurotransmitter

A neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger released by a presynaptic neuron that diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on a postsynaptic neuron, muscle cell, or gland, converting an electrical signal into a chemical one and back again. When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open, calcium influx triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane, and neurotransmitter molecules are released by exocytosis. Once bound to receptors on the far side of the cleft, the neurotransmitter either excites the postsynaptic cell (making it more likely to fire, as with glutamate) or inhibits it (making it less likely to fire, as with GABA). The signal is then terminated by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion away from the synapse.

**Worked example.** Consider a motor neuron communicating with a muscle fiber via acetylcholine (ACh) at the neuromuscular junction. An action potential arrives, calcium enters the terminal, and vesicles release ACh into the cleft. ACh binds receptors on the muscle membrane, opening ion channels that depolarize the muscle cell and trigger contraction. The enzyme acetylcholinesterase then rapidly breaks down ACh, clearing the synapse so the muscle can either reset or receive the next signal distinctly. This clearance step is what allows rapid, repeated firing — without it, ACh would linger and the receptors would stay activated, producing sustained rather than controlled contraction.

**Problem-solving application.** Now suppose a substance blocks acetylcholinesterase itself, rather than acting anywhere else in the cycle — this is exactly how nerve agents and some pesticides work. Reasoning through the mechanism: if the enzyme that normally breaks down ACh is disabled, ACh released into the cleft is no longer cleared. It keeps binding and rebinding receptors on the muscle membrane, so the postsynaptic cell stays depolarized instead of resetting between signals. The result is sustained, uncontrolled muscle activation rather than the brief, distinct contractions a normal cycle produces — which is why acetylcholinesterase inhibition is so dangerous at the neuromuscular junction. Notice that the amount of ACh released per action potential and the receptors it binds haven't changed at all; only the clearance step has been disrupted, and that alone is enough to change the outcome. This is the general strategy for predicting the effect of any drug or toxin acting on a chemical synapse: identify which single step of the release–binding–clearance cycle is being targeted, then infer whether disrupting that one step increases or decreases net signal transmission, holding everything else in the cycle fixed.

## Chemical Synapse

A chemical synapse is the junction where a presynaptic neuron communicates with a postsynaptic neuron by releasing a chemical messenger—a neurotransmitter—into the narrow gap between them, the synaptic cleft (roughly 20 nanometers wide). Unlike an electrical synapse, where current flows directly through gap junctions, a chemical synapse converts an electrical signal into a chemical one and back again, introducing a brief delay but allowing far greater flexibility: signals can be amplified, filtered, or reversed in sign (excitatory or inhibitory) depending on which neurotransmitter and receptor are involved.

**How transmission works.** When an action potential arrives at the presynaptic terminal, it opens voltage-gated calcium channels. The resulting influx of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ triggers synaptic vesicles—membrane-bound sacs filled with neurotransmitter—to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release their contents into the cleft via exocytosis. The neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the cleft and bind to receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, opening ion channels there. The result is a postsynaptic potential: a small depolarization (excitatory) or hyperpolarization (inhibitory) in the receiving neuron. (Some receptors act more slowly through internal signaling cascades rather than opening a channel directly, but the outcome is the same kind of postsynaptic potential.)

**Worked example.** Consider glutamate, the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the vertebrate brain. At an excitatory synapse, an action potential triggers glutamate release; it binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane that open channels permeable to $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$. Sodium influx depolarizes the postsynaptic cell, producing an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP). If enough EPSPs arrive close together, their combined effect can push the postsynaptic neuron's membrane potential past threshold, triggering its own action potential and propagating the signal onward.

**Problem-solving application.** Chemical synapses are also the primary target of most psychoactive drugs and neurological therapies, because each step in the process—release, diffusion, binding, or clearance—is a distinct point of intervention. For example, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) block the transporter that normally clears serotonin from the cleft, prolonging its action on postsynaptic receptors and intensifying signaling at that synapse. Understanding synaptic transmission as a multistep pathway, rather than a single event, lets you predict a drug's effect from which step it targets: interfering with clearance prolongs a signal, while interfering with an earlier step (release or binding) would instead weaken or block it.
