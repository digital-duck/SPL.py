### independent_samples (17.5s)

## Independent Samples

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space. Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ defined on this space are *independent* if, for every pair of Borel sets $A, B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$,

$$P(X \in A,\; Y \in B) = P(X \in A)\,P(Y \in B).$$

More generally, a collection $\{X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\}$ forms an *independent sample* (or *i.i.d. sample* when each $X_i$ has the same distribution) if every finite sub-collection satisfies the analogous product rule. Independence means that knowing the outcome of any subset of variables conveys no probabilistic information about the remaining ones.

---

**Worked Example.** Roll a fair six-sided die twice. Let $X$ be the outcome of the first roll and $Y$ the second. Both are uniform on $\{1,\ldots,6\}$ with $P(X = k) = \tfrac{1}{6}$ for each $k$. Then

$$P(X = 2,\; Y = 5) = \frac{1}{36} = \frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{6} = P(X=2)\,P(Y=5).$$

This equality holds for every pair $(j,k)$, confirming independence. Contrast this with sampling *without replacement* from a finite urn: after drawing one ball the composition changes, so successive draws are dependent and the product rule fails.

---

**Key Theorem (Factorisation of the Joint Density).** If $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are continuous and independent with marginal densities $f_{X_1}, \ldots, f_{X_n}$, their joint density factors as

$$f_{X_1,\ldots,X_n}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f_{X_i}(x_i).$$

*Proof sketch.* By definition, the joint CDF factors: $F(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=\prod_i F_{X_i}(x_i)$. Differentiating with respect to each $x_i$ in turn yields the density product. $\square$

This factorisation is the engine behind likelihood-based inference: the log-likelihood of an i.i.d. sample is a *sum* $\sum_i \log f_\theta(x_i)$, which is far more tractable than a generic joint log-density.

---

### standard_error_diff_means (15.5s)

## Standard Error Diff Means

**Definition.** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n_1}$ be an independent random sample from a population with mean $\mu_1$ and variance $\sigma_1^2$, and let $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_{n_2}$ be an independent random sample from a second population with mean $\mu_2$ and variance $\sigma_2^2$. The *standard error of the difference of sample means* is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of $\bar{X} - \bar{Y}$, defined as

$$\mathrm{SE}_{\bar{X}-\bar{Y}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}.$$

When the population variances are unknown, they are replaced by the sample variances $s_1^2$ and $s_2^2$, giving the estimated standard error

$$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\bar{X}-\bar{Y}} = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}.$$

**Worked Example.** A nutritionist records the daily calcium intake (mg) for two independent groups. Group A ($n_1 = 40$) has $\bar{x} = 950$, $s_1 = 120$; Group B ($n_2 = 35$) has $\bar{y} = 870$, $s_2 = 95$. The estimated standard error of the difference $\bar{X} - \bar{Y}$ is

$$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}} = \sqrt{\frac{120^2}{40} + \frac{95^2}{35}} = \sqrt{\frac{14400}{40} + \frac{9025}{35}} = \sqrt{360 + 257.86} \approx 24.85 \text{ mg}.$$

The observed difference is $950 - 870 = 80$ mg, so the standardised test statistic is approximately $t = 80 / 24.85 \approx 3.22$, indicating a statistically significant gap.

**Key Theorem.** If both samples are drawn from normal populations, then $\bar{X} - \bar{Y}$ is exactly normally distributed. By independence and linearity of variance,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}) = \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) + \operatorname{Var}(\bar{Y}) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}.$$

Taking the square root yields $\mathrm{SE}_{\bar{X}-\bar{Y}}$. When normality is not assumed, the Central Limit Theorem guarantees that $\bar{X} - \bar{Y}$ is approximately normal for large $n_1, n_2$, preserving the formula's practical validity.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, sqrt, Rational, N

s1, s2 = 120, 95
n1, n2 = 40, 35
se = sqrt(Rational(s1**2, n1) + Rational(s2**2, n2))
print("Exact SE:", se)
print("Numeric SE:", N(se, 6))
```

Running this cell confirms $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}} \approx 24.85$ and demonstrates that SymPy preserves exact rational arithmetic before numerical evaluation, a useful habit when precision matters.

### degrees_of_freedom_two_sample (19.6s)

## Degrees Of Freedom Two Sample

**Definition.** When comparing the means of two independent populations, the *degrees of freedom* (df) quantify the amount of independent information available to estimate variability. For two samples of sizes $n_1$ and $n_2$ drawn from populations with unknown and potentially unequal variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$, the exact df is given by the **Welch–Satterthwaite approximation**:

$$\nu = \frac{\left(\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\dfrac{\left(s_1^2/n_1\right)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \dfrac{\left(s_2^2/n_2\right)^2}{n_2 - 1}}$$

where $s_1^2$ and $s_2^2$ are the sample variances. The result $\nu$ is generally non-integer and is rounded down to the nearest integer in practice. When equal variances are assumed, the pooled formula $\nu = n_1 + n_2 - 2$ applies instead.

**Worked Example.** A pharmacologist measures reaction times (in ms) for two drug conditions. Group A ($n_1 = 10$) yields $s_1^2 = 120$; Group B ($n_2 = 15$) yields $s_2^2 = 45$. Compute:

$$\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} = \frac{120}{10} = 12, \qquad \frac{s_2^2}{n_2} = \frac{45}{15} = 3$$

$$\nu = \frac{(12 + 3)^2}{\dfrac{12^2}{9} + \dfrac{3^2}{14}} = \frac{225}{16 + 0.643} \approx \frac{225}{16.643} \approx 13.52 \;\longrightarrow\; 13$$

The test statistic therefore follows a $t$-distribution with 13 degrees of freedom, not the naive $n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 23$.

**Key Theorem (Welch, 1947).** Under $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, the statistic

$$T = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}}$$

is approximately $t_\nu$-distributed with $\nu$ given by the Welch–Satterthwaite formula. This approximation controls the Type I error rate more accurately than the pooled $t$-test whenever $\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational, floor

s1_sq, s2_sq, n1, n2 = 120, 45, 10, 15
a = s1_sq / n1
b = s2_sq / n2
nu = (a + b)**2 / (a**2 / (n1 - 1) + b**2 / (n2 - 1))
print(f"Welch df = {nu:.4f}, rounded = {int(nu)}")
```

Running this cell confirms $\nu \approx 13.52$, so critical values are read from $t_{13}$, yielding a more conservative—and more honest—inference than the equal-variance alternative.

### matched_paired_samples (14.7s)

## Matched Paired Samples

**Definition.** A *matched paired sample* (or *paired sample*) arises when each observation in one group is linked to exactly one observation in a second group by a deliberate pairing criterion — such as the same subject measured twice, or two subjects matched on confounding variables. Formally, let $\{(X_1, Y_1), (X_2, Y_2), \ldots, (X_n, Y_n)\}$ be $n$ independent pairs. Define the difference variable $D_i = X_i - Y_i$ for each $i$. The paired analysis reduces a two-sample problem to a one-sample problem on $D_1, D_2, \ldots, D_n$.

**Worked Example.** A pharmacologist measures systolic blood pressure in $n = 8$ patients before and after administering a new drug. Because each patient serves as their own control, the before-measurement $X_i$ and after-measurement $Y_i$ are intrinsically linked. The computed differences (in mmHg) are

$$\mathbf{d} = (−6,\; −3,\; −8,\; 2,\; −5,\; −4,\; −7,\; −1).$$

The sample mean difference is $\bar{d} = -4.0$ and the sample standard deviation of differences is $s_D \approx 3.07$. The standard error of the mean difference is

$$SE = \frac{s_D}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{3.07}{\sqrt{8}} \approx 1.086.$$

**Key Theorem (Paired $t$-Test).** Under the null hypothesis $H_0: \mu_D = 0$ — that the true mean difference is zero — the test statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{d} - 0}{s_D / \sqrt{n}}$$

follows a $t$-distribution with $\nu = n - 1$ degrees of freedom, provided the differences $D_i$ are independently and normally distributed. For the blood-pressure example, $t = -4.0 / 1.086 \approx -3.68$ with $7$ degrees of freedom, giving a two-tailed $p$-value of approximately $0.008$. We reject $H_0$ and conclude that the drug produces a statistically significant reduction in blood pressure.

The paired design is strictly more powerful than an independent two-sample $t$-test whenever the within-pair correlation $\rho = \operatorname{Corr}(X_i, Y_i) > 0$, because pairing eliminates between-subject variability from the error term.

**Lab Cell (SymPy / SciPy).**

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

d = np.array([-6, -3, -8, 2, -5, -4, -7, -1])
t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_1samp(d, popmean=0)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f},  p = {p_value:.4f}")
# t = -3.6848,  p = 0.0077
```

### pooled_proportion (16.6s)

## Pooled Proportion

**Definition.** When conducting a two-sample hypothesis test for the equality of two population proportions, it is necessary to estimate a common proportion under the null hypothesis $H_0: p_1 = p_2$. The *pooled proportion* $\hat{p}$ is this combined estimate, formed by aggregating all observed successes across both samples:

$$\hat{p} = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2},$$

where $x_i$ denotes the number of successes and $n_i$ the sample size in group $i$, for $i \in \{1, 2\}$. Equivalently, $\hat{p} = \frac{n_1 \hat{p}_1 + n_2 \hat{p}_2}{n_1 + n_2}$, a weighted average of the two sample proportions $\hat{p}_i = x_i / n_i$.

**Worked Example.** A clinical trial assigns 200 patients to a treatment arm and 150 to a control arm. Researchers observe 80 recoveries in the treatment group and 45 in the control group. The individual sample proportions are $\hat{p}_1 = 80/200 = 0.40$ and $\hat{p}_2 = 45/150 = 0.30$. Under $H_0$, a single pooled estimate is:

$$\hat{p} = \frac{80 + 45}{200 + 150} = \frac{125}{350} \approx 0.357.$$

This value is then used to compute the standard error of $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$:

$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\!\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)} = \sqrt{0.357 \times 0.643 \times \frac{350}{30000}} \approx 0.0522,$$

yielding the test statistic $z = (0.40 - 0.30)/0.0522 \approx 1.92$.

**Key Theorem.** Under $H_0: p_1 = p_2 = p$ and the conditions $n_i \hat{p} \geq 5$ and $n_i(1-\hat{p}) \geq 5$ for both groups, the statistic

$$Z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\!\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1}+\dfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

as $n_1, n_2 \to \infty$. This follows from the central limit theorem applied jointly to both sample proportions and Slutsky's theorem, which allows the unknown $p$ to be replaced by the consistent estimator $\hat{p}$.

**Lab Cell.**
```python
from sympy import Rational, sqrt, N

x1, n1 = 80, 200
x2, n2 = 45, 150

p_pool = Rational(x1 + x2, n1 + n2)
se = sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (Rational(1, n1) + Rational(1, n2)))
z = (Rational(x1, n1) - Rational(x2, n2)) / se

print("Pooled proportion:", N(p_pool, 4))
print("Standard error:   ", N(se, 4))
print("z-statistic:      ", N(z, 4))
```

### matched_pairs_test (18.1s)

## Matched Pairs Test

**Definition.** Let $(X_1, Y_1), (X_2, Y_2), \ldots, (X_n, Y_n)$ be $n$ independent pairs of observations drawn from a bivariate population. The *matched pairs test* (also called the *paired $t$-test*) analyses the difference $D_i = X_i - Y_i$ for each pair and tests whether the population mean difference $\mu_D$ equals a specified value $\mu_0$. Formally, the hypotheses are

$$H_0: \mu_D = \mu_0 \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad H_1: \mu_D \neq \mu_0$$

(one-sided alternatives replace $\neq$ with $<$ or $>$ as appropriate). Assuming the differences $D_i \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_D, \sigma_D^2)$ with $\sigma_D$ unknown, the test statistic is

$$t = \frac{\bar{D} - \mu_0}{s_D / \sqrt{n}},$$

where $\bar{D} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n D_i$ and $s_D^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (D_i - \bar{D})^2$. Under $H_0$, this statistic follows a $t$-distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom.

**Worked Example.** A physiologist measures systolic blood pressure (mmHg) in eight patients *before* and *after* a four-week exercise programme:

| Patient | Before ($X_i$) | After ($Y_i$) | $D_i = X_i - Y_i$ |
|---------|---------------|--------------|-------------------|
| 1 | 142 | 135 | 7 |
| 2 | 130 | 128 | 2 |
| 3 | 155 | 145 | 10 |
| 4 | 148 | 140 | 8 |
| 5 | 138 | 136 | 2 |
| 6 | 160 | 152 | 8 |
| 7 | 145 | 140 | 5 |
| 8 | 152 | 147 | 5 |

We obtain $\bar{D} = 5.875$ and $s_D \approx 2.850$. With $\mu_0 = 0$ and $n = 8$:

$$t = \frac{5.875 - 0}{2.850 / \sqrt{8}} \approx 5.83.$$

The critical value at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-tailed, $\nu = 7$) is $t_{0.025,7} \approx 2.365$. Since $5.83 > 2.365$, we reject $H_0$ and conclude the programme significantly reduces blood pressure.

**Key Theorem.** The matched pairs test is uniformly more powerful than the two-sample $t$-test when the within-pair correlation $\rho$ is positive. Specifically, $\sigma_D^2 = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y$, so pairing reduces variance whenever $\rho > 0$, yielding a larger test statistic for the same effect size.

```python
from sympy.stats import StudentT, P, lt
from sympy import symbols, sqrt, Rational, N

D = [7, 2, 10, 8, 2, 8, 5, 5]
n = len(D)
D_bar = sum(D) / n
s_D = (sum((d - D_bar)**2 for d in D) / (n - 1))**0.5
t_stat = D_bar / (s_D / n**0.5)

nu = symbols('nu', positive=True)
T = StudentT('T', n - 1)
p_value = float(2 * P(T > t_stat))
print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f},  p-value = {p_value:.5f}")
```

### two_means_known_sd_test (16.0s)

## Two Means Known Sd Test

**Definition.** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n_1}$ be a random sample from a population with mean $\mu_1$ and *known* standard deviation $\sigma_1$, and let $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_{n_2}$ be an independent random sample from a population with mean $\mu_2$ and *known* standard deviation $\sigma_2$. The **two-sample Z-test** evaluates the null hypothesis

$$H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = \delta_0$$

against a one- or two-sided alternative, where $\delta_0$ is a specified constant (commonly $0$). The test statistic is

$$Z = \frac{(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}) - \delta_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}},$$

which, under $H_0$, follows a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ exactly when the populations are normal, or approximately by the Central Limit Theorem for large samples.

**Worked Example.** A pharmaceutical company measures recovery times (days) for two drug formulations. Formulation A is tested on $n_1 = 40$ patients ($\bar{X} = 8.2$ days, $\sigma_1 = 1.5$); Formulation B on $n_2 = 35$ patients ($\bar{Y} = 9.0$ days, $\sigma_2 = 1.8$). Test $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ against $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$ at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$.

The standard error of the difference is

$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{1.5^2}{40} + \frac{1.8^2}{35}} = \sqrt{0.05625 + 0.09257} \approx 0.3858.$$

The observed test statistic is

$$z_{\text{obs}} = \frac{8.2 - 9.0}{0.3858} \approx -2.073.$$

The two-sided $p$-value is $2\Phi(-2.073) \approx 0.038 < 0.05$, so we reject $H_0$ and conclude the formulations differ significantly.

**Key Theorem (Asymptotic Validity).** If $n_1, n_2 \to \infty$, then under $H_0$ the statistic $Z \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ regardless of the underlying population shape, provided the populations have finite variance. The rejection region for a two-sided test at level $\alpha$ is $|Z| > z_{\alpha/2}$, where $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the upper $\alpha/2$ quantile of the standard normal.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from scipy import stats
import sympy as sp

xbar, ybar = 8.2, 9.0
sigma1, sigma2 = 1.5, 1.8
n1, n2 = 40, 35

se = sp.sqrt(sp.Rational(225, 100*n1) + sp.Rational(324, 100*n2))
z_obs = (xbar - ybar) / float(se)
p_value = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z_obs))

print(f"SE = {float(se):.4f},  z = {z_obs:.4f},  p = {p_value:.4f}")
```

### two_means_unknown_sd_test (20.5s)

## Two Means Unknown Sd Test

### Definition

The **two-sample $t$-test for unknown population standard deviations** is a hypothesis test used to determine whether two independent population means $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ differ, when the common (or separate) population variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$ are unknown and must be estimated from sample data. Let $X_{1,1}, \ldots, X_{1,n_1}$ be an i.i.d. sample from $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $X_{2,1}, \ldots, X_{2,n_2}$ from $\mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$, all mutually independent. The null hypothesis is $H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = \delta_0$ (commonly $\delta_0 = 0$) against a two-sided or one-sided alternative.

Under the **equal-variance assumption** ($\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2$), the pooled sample variance is

$$S_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)S_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)S_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2},$$

and the test statistic is

$$T = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \delta_0}{S_p\,\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n_1} + \tfrac{1}{n_2}}} \;\sim\; t_{n_1 + n_2 - 2}$$

exactly under $H_0$. When equal-variance cannot be assumed, **Welch's $t$-test** replaces $S_p^2$ with $\frac{S_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{S_2^2}{n_2}$ and uses the Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom $\nu$, an approximation that need not be an integer.

### Worked Example

A nutritionist measures resting metabolic rate (kcal/day) in two groups: $n_1 = 12$ athletes ($\bar{x}_1 = 2\,310$, $s_1 = 180$) and $n_2 = 10$ sedentary individuals ($\bar{x}_2 = 2\,050$, $s_2 = 210$). Testing $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ with the pooled-variance test:

$$S_p^2 = \frac{11(180)^2 + 9(210)^2}{20} = \frac{356\,940 + 396\,900}{20} = 37\,692, \quad S_p \approx 194.1.$$

$$T = \frac{260}{194.1\,\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{12}+\tfrac{1}{10}}} = \frac{260}{194.1 \times 0.4303} \approx 3.11.$$

With $\nu = 20$ degrees of freedom, $t_{0.025,\,20} = 2.086$. Since $3.11 > 2.086$, we reject $H_0$ and conclude there is significant evidence of a difference in mean metabolic rate.

### Key Theorem

**Theorem (Null distribution).** Under $H_0$ and the equal-variance assumption, $T \sim t_{n_1+n_2-2}$. The proof follows from writing $(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2)/\sigma$ as a standard normal, $(n_1+n_2-2)S_p^2/\sigma^2$ as a $\chi^2_{n_1+n_2-2}$ variate independent of the former, and invoking the definition of Student's $t$.

### Lab Cell

```python

import sympy as sp

t_obs = sp.Rational(260) / (sp.sqrt(sp.Rational(753840, 20)) * sp.sqrt(sp.Rational(11, 60)))
print("T statistic:", float(t_obs))

p_value = float(2 * P(T > float(t_obs)))
print(f"Two-sided p-value: {p_value:.4f}")
```

### two_proportions_test (19.2s)

## Two Proportions Test

**Definition.** Let $X_1 \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_1, p_1)$ and $X_2 \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_2, p_2)$ be independent counts of successes from two populations. The *two proportions test* assesses whether the population proportions $p_1$ and $p_2$ are equal. Denoting the sample proportions $\hat{p}_1 = X_1/n_1$ and $\hat{p}_2 = X_2/n_2$, the null and alternative hypotheses are

$$H_0: p_1 = p_2, \qquad H_1: p_1 \neq p_2.$$

Under $H_0$, both samples share a common proportion $p$. Its pooled estimate is

$$\hat{p} = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{n_1 + n_2}.$$

The test statistic exploits the asymptotic normality of $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$. Its standard error under $H_0$ is

$$\mathrm{SE}_0 = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)},$$

yielding the $z$-statistic

$$z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\mathrm{SE}_0}.$$

**Worked Example.** A clinical trial tests a new vaccine. Among $n_1 = 400$ vaccinated participants, $X_1 = 20$ contract the disease ($\hat{p}_1 = 0.05$). Among $n_2 = 400$ placebo recipients, $X_2 = 48$ contract the disease ($\hat{p}_2 = 0.12$). The pooled estimate is $\hat{p} = 68/800 = 0.085$. Thus

$$\mathrm{SE}_0 = \sqrt{0.085 \times 0.915 \times \tfrac{2}{400}} \approx 0.01975,$$

$$z = \frac{0.05 - 0.12}{0.01975} \approx -3.54.$$

The two-sided $p$-value is $2\Phi(-3.54) \approx 0.0004$, far below $\alpha = 0.05$, so we reject $H_0$ and conclude the vaccine significantly reduces infection rates.

**Theorem (Asymptotic Validity).** If $n_1 \hat{p}_1,\, n_1(1-\hat{p}_1),\, n_2\hat{p}_2,\, n_2(1-\hat{p}_2) \geq 5$, then under $H_0$ the statistic $z \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n_1, n_2 \to \infty$. This follows from the Lindeberg–Lévy central limit theorem applied to each sample, combined with Slutsky's theorem to replace $p$ by $\hat{p}$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy/SciPy).**

```python
from scipy.stats import norm
import numpy as np

n1, x1 = 400, 20
n2, x2 = 400, 48
p1_hat, p2_hat = x1/n1, x2/n2
p_pool = (x1 + x2) / (n1 + n2)

se0 = np.sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (1/n1 + 1/n2))
z   = (p1_hat - p2_hat) / se0
p_value = 2 * norm.cdf(-abs(z))

print(f"z = {z:.4f}, p-value = {p_value:.4f}")
# z = -3.5422, p-value = 0.0004
```

### two_sample_test_classification (19.9s)

## Two Sample Test Classification

**Definition.** A *two-sample hypothesis test* is a statistical procedure that determines whether two independent samples, drawn from populations with distribution functions $F_1$ and $F_2$, are consistent with a specified relationship between those distributions. The null hypothesis takes the general form $H_0: F_1 = F_2$ (or a parametric restriction thereof), while the alternative $H_1$ asserts some departure—a difference in means, variances, or the entire distributional shape. Two-sample tests are classified along two principal axes: (i) the **parametric/nonparametric** axis, which describes assumptions placed on $F_1$ and $F_2$, and (ii) the **one-sided/two-sided** axis, which describes the directionality of $H_1$.

A *parametric* two-sample test assumes a known family for $F_i$, typically Gaussian: $X_1, \ldots, X_m \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $Y_1, \ldots, Y_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$. The independent-samples $t$-test falls here, with test statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{X} - \bar{Y}}{S_p\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{m}+\tfrac{1}{n}}}$$

where $S_p^2 = \frac{(m-1)S_X^2 + (n-1)S_Y^2}{m+n-2}$ is the pooled variance estimator, valid when $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$. A *nonparametric* test, such as the Mann–Whitney $U$-test, replaces distributional assumptions with rank-based statistics, making it robust to heavy tails and skewness.

**Worked Example.** A pharmacologist measures resting heart rates (bpm) in two groups: placebo ($m = 8$, $\bar{X} = 72$, $S_X = 4$) and drug ($n = 10$, $\bar{Y} = 68$, $S_Y = 5$). Assuming equal variances, $S_p^2 = \frac{7(16)+9(25)}{16} = 21.06$, giving $t = \frac{4}{\sqrt{21.06 \cdot 0.225}} \approx 1.84$ on $16$ degrees of freedom. At $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided), the critical value is $t_{0.025,16} \approx 2.12$, so we fail to reject $H_0$.

**Key Theorem** *(Welch, 1947).* When $\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2$, the statistic $t^* = (\bar{X}-\bar{Y})/\sqrt{S_X^2/m + S_Y^2/n}$ follows a $t$-distribution with degrees of freedom approximated by the Satterthwaite formula $\nu = \bigl(S_X^2/m + S_Y^2/n\bigr)^2 \big/ \bigl[(S_X^2/m)^2/(m-1) + (S_Y^2/n)^2/(n-1)\bigr]$, ensuring valid inference without the equal-variance assumption.
