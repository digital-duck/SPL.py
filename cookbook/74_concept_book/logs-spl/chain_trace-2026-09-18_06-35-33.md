## 丨

You've likely seen the vertical bar in expressions like $a \mid b$, read "$a$ divides $b$." At first glance it's just shorthand — but it's actually defining a precise mathematical relationship, not just a notation trick.

**Formal definition.** For integers $a$ and $b$, we say $a \mid b$ if there exists an integer $k$ such that

$$b = ka$$

So $3 \mid 12$ because $12 = 4 \cdot 3$. But $3 \nmid 10$, since no integer $k$ satisfies $10 = 3k$.

**Structure worth noticing.** This relation isn't random — it behaves in predictable ways:

- **Reflexive:** $a \mid a$ for every integer $a$ (take $k = 1$).
- **Transitive:** if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$. (Proof sketch: $b = ka$ and $c = mb$, so $c = mka$ — and $mk$ is still an integer.)
- **Every integer divides 0**, since $0 = 0 \cdot a$.
- **Only $\pm 1$ divide every integer.**

Because of transitivity and reflexivity, divisibility acts like an ordering on the positive integers — not a full ranking like $\leq$, but a structure mathematicians call a *partial order*: some numbers are comparable ($2 \mid 8$), others aren't ($5$ and $8$ share no divisibility relation at all).

This same bar reappears elsewhere in math — in conditional probability $P(A \mid B)$, or set-builder notation $\{x \mid x > 0\}$ — always signaling "under this condition." Divisibility is the cleanest place to see its logical bite.

**Practice problem:** Using the transitivity rule above, explain why $4 \mid 60$ given that $4 \mid 20$ and $20 \mid 60$. Then find a counterexample showing $a \mid c$ does *not* imply $c \mid a$.
