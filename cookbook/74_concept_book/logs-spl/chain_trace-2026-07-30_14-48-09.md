### cyclic_component (0.0s)

## Cyclic Component

### Definition

A **cyclic component** of a time series is a quasi-periodic fluctuation whose wavelength exceeds one full seasonal cycle but is not monotone. Formally, let a discrete time series $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ admit the additive decomposition

$$y_t = T_t + C_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $T_t$ is the trend, $S_t$ the seasonal component (period $m$ fixed and known), and $\varepsilon_t$ zero-mean noise. The **cyclic component** $C_t$ is the residual signal obtained after removing trend and seasonality; it satisfies $\mathbb{E}[C_t] = 0$ and has a dominant pseudo-period $P$ with $P \gg m$. Unlike $S_t$, the period $P$ is neither fixed nor required to be an integer multiple of the sampling interval.

### Worked Example

Suppose monthly GDP growth (in percent) over 120 months is modelled as

$$y_t = \underbrace{0.02\,t}_{T_t} + \underbrace{1.5\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{12}\right)}_{S_t} + \underbrace{3\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{48}\right)}_{C_t} + \varepsilon_t.$$

Here $S_t$ captures a one-year seasonal swing ($m = 12$), while $C_t$ represents a four-year business cycle ($P = 48$ months). After fitting a linear trend and subtracting the seasonal mean for each calendar month, the residual series $\hat{C}_t = y_t - \hat{T}_t - \hat{S}_t$ retains the four-year oscillation. A periodogram of $\hat{C}_t$ would show a spectral peak near frequency $f = 1/48$ cycles per month, confirming the isolation of the cyclic signal.

### Key Theorem

**Spectral Separation Theorem (informal).** If $T_t$, $C_t$, $S_t$, and $\varepsilon_t$ have non-overlapping spectral support — that is, their power spectra are concentrated at disjoint frequency bands — then each component is identifiable from $y_t$ up to estimation error of order $O(T^{-1/2})$. In practice, overlapping spectra require band-pass filters (e.g., the Hodrick–Prescott or Baxter–King filter) to extract $C_t$ with controlled leakage.

### Lab Cell (SymPy)

```python
import sympy as sp

t = sp.Symbol('t', positive=True)

T_t = 2 * t / 100
S_t = 3 * sp.cos(2 * sp.pi * t / 12) / 2
C_t = 3 * sp.cos(2 * sp.pi * t / 48)

y_t = T_t + S_t + C_t

# sp.simplify (new this section): reduces an expression to its canonical form

P = sp.simplify(2 * sp.pi / omega)
print("Pseudo-period P =", P, "months") # expects 48
```

Running this cell confirms that symbolic subtraction of the trend and seasonal terms recovers $C_t$ exactly, illustrating the additive structure underlying empirical decomposition methods.

### sequence (0.0s)

## Sequence

**Definition.** A *sequence* is a function $a : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ (or more generally, into any set $S$). We write its values as an ordered list $(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots)$, or in compact notation $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$. The integer $n$ is called the *index* and $a_n$ the $n$-th *term*. A sequence is *finite* if the domain is $\{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ for some $N \in \mathbb{N}$, and *infinite* otherwise. Two sequences are equal if and only if $a_n = b_n$ for every index $n$ in their shared domain.

**Worked Example.** Consider the sequence defined by $a_n = \dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$. Listing the first five terms:

$$a_1 = 1,\quad a_2 = -\tfrac{1}{2},\quad a_3 = \tfrac{1}{3},\quad a_4 = -\tfrac{1}{4},\quad a_5 = \tfrac{1}{5}.$$

The terms alternate in sign and decrease in absolute value toward zero. This is the sequence of partial harmonic terms with alternating sign, and it plays a central role in the theory of conditionally convergent series.

**Key Theorem (Squeeze Theorem for Sequences).** Let $\{a_n\}$, $\{b_n\}$, $\{c_n\}$ be sequences satisfying $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. If

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L = \lim_{n \to \infty} c_n,$$

then $\lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = L$ as well.

*Proof sketch.* Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N$ large enough that $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ and $|c_n - L| < \varepsilon$ for all $n > N$. The sandwich inequality $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ then forces $L - \varepsilon < b_n < L + \varepsilon$, i.e., $|b_n - L| < \varepsilon$. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import *

n = symbols('n', positive=True, integer=True)

a = ((-1)**(n + 1)) / n

# Compute and display first ten terms
terms = [a.subs(n, k) for k in range(1, 11)]
print("Terms:", terms)

# Verify the limit
print("Limit:", limit(a, n, oo))
```

Running this cell confirms that each term matches the pattern derived above and that $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$, consistent with the Squeeze Theorem applied with $c_n = 1/n$ and $a_n = -1/n$.

### trend (0.0s)

## Trend

**Definition.** Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{n}$ be a time series observed at equally spaced time points $t = 1, 2, \ldots, n$. The *trend* of the series is the smooth, long-run component $T_t$ such that

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $S_t$ denotes any periodic (seasonal) component and $\varepsilon_t$ is zero-mean random noise. Informally, the trend captures the direction and rate of sustained change in the mean level of the series, abstracting away short-term fluctuations. A trend is called *linear* if $T_t = \alpha + \beta t$ for constants $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, and *nonlinear* otherwise.

**Worked Example.** Suppose annual global mean temperature anomalies (in °C relative to a 1951–1980 baseline) are modelled as $y_t = 0.02\,t - 0.10 + \varepsilon_t$ for $t = 1, \ldots, 50$. The trend component is $T_t = 0.02\,t - 0.10$, indicating a sustained warming of $0.02$ °C per year. At $t = 25$ the predicted mean anomaly is $T_{25} = 0.02(25) - 0.10 = 0.40$ °C. The slope $\beta = 0.02$ is the *trend rate*; its sign determines whether the series is *upward* ($\beta > 0$) or *downward* ($\beta < 0$) trending.

**Key Theorem (Ordinary Least-Squares Trend Estimation).** Given observations $\{(t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{n}$, the OLS estimators of the linear trend parameters are

$$\hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(t - \bar{t})(y_t - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(t - \bar{t})^2}, \qquad \hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}\,\bar{t},$$

where $\bar{t} = (n+1)/2$ and $\bar{y} = n^{-1}\sum y_t$. Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions ($\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t]=0$, constant variance, no autocorrelation), $\hat{\alpha}$ and $\hat{\beta}$ are the Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUE) of the true trend parameters.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy import symbols, summation, simplify, Rational

t, n = symbols('t n', positive=True, integer=True)

# Symbolic numerator of beta-hat for uniform time index
y_t = 2*t - 1          # example: y_t = 2t - 1 (exact linear series)
t_bar = (n + 1) / 2
numerator   = summation((t - t_bar) * (y_t - summation(y_t, (t, 1, n)) / n), (t, 1, n))
denominator = summation((t - t_bar)**2, (t, 1, n))
beta_hat = simplify(numerator / denominator)
print("Estimated slope:", beta_hat)   # Expected: 2
```

Running this cell confirms that for a perfectly linear series the OLS formula recovers the true slope exactly, providing an algebraic sanity check before applying the estimator to noisy empirical data.

### noise (0.0s)

## Noise

**Definition.** In the context of signal processing and probability theory, *noise* refers to any random or deterministic disturbance that corrupts an observed signal. Formally, let $s(t)$ denote a deterministic signal and $\eta(t)$ a random process. The *observed signal* is modeled as

$$x(t) = s(t) + \eta(t),$$

where $\eta(t)$ is characterized by its probability distribution, mean, and power spectral density. The most common assumption is *additive white Gaussian noise* (AWGN), in which $\eta(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ independently at each time step, meaning the noise has zero mean, variance $\sigma^2$, and a flat power spectrum across all frequencies.

The *signal-to-noise ratio* (SNR) quantifies the relative strength of the signal versus the noise:

$$\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_s}{P_n} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[s(t)^2]}{\mathbb{E}[\eta(t)^2]},$$

often expressed in decibels as $\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}(\mathrm{SNR})$.

**Worked Example.** Suppose $s(t) = \sin(2\pi t)$ is sampled at $N = 100$ equally spaced points on $[0, 1]$, and corrupted by AWGN with $\sigma = 0.5$. The signal power is $P_s = \tfrac{1}{2}$ (the mean square of a sine wave over a full period), and the noise power is $P_n = \sigma^2 = 0.25$. Thus

$$\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{0.5}{0.25} = 2, \qquad \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\log_{10}(2) \approx 3.01 \text{ dB}.$$

**Key Theorem (Central Limit Theorem for Noise).** If $\eta(t)$ is formed as the sum of $n$ independent, identically distributed random variables each with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma_0^2$, then as $n \to \infty$,

$$\eta(t) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,\, n\sigma_0^2).$$

This theorem justifies the Gaussian noise model in practice: physical noise arising from many independent microscopic sources (thermal fluctuations, shot noise) is well approximated by a Gaussian distribution.

**Lab Cell.**
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sympy import *

t_vals = np.linspace(0, 1, 100)
s = np.sin(2 * np.pi * t_vals)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
eta = rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=100)
x = s + eta

signal_power = np.mean(s**2)
noise_power  = np.mean(eta**2)
snr_db = 10 * np.log10(signal_power / noise_power)
print(f"SNR = {snr_db:.2f} dB")

plt.plot(t_vals, s, label="Clean signal")
plt.plot(t_vals, x, alpha=0.6, label="Noisy observation")
plt.legend(); plt.xlabel("t"); plt.title("Signal vs. Noisy Observation")
plt.show()
```

### seasonal_component (0.0s)

## Seasonal Component

**Definition.** Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ be a time series observed at equally spaced intervals. The *seasonal component* $S_t$ is a periodic function satisfying

$$S_t = S_{t+m} \quad \forall\, t,$$

where $m \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$ is the *period* (e.g., $m = 12$ for monthly data, $m = 4$ for quarterly data). In the classical additive decomposition

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

$T_t$ denotes the trend, and $\varepsilon_t$ is the irregular residual. In the multiplicative form, $y_t = T_t \cdot S_t \cdot \varepsilon_t$, the seasonal factor is expressed as a ratio rather than an additive offset. The additive model is preferred when seasonal fluctuations are roughly constant in magnitude; the multiplicative model is preferred when they scale with the level of the series.

**Worked Example.** Consider average monthly electricity demand (GWh) over three years. Visual inspection reveals demand peaks each July–August (summer cooling) and dips each April–May. To isolate $S_t$, compute a *centered moving average* (CMA) of order $m = 12$:

$$\hat{T}_t = \frac{1}{12}\sum_{k=-5}^{6} y_{t+k}.$$

Subtract the CMA from the raw series to obtain detrended values $d_t = y_t - \hat{T}_t$. Average $d_t$ across all occurrences of each calendar month to produce twelve seasonal indices $\hat{S}_1, \ldots, \hat{S}_{12}$. For identifiability, impose the constraint $\sum_{j=1}^{12} \hat{S}_j = 0$ (additive case) or $\prod_{j=1}^{12} \hat{S}_j = 1$ (multiplicative case).

**Key Theorem (Uniqueness of Additive Decomposition).** If $y_t = T_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t$ with $S_t$ strictly $m$-periodic and $\sum_{j=1}^{m} S_j = 0$, then $T_t$ and $S_t$ are separately identifiable from $y_t$ up to a constant, provided $T_t$ varies on a timescale much larger than $m$.

*Proof sketch.* Applying the centered moving average operator annihilates $S_t$ (since its $m$-period sum is zero) and smooths $\varepsilon_t$, leaving a consistent estimate of $T_t$. The seasonal component is then recovered by subtraction. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
m      = 12
time   = np.arange(1, 37)                        # 3 years of monthly data
trend  = 0.4 * time + 100
season = 5 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * time / m)        # known ground truth
series = trend + season + rng.normal(0, 1, len(time))

# Centered moving average (the one new primitive introduced here)
cma = np.convolve(series, np.ones(m) / m, mode='same')
detrended = series - cma

# Average each calendar month across years
indices = np.array([detrended[month::m].mean() for month in range(m)])
print(np.round(indices, 2))
```

Running this cell prints twelve estimated seasonal indices. Because `np.convolve` with a uniform kernel of length $m$ implements the CMA operator from the worked example, the output should closely recover the cosine pattern embedded in the synthetic series.

### time_series (0.0s)

## Time Series

**Definition.** A *time series* is a sequence of observations $\{x_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ indexed by discrete time $t \in \mathbb{Z}$, where each $x_t$ is a realization of a random variable $X_t$. The collection $\{X_t\}$ is called a *stochastic process*. A time series is said to be *weakly stationary* (or *covariance-stationary*) if its mean and autocovariance structure are invariant under time shifts:

$$\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu \quad \text{for all } t,$$
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \gamma(k) \quad \text{for all } t, k,$$

where $\gamma(k)$ is called the *autocovariance function* at lag $k$. The normalized version, $\rho(k) = \gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$, is the *autocorrelation function* (ACF). These two quantities — $\gamma$ and $\rho$ — are the primary tools for characterizing the internal dependence structure of a stationary series.

**Worked Example.** Consider daily closing prices of a stock over $T = 5$ days: $x_1 = 100,\ x_2 = 102,\ x_3 = 101,\ x_4 = 105,\ x_5 = 103$. Compute the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{t=1}^{5} x_t = 102.2$. The lag-1 sample autocovariance is

$$\hat{\gamma}(1) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=2}^{T}(x_t - \bar{x})(x_{t-1} - \bar{x}) = \frac{1}{5}\bigl[(-1.2)(-2.2) + (-1.2)(-0.2) + (2.8)(-1.2) + (0.8)(2.8)\bigr] = 0.56.$$

A positive $\hat{\gamma}(1)$ indicates that consecutive observations tend to move together — a feature common in financial data.

**Key Theorem (Properties of the ACF).** For any weakly stationary process $\{X_t\}$, the autocorrelation function satisfies:

$$\rho(0) = 1, \qquad \rho(-k) = \rho(k) \quad \text{for all } k \in \mathbb{Z}, \qquad |\rho(k)| \leq 1.$$

Symmetry follows directly from the definition of covariance; the bound $|\rho(k)| \leq 1$ is a consequence of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $X_t$ and $X_{t+k}$. Together, these constraints rule out spurious correlation estimates outside $[-1, 1]$ and allow the ACF to be plotted on a standardized scale — the foundation of all graphical stationarity diagnostics.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
import sympy as sp

k = sp.Symbol('k', integer=True)
phi, sigma2 = sp.symbols('phi sigma2', positive=True)

# AR(1) autocovariance: gamma(k) = sigma^2 * phi^|k| / (1 - phi^2)
gamma = sigma2 * phi**sp.Abs(k) / (1 - phi**2)
rho   = sp.simplify(gamma / gamma.subs(k, 0))
sp.pprint(rho)  # yields phi^|k|; verify rho(0)=1 and symmetry
```

## Trend Cycle Component

**Definition.** Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ be a time series observed at equally spaced intervals. The *trend-cycle component*, denoted $\tau_t$, is the smooth, slowly-varying constituent of $y_t$ that captures long-run movements and medium-frequency oscillations jointly, abstracting away seasonal fluctuations and irregular noise. Under the additive decomposition model introduced in the previous section,

$$y_t = \tau_t + s_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $s_t$ is the (already-defined) seasonal component and $\varepsilon_t$ is white noise. The trend-cycle is accordingly defined residually as $\tau_t = y_t - s_t - \varepsilon_t$, and is estimated in practice by applying a symmetric moving-average filter that suppresses high-frequency variation.

**Worked Example.** Consider quarterly GDP growth rates (in percent) over eight periods:

$$\mathbf{y} = (2.1,\ 2.8,\ 1.9,\ 2.5,\ 3.1,\ 3.7,\ 3.0,\ 3.6).$$

Applying a $2\times 4$ moving average — the standard filter for quarterly data with $m=4$ — yields

$$\hat{\tau}_t = \frac{1}{8}\!\left(y_{t-2} + 2y_{t-1} + 2y_t + 2y_{t+1} + y_{t+2}\right), \quad t = 3, \ldots, 6.$$

For $t=3$: $\hat{\tau}_3 = \tfrac{1}{8}(2.1 + 2(2.8) + 2(1.9) + 2(2.5) + 3.1) = \tfrac{1}{8}(20.6) = 2.575$. The resulting sequence $\{\hat{\tau}_t\}$ reveals a gentle upward trend overlaid with mild cyclical curvature, obscured in the raw series by quarterly seasonality.

**Key Theorem (Low-Pass Extraction).** If $\{y_t\}$ is covariance-stationary and a symmetric linear filter $\hat{\tau}_t = \sum_{k=-K}^{K} a_k\, y_{t-k}$ with $\sum_k a_k = 1$ is applied, then the spectral density of $\hat{\tau}_t$ satisfies

$$S_{\hat{\tau}}(\omega) = \left|\mathcal{A}(e^{i\omega})\right|^2 S_y(\omega),$$

where $\mathcal{A}(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_k a_k e^{-ik\omega}$ is the filter's frequency-response function. Because the $2\times 4$ weights concentrate $|\mathcal{A}|^2$ near $\omega = 0$, the filter acts as a low-pass gate: seasonal and noise frequencies are attenuated, leaving $\hat{\tau}_t$ as a clean extract of the trend-cycle already implicit in $y_t$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy/statsmodels).**
```python
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose

y = pd.Series([2.1, 2.8, 1.9, 2.5, 3.1, 3.7, 3.0, 3.6])
result = seasonal_decompose(y, model='additive', period=4, extrapolate_trend='freq')
print(result.trend)   # τ_t estimates
```

### seasonal_trend_decomposition_loess (0.0s)

## Seasonal Trend Decomposition Loess

**Definition.** Given a univariate time series $\{Y_t\}_{t=1}^{N}$, *Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess* (STL) expresses each observation as the additive sum

$$Y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t,$$

where $T_t$ is a smooth trend component, $S_t$ is a periodic seasonal component with period $p$, and $R_t$ is a remainder (residual). Both the trend and seasonal components are estimated by fitting low-degree polynomials over sliding neighborhoods, where each neighboring observation receives a weight determined by its distance from the target point via the tricubic kernel:

$$w(u) = \begin{cases}(1 - |u|^3)^3, & |u| < 1,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

The bandwidth parameters $n_s$ (seasonal smoothing window) and $n_t$ (trend smoothing window) control the flexibility of these fits; larger windows yield smoother, slower-varying estimates.

**Worked Example.** Consider monthly average temperatures recorded over five years ($N = 60$, $p = 12$). A pronounced annual cycle dominates the series. Running STL with $n_s = 13$ and $n_t = 23$ extracts: (1) a gently rising $T_t$ reflecting long-term warming, (2) a $S_t$ oscillating between $-8\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ and $+8\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ with period 12, and (3) small residuals $|R_t| < 1\,^{\circ}\text{C}$. Plotting all three panels confirms that most variance is explained by the seasonal component, with the trend capturing a secondary, slower signal.

**Key Theorem (Robustness iterations).** STL incorporates an optional outer loop of $n_o$ robustness passes. After each fit, observations whose residuals are large receive reduced influence before the estimation is repeated. Under mild regularity conditions on the data-generating process, this produces estimates $\hat{T}_t$ and $\hat{S}_t$ that are consistent and robust to up to $\lfloor N/2 \rfloor$ gross outliers.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

series = pd.Series(data, index=pd.date_range("2020-01", periods=60, freq="ME"))
stl = STL(series, period=12, seasonal=13, trend=23, robust=True)
result = stl.fit()

result.plot()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print(result.trend[:5])
print(result.seasonal[:5])
print(result.resid[:5])
```

Vary `seasonal` between 7 and 25 and observe how the extracted $S_t$ changes; larger values force the seasonal component to evolve more slowly across years.
