### parent_structure (0.0s)

## Parent Structure

You already know that in Sage, `5` behaves differently depending on context — sometimes it's a plain integer, sometimes a fraction, sometimes a symbolic variable's coefficient. What decides this? Every object in Sage lives inside a **parent**: a mathematical structure that defines what operations mean and what results are allowed.

Think of a parent as the "address" of a number's mathematics. The integer $5$ can live in $\mathbb{Z}$ (the integers), $\mathbb{Q}$ (the rationals), or even $\mathbb{F}_7$ (integers mod 7). The symbol `5` looks the same, but $5$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ has no inverse under multiplication, while $5$ in $\mathbb{Q}$ does — $\frac{1}{5}$ exists there but not in $\mathbb{Z}$. The parent decides which rules apply.

In Sage, you check an object's parent with `.parent()`:

```
sage: x = ZZ(5)
sage: x.parent()
Integer Ring
sage: y = QQ(5)
sage: y.parent()
Rational Field
```

This matters because operations between objects from *different* parents trigger **coercion** — Sage finds a common parent where both objects can meaningfully interact, following a discoverable, rule-based hierarchy (not guesswork). This is why $\frac{1}{2} + 3$ works and correctly returns a rational number: Sage promotes $3 \in \mathbb{Z}$ into $\mathbb{Q}$ first.

**Rule of thumb:** if you're ever confused about why an operation succeeded, failed, or gave a surprising type, ask `.parent()` first — the object's mathematics is defined by where it lives, not just its printed value.

**Practice:** In Sage, evaluate `a = GF(7)(10)` and then call `a.parent()`. What integer does `a` actually equal, and why?

### symbolic_variable (9.9s)

## Symbolic Variable

You already know that in arithmetic, $x$ often stands for one hidden number you're trying to uncover — like in $x + 3 = 7$, where $x$ can only be $4$. A symbolic variable takes this idea and stretches it much further: instead of representing one unknown number, it can represent *every* number at once.

When you write `var('x')` in a computer algebra system, you're not asking the computer to solve for a specific value. You're creating a symbol — a placeholder that behaves like a number, follows all the rules of arithmetic and algebra, but isn't tied down to any particular value. This is the floor of generality: the idea that you can build true statements about *all* numbers simultaneously, without ever plugging one in.

This is why algebra comes before arithmetic in a real sense. The identity
$$(x+1)^2 = x^2 + 2x + 1$$
is true for every number $x$ — not because you checked it for $x=1$, $x=2$, $x=3$, and so on, but because the symbol $x$ carries the pattern itself. Arithmetic tells you facts about specific numbers; algebra, powered by symbolic variables, tells you facts about *all numbers at once*.

The simple rule: a symbolic variable is a placeholder, not a value. You manipulate it using algebra's rules (distributing, combining like terms, factoring), and whatever relationship you prove holds for every number that could ever be substituted in.

**Practice:** Let $x$ be a symbolic variable. Expand $(x+3)(x-3)$ and simplify. What pattern do you notice, and does it still work if you substitute $x=10$?

### symbolic_expression (9.1s)

## Symbolic Expression

A symbolic variable like $x$ is a single named placeholder. A **symbolic expression** is what happens when you start combining variables with operations and functions, but Sage refuses to collapse the result into a number — it keeps the *structure* exactly as written, living in what's called the Symbolic Ring, written $SR$.

So $\sin(x)^2 + \cos(x)^2$ isn't silently rewritten to $1$. Sage holds onto every piece — the exponents, the function calls, the addition — because that structure is the information. You decide later whether to simplify it, substitute a value into $x$, or leave it alone entirely.

This is the key shift from ordinary variables: an expression is a *tree*, not just a symbol. $x^2 + 3x + 5$ has an addition at the top, with a power and a product as its branches, and those branches have their own branches down to the symbols and constants at the leaves. Sage can walk this tree — that's how `.expand()`, `.factor()`, and `.simplify_full()` work: they're tree transformations, not number-crunching.

**The rule**: expressions in $SR$ are built recursively — a symbol is an expression, a number is an expression, and combining expressions with $+, -, \times, \div, {}^\wedge$, or a function like $\sin$, $\log$, $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ produces a new expression. Nothing evaluates until you explicitly ask it to, with `.subs()` or `.n()`.

**Practice problem:** Let $f = (x+1)^2 - (x^2+2x)$. Without expanding by hand, predict what `f.expand()` should simplify to, then check: is the result still "symbolic," or is it now just a plain number wearing a symbolic expression's clothing?

### exact_rational (9.9s)

## Exact Rational

You already know that division doesn't always come out even. $1 \div 3$ isn't a tidy whole number — but that doesn't mean the answer is fuzzy or approximate. In an **Exact Rational** system, $1 \div 3$ produces the value $\frac{1}{3}$, stored and carried forward exactly as that fraction, forever. No rounding, no drift, no "0.333..." unless you specifically ask to see it that way.

Here's the structural idea: a rational number is really a pair of integers, a numerator and a denominator, written $\frac{p}{q}$ with $q \neq 0$. The system keeps that pair intact. When you add, subtract, multiply, or divide rationals, it applies the exact rules you already know from fractions:

$$
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad+bc}{bd}, \qquad \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}
$$

and then simplifies by dividing out the greatest common divisor of the result, so $\frac{2}{4}$ becomes $\frac{1}{2}$ automatically. Every step stays a fraction — the *decimal* $0.5$ or $0.333...$ only appears if you explicitly convert it, and that conversion is a one-way street: once you round to a decimal, you can lose information that the fraction never lost.

**The rule:** decimals are a *view*, not the *value*. The value is the pair $(p,q)$; the decimal is just one way to display it, and often an incomplete one.

**Practice problem:** Using exact fractions (no decimals), compute $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6}$, and simplify your answer to lowest terms.

### simplification (8.4s)

## Simplification

You already know that $x + x$ and $2x$ are "the same" — you just rewrite one as the other in your head. Simplification is that instinct turned into a rigorous procedure: a set of rewrite rules applied to an expression until no rule can fire anymore, producing a canonical form.

Here's the structural piece worth sitting with: **simplification and zero-testing are different problems.** When you write `expr == 0` in a computer algebra system, you're not asking a question — you're building an *equation object*, a symbolic statement waiting to be evaluated. The function `is_zero()` is what actually answers it, and answering it can be surprisingly hard. Consider:

$$\sin^2(x) + \cos^2(x) - 1$$

A human recognizes this as $0$ instantly via the Pythagorean identity. But `simplify_full()` has to *search*: it applies a library of trig identities — double-angle, sum-to-product, Pythagorean — trying rewrites until the expression collapses or no further rule applies. This is why simplification isn't guaranteed to terminate at the "simplest" form; different rule orderings can produce different-looking (but equal) results.

**The rule:** two expressions are mathematically identical exactly when their difference simplifies to $0$ — that's the formal meaning of "same," and it's what `is_zero()` checks: $f \equiv g \iff \text{is\_zero}(f - g)$.

**Practice problem:** Using the identity $\cos(2x) = 1 - 2\sin^2(x)$, show by hand that
$$\cos(2x) + 2\sin^2(x) - 1$$
simplifies to $0$. Write out each substitution step — this is exactly what `simplify_full()` automates.

### calculus_sage (8.9s)

## Calculus Sage

You've learned to differentiate and integrate by hand, following rules like $\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$ or $\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C$. A Calculus Sage does exactly this — but as a *symbolic* computation, manipulating the expression itself rather than a number.

Structurally, a Calculus Sage combines two building blocks you've already met: a **symbolic expression** (the formula, kept in its exact algebraic form — $\sin(x^2)$, not a decimal) and a **simplification** engine (rules that rewrite expressions into cleaner equivalent forms). Differentiation, integration, limits, and series expansion are each just a set of transformation rules applied to that expression tree. For example, given $f(x) = x^3 - 2x$, the derivative operator applies the power rule term-by-term to produce $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2$ — still a formula, ready for further work.

The key safeguard is the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**, which states:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)
$$

A well-built Calculus Sage exploits this as a built-in check: after finding an antiderivative $F(x)$ for $f(x)$, it differentiates $F(x)$ right back and confirms the result matches $f(x)$ through simplification. If they don't match exactly, something in the integration step was wrong.

**Simple rule:** integrate, then differentiate your answer — if you don't recover the original function, the antiderivative is incorrect.

**Practice problem:** Let $f(x) = 6x^2 + 4$. Find an antiderivative $F(x)$, then differentiate $F(x)$ to verify $F'(x) = f(x)$.

### exact_linear_algebra (10.4s)

## Exact Linear Algebra

You've solved systems of equations by hand — combining rows, eliminating variables, checking your work. That process is called Gaussian elimination, and it has a target: **reduced row echelon form (RREF)**, a unique "simplest version" of any matrix that reveals a system's solutions at a glance.

The catch with doing this on a computer is that most software stores numbers as decimals with rounding error. Divide by 3 enough times and tiny errors creep in, until a matrix that should be singular looks almost-but-not-quite singular. Working over $\mathbb{Q}$, the rationals, sidesteps this entirely: $\frac{1}{3}$ stays $\frac{1}{3}$ forever, with no decimal approximation at any step.

This matters for three core computations. First, RREF of a matrix $A$: elementary row operations (swap, scale, add a multiple of one row to another) reduce $A$ to a form where each pivot is $1$ and is the only nonzero entry in its column. Second, the **kernel** (null space), $\ker(A) = \{x \in \mathbb{Q}^n : Ax = 0\}$ — found directly from RREF's free variables, with no rounding blurring which vectors are "basically zero." Third, **eigenvalues**: solving $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ exactly, over $\mathbb{Q}$ or an algebraic extension like $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, so $\lambda = \sqrt{2}$ appears as itself, not $1.41421356...$

The rule: exact arithmetic means exact answers, at the cost of numbers that can grow large as computations chain together — a tradeoff of speed for certainty.

**Practice:** Using exact fractions (no decimals), row-reduce
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$
to RREF, then find a nonzero vector in $\ker(A)$.

### manifolds_geometry (9.1s)

## Manifolds Geometry

You've met curved spaces intuitively — the surface of a sphere, the warped fabric of spacetime near a mass. Now let's give that intuition a precise skeleton. A manifold is a space that looks flat up close (like Euclidean space) even if it's curved overall. We describe it with a chart: a coordinate map $\phi: U \to \mathbb{R}^n$ that assigns coordinates $(x^1, \dots, x^n)$ to points in an open patch $U$. Where two charts overlap, transition functions tell you how to convert between coordinate systems — this is what lets the manifold be curved globally while flat locally.

The key structure is the metric tensor $g_{ij}$, which measures distance: $ds^2 = g_{ij}\, dx^i dx^j$ (using the Einstein summation convention). From the metric, we build the Levi-Civita connection $\nabla$, which tells vectors how to change as you move across the manifold — this generalizes the derivative to curved space. Applying the connection twice and comparing paths gives the Riemann curvature tensor $R^i_{\ jkl}$, the object that actually detects curvature. Flat space has $R = 0$ everywhere; a sphere doesn't.

SageManifolds turns this from symbol-pushing into computation: you declare a chart, input a metric, and Sage computes the Christoffel symbols, curvature tensors, and geodesic equations symbolically — exactly, not numerically approximated.

**Rule to remember**: curvature is measured by how much a vector changes when parallel-transported around a closed loop — zero change means flat, nonzero means curved.

**Practice problem**: For the flat 2D metric $ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2$, argue (without full computation) why every Christoffel symbol $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ must vanish.

### general_relativity (9.7s)

## General Relativity

You've probably heard the slogan: gravity isn't a force, it's the bending of spacetime. A massive object like the Sun curves the space around it, and planets moving in "straight lines" through that curved space trace out orbits. That's the picture. Now let's look at the structure underneath it.

Spacetime is described by a metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$, which tells you how to measure distances and time intervals at every point. Flat spacetime (no gravity) has a simple, constant metric. Near a massive object, $g_{\mu\nu}$ changes from point to point — that variation *is* the curvature.

To find how objects move through curved spacetime, you need the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}$, built from derivatives of $g_{\mu\nu}$. They appear in the geodesic equation — the curved-spacetime version of "moving in a straight line":

$$
\frac{d^2 x^{\lambda}}{d\tau^2} + \Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^{\mu}}{d\tau}\frac{dx^{\nu}}{d\tau} = 0
$$

Curvature itself is measured by the Riemann tensor, which combines into Einstein's field equations, $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$: matter and energy ($T_{\mu\nu}$) tell spacetime how to curve ($G_{\mu\nu}$), and curved spacetime tells matter how to move.

Computing $\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}$ by hand for a real metric involves dozens of derivative terms — tedious, but mechanical. This is exactly why physicists use tools like SageManifolds: give it a metric, and it symbolically computes Christoffel symbols, geodesics, and curvature tensors, the same calculations used in published research.

**Practice problem:** In flat 2D polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the metric is $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 d\theta^2$. One nonzero Christoffel symbol is $\Gamma^{r}_{\theta\theta} = -r$.
