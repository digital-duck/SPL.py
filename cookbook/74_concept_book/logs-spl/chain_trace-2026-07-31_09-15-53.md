## Data Privacy

You already know the basic idea: some information about you is personal, and sharing it without your consent can be harmful. But "just remove the name" turns out to be a surprisingly weak shield.

**The re-identification problem.** Suppose a hospital publishes a dataset with names stripped out, leaving only age, ZIP code, and diagnosis. Researcher Latanya Sweeney showed in 1997 that about 87% of Americans could be uniquely identified using just three fields: date of birth, gender, and 5-digit ZIP code. Removing a name does not make data anonymous — it just raises the effort needed to match it back to a real person.

**A formal standard: $k$-anonymity.** A dataset satisfies $k$-anonymity if every combination of *quasi-identifiers* (fields like age or ZIP that aren't direct names but can be linked to identity) appears in at least $k$ rows. In other words, any one person is hidden among at least $k - 1$ lookalikes.

$$\text{group size} \geq k$$

Higher $k$ means stronger privacy. $k = 1$ gives no protection at all — you're the only one with that profile. A common practical target is $k = 5$ or $k = 10$.

**The trade-off.** Achieving higher $k$ usually requires *generalizing* data — rounding ages into ranges (e.g., 25–34) or broadening ZIP codes to just the first 3 digits. Privacy goes up; precision goes down. This tension between privacy and utility is central to the field.

## Ethics In Data Science

**Recap:** You've probably heard that data science is powerful — it can predict diseases, recommend movies, and even influence elections. But power comes with responsibility. Ethics in data science is about asking not just *can we do this?* but *should we?*

**The Structure of the Problem: Bias**

The single most important concept to understand first is **bias** — a systematic error that causes a model to treat some groups unfairly compared to others.

Here's how it sneaks in: suppose you train a loan-approval model on historical bank decisions. If the bank historically denied loans to certain neighborhoods at higher rates, that pattern gets absorbed into the training data. The model learns to repeat the pattern — not because anyone programmed in discrimination, but because the *data reflected a biased past*. The model can score well on accuracy tests and still be deeply unfair.

This is sometimes called **historical bias** in the training data, and it's different from a coding error. You can fix a bug in code. Fixing data bias requires actively identifying which groups are affected and measuring outcomes separately for each group.

A useful first check: after a model is trained, compare its error rates across subgroups. If the model is wrong about one demographic twice as often as another, that's a signal worth investigating — even if overall accuracy looks fine.

**A Simple Rule to Remember**

High accuracy on the full dataset does not guarantee fair outcomes for every subgroup.

**Practice Problem**

Describe one way historical bias might appear in this model's predictions, and suggest one check you could run to detect it.

## Personally Identifiable Information

**Recap:** You already know that some information feels private — your diary entries, your passwords, your secrets. But privacy in the digital world gets more specific than just "stuff I don't want shared." There's a precise category called **Personally Identifiable Information**, or **PII**.

**The Structure:** PII is any data that can be used — alone or combined with other data — to identify a specific individual. Some pieces are *direct identifiers*: a full name, a Social Security number, a phone number, or a passport number. These point straight to one person. Others are *indirect identifiers*: your birthday, your ZIP code, your employer. Each one alone describes millions of people, but combine them and the crowd shrinks fast.

Researchers have shown that just three data points — birth date, gender, and ZIP code — can uniquely identify roughly **87%** of the U.S. population. That's the combinatorial power of PII: pieces that seem harmless alone become identifying when linked together.

**A Simple Rule:** Think of a *uniqueness threshold*. A piece of information is effectively PII if combining it with a small number of other available facts narrows the population down to one (or very few) individuals:

$$\text{Anonymity} \approx \frac{\text{population matching all known attributes}}{\text{total population}}$$

The closer that fraction gets to $\frac{1}{N}$ for some specific person, the more the data acts like PII.

**Practice Problem:** You know these facts about a classmate: they are 14 years old, left-handed, live in ZIP code 94102, and were born in February. Would this combination likely constitute PII? ## Regulatory Compliance

You already know that rules exist for a reason — speed limits protect pedestrians, food labels help people avoid allergens, and building codes keep structures from collapsing. Regulatory compliance is the formal name for the practice of following rules set by governments, agencies, or industry bodies. But it goes further than just "not breaking the law."

**The structure beneath the surface.** Compliance operates in layers. At the top sits a *regulatory body* — an authority like the FDA (food and drugs), the SEC (financial markets), or the EPA (environment). It issues *regulations*: written rules with specific, measurable requirements. Below that, organizations create *internal policies* that translate those rules into day-to-day procedures. Finally, *audits* and *reporting* check whether the procedures are actually followed. Think of it as:

$$\text{Regulation} \supseteq \text{Internal Policy} \supseteq \text{Daily Practice}$$

Each inner layer must *satisfy* the outer one — your policy can be stricter than the law, but never looser.

**A simple pattern to remember.** Compliance failures usually trace back to one of three gaps: the rule was *unknown*, the rule was known but the process *didn't implement* it, or the process existed but *wasn't followed* consistently. Fixing compliance means closing whichever gap exists.

**Practice problem.** A new school rule says students must wear ID badges *in all hallways during school hours*. A student wears a badge only during lunch. Which of the three gaps does this represent — unknown rule, missing process, or inconsistent follow-through? Explain your reasoning in two sentences.

## HIPAA

You've probably heard that doctors aren't allowed to share your medical records with just anyone — not your school, not your employer, not even a curious neighbor. That protective wall has a name: **HIPAA**, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, passed by the U.S. Congress in 1996.

### The Structure Behind the Rule

HIPAA creates a category called **Protected Health Information (PHI)** — any data that links a person's identity to their health. This includes obvious things like diagnoses and prescriptions, but also subtler details: dates of treatment, zip codes, and even device serial numbers from medical equipment. The law recognizes 18 specific identifiers that, if attached to health data, turn ordinary information into PHI.

The core rule is called the **Privacy Rule**, and it defines exactly who may access PHI. These authorized parties are called **covered entities** (hospitals, clinics, insurance companies) and their **business associates** (billing services, cloud storage vendors). Everyone else is outside the circle.

A simplified way to model authorized disclosure:

$$\text{Disclosure OK} \iff \text{Recipient} \in \text{CoveredEntities} \cup \text{BusinessAssociates} \cup \{\text{Patient}\}$$

When data must travel outside this set — say, for medical research — HIPAA allows it only with explicit patient **authorization** or after the data is **de-identified** (all 18 identifiers removed).

### The Pattern to Remember

PHI = identity + health data. Remove the identity link → no longer PHI → fewer restrictions apply.
