### prefix (0.0s)

## Prefix

You already know a prefix changes meaning by attaching to the front of a word — *un*happy, *re*write, *pre*view. But look closer at what these little pieces actually do: each one is a **steerer**. It doesn't usually change what part of speech the word is (a verb stays a verb, a noun stays a noun). Instead, it grabs the stem and points its meaning in a direction — inward, backward, across, before, or together.

Think of the stem as a compass needle and the prefix as a hand turning it. *Port* means "carry." Add *trans-* (across) and you get *transport* — carry across. Add *ex-* (out) and you get *export* — carry out. Add *im-* (in) and you get *import* — carry in. Same needle, three different directions.

$$
\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = transport
$$

$$
\underbrace{ex}_{\text{out}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = export
$$

Once you notice this pattern, you can group prefixes by the direction they steer:

- **inward**: *in-*, *im-* (import, inhale)
- **backward**: *re-*, *retro-* (retreat, retrospect)
- **across**: *trans-* (transfer)
- **before**: *pre-*, *fore-* (preview, forecast)
- **together**: *co-*, *con-*, *syn-* (cooperate, synchronize)

Knowing the direction a prefix points lets you guess at unfamiliar words instead of memorizing each one separately.

**Practice:** The stem *dict* means "say" or "speak." Using what you know about direction, figure out what *predict* and *contradict* each mean, and explain the difference using the compass idea above.

### stem (0.0s)

## Stem

You already know that a word is built by attaching extra pieces onto the front or back of a central part. That central part is called the **stem** — the piece that does the naming work. It's what you'd point to if someone asked, "What is this word even about?"

Every English word has at least one stem, and that stem usually traces back to Latin, Greek, or Old English (Germanic) roots. Take the word *unbelievable*. Strip away *un-* and *-able*, and you're left with *believ(e)* — that's the stem. It carries the actual idea of trust or conviction. The other pieces just adjust that idea: *un-* flips it to a negative, and *-able* turns it into a description of possibility.

$$
\underbrace{un}_{\text{not}} + \underbrace{believ}_{\text{stem: trust}} + \underbrace{able}_{\text{capable of}} = unbelievable
$$

Notice the stem doesn't have to be a complete word by itself once you remove the *-e* — that's normal. What matters is that it's the fixed, meaning-carrying anchor. Everything attached to it depends on the stem to make sense; the stem never depends on them.

A simple rule: to find the stem, strip away every piece attached to the front or back. Whatever's left — the part that still points to a real-world idea (believing, act, form, port) — is the stem.

**Practice:** Identify the stem in each of these words, and name one attached piece: *reformat*, *disagreement*, *transportable*.

### in- (11.2s)

## In-

You've met *in-* as the prefix that turns things inward or negative — *input*, *inactive*. But look closer at this graph's node for *in-*, and you'll notice it's not one fixed shape. It's a shape-shifter: the same prefix wearing different masks depending on what sound comes right after it.

Say "inpossible" out loud. Awkward, right? Your mouth doesn't want to move from the *n* sound straight into a *p* sound — they're made in different places (tongue vs. lips). So English speakers, over centuries, smoothed that seam by letting *in-* borrow the sound of the next letter. That's how *impossible* was born.

$$\underbrace{in}_{\text{into, not}} + \underbrace{possible}_{\text{root}} \xrightarrow{p\text{-sound clash}} impossible$$

The pattern is predictable, not random:
- Before **p, b, m** → becomes **im-** (*impossible, imbalance, immature*)
- Before **l** → becomes **il-** (*illegal, illogical*)
- Before **r** → becomes **ir-** (*irregular, irresponsible*)
- Everywhere else → stays **in-** (*inactive, incorrect*)

This is called *assimilation* — one sound "assimilating" into its neighbor, like adjusting your stride to match someone walking beside you. Once you know the rule, you can predict the disguise before you even see the word.

**Practice:** Using the pattern above, figure out which form of *in-* attaches to each root, and write the resulting word: *legible*, *mobile*, *rational*, *decisive*.

### port (8.7s)

## Port

Think of a port as a doorway between two worlds. In a harbor, a port is where ships load and unload cargo — goods move from one place to another through that single opening. The Latin root *portare* means "to carry," and it's the same carrying idea whether you're talking about a seaport, a mountain pass called a "portage," or a laptop's USB port where data flows in and out.

Once you know *port* means "carry," entire families of English words open up. A porter carries luggage. A transport carries something across (*trans-* = across). An export carries something out (*ex-* = out), while an import carries something in (*in-* = in). Even a portable object is simply one that can be carried. The pattern is always the same: take the carrying root, attach a prefix that tells you the direction or manner of carrying, and the new word explains itself.

$$\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = transport$$

$$\underbrace{ex}_{\text{out}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = export$$

**The rule:** whenever you see *port* buried inside a longer word, ask "what's being carried, and which direction?" The prefix answers the direction; *port* itself always answers "carry."

**Practice problem:** The word *report* combines *re-* (back) with *port* (carry). Using the pattern above, explain in one sentence why "reporting the news" is literally "carrying information back" to an audience.

### con- (11.1s)

## Con-

You already know the feeling: someone says "let's do this *together*," and something shifts — it's not just you anymore, it's a *we*. That's the heart of **con-**. It's a prefix that means "together" or "with," and its job is to take an action or a state and turn it into something shared.

Think of con- as a gathering signal. Drop it in front of a root, and suddenly the root isn't happening alone — it's happening *jointly*, with others pulled into the same motion. "Connect" isn't just "nect" (from a Latin root meaning to bind) — it's binding *with* something else. "Congregate" isn't just gathering — it's gathering *together*, as a group. "Conspire" isn't just breathing or planning — it's plotting *with* someone else, side by side.

$$
\underbrace{con}_{\text{together, with}} + \underbrace{root}_{\text{some action or state}} = \text{that action or state, done jointly}
$$

Here's the pattern worth noticing: con- doesn't change *what* the root means — it changes *who's involved*. A root about connecting stays about connecting; con- just insists it can't happen solo. That's why con- shows up so often in words about cooperation, agreement, and shared effort: *concur* (run together, i.e. agree), *convene* (come together), *consent* (feel together, i.e. agree).

One more clue: con- often shape-shifts depending on the letter after it — becoming *com-* before certain sounds (like "compose") or *col-*, *cor-* in other spots (like "collect," "correspond"). Same meaning, different disguise.

### dict (9.8s)

## Dict

You already know that a dictionary is where you go to look up words. But the word *dictionary* itself — and a whole family of its cousins — is built from a Latin root that means something more active than "a book of definitions." The root is *dicere*, "to say," and it survives in English as **dict**.

Think about what a dictionary actually does: it *tells you* what a word means. That's the "saying" idea hiding right inside the name. Once you see it, you start noticing **dict** everywhere words involve speaking, declaring, or ruling on something.

$$
\underbrace{dict}_{\text{to say}} + \underbrace{ion + ary}_{\text{noun-forming endings}} = dictionary
$$

The root doesn't always sit at the front. In *pre**dict**, the prefix *pre-* ("before") combines with *dict* to mean "to say beforehand" — a prediction is literally something said in advance. In *ver**dict***, the Latin *verus* ("true") joins *dict* to make "a truly spoken judgment" — exactly what a jury delivers in court. And a *dict**ator*** is someone who *says* — that is, commands — without needing anyone's agreement.

$$
\underbrace{pre}_{\text{before}} + \underbrace{dict}_{\text{to say}} = predict
$$

**The pattern:** whenever a word contains *dict*, ask yourself: who is "saying" something, and what kind of saying is it — a guess, a ruling, a command, a definition?

### ject (8.7s)

## Ject

You already know *ject* as the throwing root — think *reject*, throwing something back. Now let's look at how it actually behaves when it teams up with other pieces of Latin to build words.

*Ject* rarely stands alone in English. It's a **bound morpheme** — a piece that only works attached to something else, usually a prefix that tells you *which direction* the throwing goes. That direction is the whole trick to unlocking meaning from an unfamiliar word.

$$\underbrace{re}_{back} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = reject \text{ (throw back, refuse)}$$

$$\underbrace{in}_{into} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = inject \text{ (throw in)}$$

$$\underbrace{pro}_{forward} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = project \text{ (throw forward)}$$

$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = trajectory \text{ (path thrown across space)}$$

Notice the pattern: the prefix isn't decoration, it's doing real semantic work. Once you know *pro-* means "forward," a *projector* makes instant sense — it's a machine that throws light forward onto a screen. This is the payoff of learning roots: you're not memorizing words one by one anymore, you're memorizing a small set of directional prefixes and reusing them across dozens of throw-words.

**Practice:** The prefix *ex-* means "out." Using the pattern above, figure out what *eject* should literally mean — then check it against how the word is actually used in a sentence like "the pilot had to eject from the plane." Does your literal meaning match?

### pre- (11.6s)

## Pre-

You already know that "pre-" means "before" — a preview happens before the main show, and a preschool comes before regular school. But let's look at what this little piece actually does when it attaches to a word.

"Pre-" is a prefix — it always sits at the front, and it never appears alone. Its job is to grab whatever root it's attached to and shove it earlier in time. Think of it as a steering wheel that turns the timeline backward: whatever the root describes, "pre-" makes it happen sooner than the normal or expected moment.

Here's the build:

$$\underbrace{pre}_{\text{before}} + \underbrace{view}_{\text{to see}} = preview$$

"View" alone just means to look at something. Add "pre-," and now you're looking at it *before* everyone else does — before the movie's release, before the meeting.

The pattern holds across many words: *pre-heat* (heat the oven before you cook), *pre-pay* (pay before you receive the service), *pre-historic* (before history was recorded), *pre-view* (see it before the release). In every case, "pre-" doesn't change what the root *means* — it changes *when* that meaning happens. That's the difference between a prefix like this one and a root: "view" carries the core meaning, "pre-" just adjusts its timing.

**Practice:** Take the root word "caution" (meaning: care or warning). Attach "pre-" to it, write the new word, and explain in one sentence when someone would use it — for example, before a storm hits or before a trip. What does the new word let you do that "caution" alone couldn't?

### re- (11.6s)

## Re-

You already know that *re-* means "again" or "back" — like in *replay*, *return*, or *rewind*. But once you start hunting for it, you'll notice *re-* isn't just one simple idea. It actually points in two different directions, and telling them apart is where the real skill kicks in.

Sometimes *re-* means **doing something again**: *rewrite* (write it again), *retest* (test it again), *rebuild* (build it again). Other times it means **going back to where something started**: *return* (turn back), *recall* (call something back to mind), *reclaim* (claim something back).

$$
\underbrace{re}_{\text{again / back}} + \underbrace{turn}_{\text{root: to spin/change direction}} = \text{return (to turn back)}
$$

Here's the pattern: ask yourself whether the word describes a *repeated action* or a *backward motion*. If you can insert the word "again" and it still makes sense — *redo* = "do again" — you're in the repeat camp. If "again" sounds odd but "back" fits — *retreat* = "treat back"? No — but "pull back," yes — you're in the return camp. Some roots, like *-turn* or *-call*, lean naturally toward "back," while action roots like *-write* or *-do* lean toward "again."

One tricky twist: a few *re-* words have drifted so far in meaning that neither "again" nor "back" quite fits anymore — like *relax* (not "lax again") or *remember* (not exactly "member back," though it's close to "put back together in mind"). Language shifts over time, and prefixes sometimes fossilize into words that no longer show their seams.

**Practice:** Sort these three words into "again" or "back," and explain your choice in one sentence each: *rewind*, *repay*, *refresh*.

### spect (10.7s)

## Spect

You already know the feel of this root: *spectacle*, *inspect*, *respect*, *spectator*. Whether you're watching a show, checking something closely, or holding someone in high regard, there's a kind of "looking" underneath it all. That's because **spect** comes from the Latin verb *specere*, meaning "to look" or "to watch." It's one of the most productive roots in English — once you can spot it, dozens of words start making sense at once.

Here's the structure: *spect* is a **root**, not a whole word on its own. It needs a partner — usually a prefix — to become a real English word. The prefix tells you *what kind* of looking is happening:

$$
\underbrace{in}_{\text{into}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{inspect (to look into closely)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{re}_{\text{back}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{respect (to look back at, with regard)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{pro}_{\text{forward}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{prospect (to look forward to)}
$$

Notice the pattern: the *direction* word (prefix) changes, but the *action* word (spect) stays fixed as "look." This is how Latin roots work as building blocks — swap the prefix, and you get a whole new shade of meaning without changing the core idea.

**Simple rule:** when you meet an unfamiliar word with *spect* inside it, isolate the prefix first and ask, "which direction or manner of looking does this prefix suggest?"

### struct (8.2s)

## Struct

You already know that a *structure* is something built — a building, an organization, even a sentence. But have you ever wondered why "structure," "construct," and "destroy" all sound related, even though they seem to mean different things? They share a hidden root: the Latin verb *struere*, meaning "to build" or "to pile up."

This root shows up disguised as **struct**, and it's one of the most productive building blocks in English. Once you spot it, you'll start seeing it everywhere.

$$
\underbrace{con}_{\text{together}} + \underbrace{struct}_{\text{build}} = \text{construct (to build something by putting pieces together)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{de}_{\text{down, apart}} + \underbrace{struct}_{\text{build}} = \text{destruct (to tear down / unbuild)}
$$

Notice the pattern: the prefix tells you the *direction* of building, while **struct** always supplies the core idea of assembling or arranging pieces. Add *in-* (into) and you get **instruct** — literally "to build knowledge into" someone. Add *re-* (again) and you get **restructure** — to build something over again in a new way.

This is why struct-words often describe things with parts and organization: a building's *structure*, a society's *social structure*, even a sentence's grammatical *structure* — all are things assembled from smaller pieces according to some plan.

**Rule of thumb:** if a word contains *struct*, ask what's being built, and let the prefix tell you which direction — together, apart, into, or again.

### trans- (8.5s)

## Trans-

You've already met *trans-* in words like *transport* (carry across) or *transform* (shape across into something new). It's a prefix that acts like a steerer, pointing a word's meaning toward crossing a boundary — sometimes a physical one, sometimes an idea.

Here's how it works structurally. *Trans-* attaches to a root, and the root supplies *what* is crossing, while *trans-* supplies the *direction*: over, across, or through.

$$
\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{fer}_{\text{carry}} = transfer
$$

$$
\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{late}_{\text{carried (from Latin } latus\text{)}} = translate
$$

Notice the pattern: *transfer* moves something physical (money, a file, a passenger), while *translate* moves something abstract (meaning, from one language into another). The boundary being crossed doesn't have to be a line on a map — it can be a language, a state of matter, or even a category of thing.

Simple rule: whenever you see *trans-*, ask yourself two questions — *what* is crossing, and *what boundary* is it crossing? A *transaction* crosses ownership (money changes hands). A *transplant* crosses bodies (an organ moves from one body to another). A *transcript* crosses form (spoken words become written ones).

This is why *trans-* words often show up in science, business, and grammar alike — the prefix doesn't care what kind of boundary it's describing, only that something moves *over* it.

**Practice:** The word *transparent* comes from *trans-* + *parent* (Latin *parere*, "to appear"). Using the two questions above, explain in one sentence what is "crossing" and what boundary it crosses when light passes through a transparent window.

### construct (8.9s)

## Construct

You already sense that to *construct* something is to build it deliberately — an argument, a story, a sentence — piece by piece rather than by accident. The word itself is built the same way it describes: it comes from Latin, joining two smaller pieces into one meaning.

$$\underbrace{con-}_{\text{together}} + \underbrace{struct}_{\text{to build, to pile}} = \text{construct}$$

*Struct* traces back to the Latin verb *struere*, "to pile up" or "to arrange" — think of stones stacked into a wall. Add *con-*, meaning "together," and you get the sense of *gathering pieces into a single standing structure*. That's why "construct" works as both a verb (to build something) and a noun (a thing that was built, like a "social construct" — an idea a society assembled together rather than found ready-made in nature).

Notice how this same root family shows up elsewhere once you know the pattern: *destruct* (build apart, tear down), *instruct* (build knowledge into someone), *structure* (the built thing itself). Spotting *struct* inside a word is a strong clue that building, arranging, or assembling is involved.

A simple rule: when you see *con-* attached to a root, expect the meaning "bringing parts together" — *convene* (come together), *connect* (tie together), *construct* (build together).

### import (9.5s)

## Import

You already know that if you *export* something, you carry it out. **Import** does the opposite: it means bringing something *in* — carrying a good, an idea, or a custom from outside into a place. A country imports coffee from another country; a person imports new habits after traveling abroad.

Look closely at the spelling, though: it's *im-port*, not *in-port*. The prefix hasn't changed meaning — it's still *in-*, "into" — but it changed its *shape*. This is called assimilation: when *in-* sits in front of a word starting with *p* (or *b* or *m*), it morphs into *im-* because it's easier to say. Try saying "in-port" quickly — your mouth naturally wants to shift the *n* sound toward the *p*. English spelling just locked in what your mouth was already doing.

$$
\underbrace{im\text{-}}_{\text{into (shape-shifted from }in\text{-})} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = import
$$

You met this same *port* brick in *transport* (carry across) and *export* (carry out). And you'll meet this same disguise trick again — *in-* becomes *im-* any time it lands before *p*, *b*, or *m*: think of words like *impossible* or *immortal*. Once you spot the pattern, the "irregular" spelling stops looking random and starts looking like a rule.

**Practice:** The word *inspect* uses the *un-shifted* form of *in-*, because *s* doesn't trigger the change. Using what you just learned, predict how the prefix *in-* would spell itself in front of a *b*-word — then check: does "in-balance" or "imbalance" look right to you? ### inspect (10.7s)

## Inspect

You already know that when a teacher tells you to *inspect* a poem or a specimen, you're supposed to look at it more closely than usual — not just glance and move on. But have you noticed how "inspect" itself is built from pieces, just like the careful looking it describes?

Break the word open and you find two parts. The prefix *in-* means "into" or "inward" — it's the same piece you see in *inside* and *interior*. The root *-spect* comes from a Latin word meaning "to look." Put them together, and the word literally means "to look into":

$$\underbrace{in}_{\text{into}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{inspect, "to look into"}$$

That's a neat trick this word plays: it doesn't just *tell* you to look inward — it *shows* you, in its own structure. The prefix physically steers your attention "in," toward the center of whatever you're examining, the same way the instruction steers your eyes toward the details of a painting or the fine print of a contract.

Once you notice this pattern, you can spot *-spect* everywhere. A *spectator* is someone who looks at a show. A *spectacle* is something worth looking at. *Retrospect* means looking back (*retro-* = "back"). Each time, the root *-spect* carries the "looking," while the prefix tells you the direction or manner of the look.

### predict (9.5s)

## Predict

You already know that to predict something is to guess what happens next — before it happens. But look closer at the word itself, and it tells you exactly how it works. It's built from two Latin pieces stacked together:

$$\underbrace{pre}_{\text{before}} + \underbrace{dict}_{\text{to say}} = predict$$

*Dict* is the same saying-root you find in *dictate* (say something out loud for someone to write down) and *diction* (the words someone chooses to say). On its own, *dict* just means "speak" or "declare" — it doesn't care about time. What turns plain saying into *predicting* is the prefix *pre-*, which shoves the root forward in time, aiming it at the future.

Think of *pre-* as a steering wheel you can attach to almost any saying-root. Point it backward in time and you get words like *postdict* (rare, but it exists — explaining something after it happened). Point it forward, and *dict* becomes a forecast instead of a statement. That's the whole trick: the root supplies the *action* (saying), and the prefix supplies the *timing* (before).

**The pattern:** whenever you see *pre-* attached to a root, ask what the root does on its own, then imagine that action happening early — before the main event. *Preheat* (heat before cooking), *preview* (see before release), *pretest* (test before the real one).

### reject (10.1s)

## Reject

You already know *reject* means to turn something down — a rejected application, a rejected idea. But look closer at how the word is built, and you'll see it's actually a tiny diagram of motion.

The root *-ject-* comes from Latin *iacere*, "to throw." You've met it before in *project* (throw forward) and *inject* (throw in). The piece doing the work in *reject* is the prefix *re-*, meaning "back." So the word isn't just "no" — it's a throw that goes backward, toward the sender.

$$
\underbrace{re}_{\text{back}} + \underbrace{ject}_{\text{throw}} = \text{reject: to throw back}
$$

That's the structure: this whole family of words is built the same way, with a direction-word (*re-*, *pro-*, *in-*, *e-*) bolted onto the same throwing root, and the direction tells you the meaning. *Reject* a proposal, and you're mentally hurling it back at whoever sent it. *Reject* a part on an assembly line, and it gets physically sent back down the line, away from the finished product.

Once you notice *re-* as "back" or "again," it starts showing up everywhere — *return*, *repay*, *reflect* (bend back), *respond* (answer back). Recognizing the direction-piece is a shortcut: even if you've never seen a word before, the prefix often tells you which way the action is pointing.

### transport (9.2s)

## Transport

Carrying something across is one of the oldest human acts — food, goods, people, even ideas — and "transport" is the word English built to name it precisely. You've likely felt the intuition already: a truck transports cargo, a plane transports passengers. But look closer at the word itself, and you'll find it's not a single unit — it's a tiny sentence in disguise.

$$\underbrace{trans-}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = transport$$

The root *port* comes from Latin *portare*, "to carry," and it shows up as a hidden thread running through many English words: *import* (carry in), *export* (carry out), *support* (carry from below), *deport* (carry away). Each time, a small directional prefix attaches to the same carrying root and steers it toward a different meaning. Think of *port* as a vehicle and the prefix as the driver deciding which way it goes.

The pattern to notice: prefixes like *trans-*, *im-*, *ex-*, *sup-* aren't decoration — they're instructions. Once you know *port* means "carry," you can often guess an unfamiliar *port*-word just by translating its prefix. *Trans-* almost always means "across" or "through" (as in *transatlantic*, *transform*), so *transport* literally means "carry across."

### prefix_root_composition (9.5s)

## Prefix Root Composition

You already know that prefixes change a root's direction — *export* leaves, *import* enters. Now look closer: the prefix doesn't just flavor the word, it aims the root like a compass needle, and once you see the aiming happen, dozens of "random" vocabulary words snap into a single pattern.

Take the root **spect** (to look) and **port** (to carry). Each prefix points them somewhere specific:

$$\underbrace{in}_{into} + \underbrace{spect}_{look} = inspect \;\; (\text{look } into)$$

$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = transport \;\; (\text{carry } across)$$

The same logic builds words from other roots too:

$$\underbrace{pre}_{before} + \underbrace{dict}_{say} = predict \;\; (\text{say } before)$$

$$\underbrace{con}_{together} + \underbrace{struct}_{build} = construct \;\; (\text{build } together)$$

$$\underbrace{re}_{back} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = reject \;\; (\text{throw } back)$$

$$\underbrace{im}_{into} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = import \;\; (\text{carry } into)$$

Notice the pattern: the root supplies the *action* (look, carry, say, build, throw), and the prefix supplies the *direction* (into, across, before, together, back). Once you memorize a small set of prefixes — five or six cover most of English — you can predict the meaning of words you've never seen, just by asking: "which way is this prefix pointing the root?"

## Cross Language Transfer

You already know that "transport" is built from pieces:

$$\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = \text{transport}$$

Here's the exciting part: that same brick, *port* ("carry"), doesn't just work in English. It shows up, barely changed, in Spanish, French, and Portuguese too — because all four languages borrowed it from the same Latin root, *portare*.

$$\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} + \underbrace{ar}_{\text{verb ending}} = \textit{transportar} \text{ (Spanish/Portuguese)}$$

Look what happened: the *meaning-carrying* brick (*port*) stayed almost identical, while only the grammar wrapper around it (the verb ending) changed to fit each language's rules. Once you notice this, you start seeing it everywhere — *predict* and *predicción/prédiction* both keep the brick *dict* ("say"), just dressed in different grammatical clothing.

This is why word-detective skills transfer across languages: you're not memorizing new vocabulary from zero, you're recognizing an old brick wearing a new outfit. The Simple Rule is: **when languages share a common ancestor, they often share the same meaning-bricks, even if the surrounding grammar differs.** That's also why the same checking method (the "verifier") that confirms an English breakdown is correct can confirm a Spanish or French one too — the underlying schema, brick + brick = whole, doesn't change, only the language does.
