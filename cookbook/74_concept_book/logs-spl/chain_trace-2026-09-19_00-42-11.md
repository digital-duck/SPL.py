## Central Nervous System

The central nervous system (CNS) comprises the brain and spinal cord, the two structures that receive, integrate, and generate the outgoing commands for nearly all coordinated behavior in vertebrates. Unlike the peripheral nervous system, which carries signals to and from the body's periphery, the CNS is enclosed in bone — the skull and vertebral column — and cushioned by cerebrospinal fluid and three protective membrane layers (the meninges). This anatomical isolation reflects a functional truth: CNS tissue integrates enormous volumes of information and cannot easily regenerate if damaged, so protection is structurally built in.

Consider a concrete case: touching a hot stove. Sensory neurons in the skin detect the temperature change and fire action potentials that travel along peripheral nerves into the spinal cord. There, interneurons can trigger an immediate withdrawal reflex — a fast, local circuit that pulls the hand back before the signal even finishes traveling to the brain. Simultaneously, the sensory signal ascends the spinal cord to the brain, where it is processed by the somatosensory cortex (registering pain and location), the thalamus (relaying and filtering the signal), and potentially the prefrontal cortex (deciding whether to yell, check for a burn, or run cold water on the hand). This example illustrates the CNS's core division of labor: the spinal cord handles rapid, stereotyped responses, while the brain performs slower, more flexible integration, interpretation, and decision-making.

Applying this concept requires recognizing which CNS structure handles a given task and why lesion location determines symptom pattern — a principle central to clinical neuroscience. A spinal cord injury at the neck (cervical level) disrupts signals to and from everything below it, potentially causing paralysis in both arms and legs (quadriplegia), because all ascending sensory and descending motor pathways for the lower body pass through that segment. An injury lower in the spine (lumbar level) affects only the legs, since arm and torso pathways branch off above that point. Similarly, a stroke damaging the brain's left hemisphere motor cortex typically impairs movement on the right side of the body, because motor pathways cross (decussate) as they descend from the brain into the spinal cord. Correctly diagnosing the location and extent of neurological damage — from a physical exam alone — depends on understanding this precise mapping between CNS anatomy and function.

## Brain Imaging Techniques

Brain imaging techniques are methods for producing visual representations of the brain's anatomy or activity, used in both research and clinical diagnosis. They fall into two broad categories: **structural imaging**, which reveals physical anatomy, and **functional imaging**, which reveals patterns of ongoing neural activity.

Structural techniques include CT (computed tomography), MRI (magnetic resonance imaging), and PET (positron emission tomography) when used for tracer-based anatomical mapping. CT uses rotating X-ray beams to reconstruct cross-sectional images, differentiating tissues by density — fast and widely available, making it the default choice for detecting acute events like hemorrhage or skull fracture in an emergency room. MRI instead exploits the magnetic behavior of hydrogen nuclei in water: a strong magnetic field aligns these nuclei, a radiofrequency pulse briefly disturbs that alignment, and the signal emitted as nuclei relax back is used to reconstruct detailed images. Because soft tissues differ in water content, MRI distinguishes gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid far more clearly than CT, at the cost of longer scan times and higher expense.

Functional imaging, principally fMRI, tracks *activity* rather than structure. It measures the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal: active neurons demand more oxygen, triggering a local increase in oxygenated blood flow that alters the surrounding magnetic field in a detectable way. fMRI does not measure neurons firing directly — it measures a downstream vascular consequence, with a delay of several seconds. PET can also be used functionally by injecting a radioactive tracer (commonly attached to glucose) and detecting where it accumulates, revealing metabolic activity, though at lower temporal resolution than fMRI and with radiation exposure.

**Worked example.** A researcher wants to know which brain region activates when a subject performs a memory task. The design compares BOLD signal during the task period against a resting baseline: voxels showing a statistically significant increase are candidate regions. If instead the goal were to rule out a tumor or confirm a stroke's location, structural MRI or CT would be the appropriate tool, since these questions concern anatomy, not moment-to-moment activity.

**Problem-solving application.** Choosing a technique requires weighing tradeoffs in *spatial resolution* (how precisely a signal can be localized in the brain), *temporal resolution* (how quickly changes can be tracked), invasiveness, and cost. fMRI offers good spatial resolution (millimeters) but poor temporal resolution (seconds), since it depends on blood flow rather than direct electrical activity. A researcher who instead needs millisecond-level tracking of neural timing would supplement or replace fMRI with EEG, illustrating that imaging choice is itself a problem-solving decision driven by the specific scientific question at hand.
