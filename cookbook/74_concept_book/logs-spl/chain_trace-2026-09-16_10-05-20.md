## 子

You already know 子 as a simple pictograph: a swaddled baby, arms wrapped snug, head round at the top. That's the "child" meaning — still visible in words like 孩子 (child) and 儿子 (son). But 子 lives a double life in Chinese, and once you notice it, you'll see it everywhere.

Look at ordinary nouns: 桌子 (table), 椅子 (chair), 帽子 (hat), 房子 (house). None of these have anything to do with babies. Here, 子 has drifted from "meaning" to "job" — it's a grammatical suffix that signals "this word is a noun." Linguists call this kind of shift *grammaticalization*: a word with real meaning slowly hollows out into a marker that just labels a word's category.

$$
\underbrace{桌}_{table} + \underbrace{子}_{noun\ suffix} = 桌子 \ (\text{table})
$$

The pattern to remember: single-syllable nouns in Chinese often feel "too short" to stand alone comfortably, so 子 gets attached to round them out — much like English adds *-er* to a verb to make a noun (*teach* → *teacher*), except 子 doesn't change the meaning, just the shape.

Notice too that when 子 works this way, it loses its tone and is pronounced lightly — a small sonic clue that it's playing a grammatical role, not a meaningful one.
