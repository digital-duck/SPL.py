## Conductance

Conductance measures how easily electric charge flows through a given pathway — an ion channel, a patch of membrane, or a segment of cytoplasm. It is the reciprocal of resistance, $G = 1/R$, and is measured in siemens ($\text{S} = \text{A}/\text{V}$). Where resistance describes how much a pathway impedes current, conductance describes how much it permits current — a single scalar that captures channel density, pore geometry, and ion selectivity all at once.

The defining relationship comes directly from Ohm's law. Current through a conductive pathway is:

$$
I = G \cdot V
$$

where $V$ is the voltage (electrochemical driving force) across the pathway and $I$ is the resulting current. Because $G$ folds resistance's inverse into a single term, a *larger* conductance means a *larger* current for the same driving force — the opposite of how resistance behaves. Physiologically, $G$ rises or falls as channels open or close: more open channels means a larger conducting pathway means a higher $G$.

**Worked example.** Suppose a patch of membrane has a total potassium conductance of $G = 2\ \text{nS}$ (a value reflecting how many potassium channels happen to be open at that moment). If the driving force across the membrane at that instant is $V = 20\ \text{mV}$, the resulting current is:

$$
I = G V = (2 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{S})(0.02\ \text{V}) = 4 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{A} = 40\ \text{pA}
$$

**Problem-solving application.** This relationship lets you predict how a cell's electrical behavior changes when channel populations shift — the basis of neuronal excitability. If a drug blocks half the open potassium channels, the pathway's conductance is cut in half, from 2 nS to 1 nS. For the same 20 mV driving force, the current drops proportionally, from 40 pA to 20 pA — because $I$ scales linearly with $G$ whenever $V$ is fixed. Conversely, if a neuron's conductance for a given ion increases sharply (as happens when voltage-gated channels open during an action potential), the membrane's voltage will shift rapidly toward that ion's equilibrium potential, since higher conductance means that pathway dominates the total current balance across the membrane. Working through $G$, $V$, and $I$ together — and tracking how a change in one forces a change in the others — is the standard toolkit for modeling how shifts in channel behavior translate into measurable electrical signals.
