### atom (8.7s)

## Atom

An atom is the smallest unit of matter that retains the chemical identity of an element. Every atom consists of a dense central **nucleus**, made of positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons. The number of protons — the **atomic number** — defines which element the atom is: an atom with 6 protons is always carbon, whether it has 6, 7, or 8 neutrons (giving carbon-12, carbon-13, or carbon-14, its isotopes). Because protons and electrons carry equal and opposite charges, a neutral atom has the same number of each; gaining or losing electrons produces a charged ion instead.

**Worked example.** Consider a neutral atom of oxygen, atomic number 8. It has 8 protons and 8 electrons. If its mass number (protons + neutrons) is 16, it has 8 neutrons — this is the common isotope oxygen-16. Now suppose the same atom gains two electrons, forming the oxide ion, $\text{O}^{2-}$. It still has 8 protons (still oxygen), but now 10 electrons, giving it a net charge of $-2$. Note that gaining or losing electrons never changes which element the atom is — only proton number does that.

**Problem-solving application.** Atomic structure lets you predict an atom's behavior directly from the periodic table. Suppose you're told an atom has 17 protons, 18 neutrons, and 18 electrons. First, identify the element from the proton count: 17 protons corresponds to chlorine (Cl). Second, calculate its mass number: $17 + 18 = 35$, so it's the isotope chlorine-35. Third, compare protons to electrons: 17 protons but 18 electrons means one extra negative charge, so this is the chloride ion, $\text{Cl}^-$ — the same ion found in table salt (NaCl).

This kind of reasoning — using proton count to fix identity, neutron count to determine the isotope, and the proton–electron balance to determine charge — is the basic toolkit for interpreting any atomic notation you'll encounter in chemistry, from isotope labels used in radiometric dating to the ions that drive nerve signaling in biology.

### molecule (11.0s)

## Molecule

A molecule is a discrete chemical structure formed when two or more atoms are held together by chemical bonds. The atoms may be identical, as in oxygen gas ($\mathrm{O_2}$) or ozone ($\mathrm{O_3}$), or different, as in water ($\mathrm{H_2O}$) or glucose ($\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$). What defines a molecule is not size or complexity but the presence of bonding — a stable arrangement of shared or transferred electrons that links the atoms into a single unit with its own distinct chemical identity, separate from the properties of its constituent atoms.

The bonds holding a molecule together are typically covalent, meaning atoms share electron pairs to fill their outer electron shells. Consider methane, $\mathrm{CH_4}$: one carbon atom shares one electron pair with each of four hydrogen atoms, satisfying carbon's need for eight valence electrons and each hydrogen's need for two. The result is a tetrahedral molecule with specific bond angles (approximately $109.5°$) and predictable chemical behavior — methane burns, dissolves poorly in water, and exists as a gas at room temperature, none of which can be inferred from carbon or hydrogen atoms in isolation.

Recognizing molecular structure is essential for solving real chemistry problems. Suppose you are asked to determine the molecular formula of a compound known to be 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, and 53.3% oxygen by mass, with a molar mass of $180\ \mathrm{g/mol}$. Converting mass percentages to moles per 100 g sample gives roughly 3.33 mol C, 6.7 mol H, and 3.33 mol O, yielding a simplest ratio of $\mathrm{CH_2O}$ (empirical formula, mass $30\ \mathrm{g/mol}$). Dividing the given molar mass by this empirical mass ($180/30 = 6$) shows the actual molecule is six times larger: $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$ — glucose. This kind of calculation, moving from percent composition to empirical formula to true molecular formula, is a standard skill for identifying unknown compounds in the lab.

Distinguishing molecules from ions or bulk ionic compounds (like table salt, $\mathrm{NaCl}$, which forms an extended lattice rather than discrete units) also matters when predicting physical properties such as melting point, solubility, and electrical conductivity in solution.

### macromolecule (9.8s)

## Macromolecule

A macromolecule is a large molecule built from repeating structural units called monomers, linked together through covalent bonds in a process called polymerization. The resulting polymer can range from a few hundred to millions of daltons in molecular weight. Biology recognizes four major classes of macromolecules: carbohydrates (built from monosaccharide monomers), proteins (built from amino acids), nucleic acids (built from nucleotides), and lipids, which are technically not true polymers since they lack a repeating monomer-linkage pattern but are grouped with macromolecules due to their size and biological role.

The chemistry behind assembling most macromolecules is dehydration synthesis (also called a condensation reaction): each time two monomers join, a water molecule is released, and a new covalent bond — a glycosidic bond in carbohydrates, a peptide bond in proteins, a phosphodiester bond in nucleic acids — forms between them. The reverse process, hydrolysis, breaks these bonds by adding water back, which is how digestion disassembles dietary macromolecules into absorbable monomers.

Consider glucose polymerizing into starch. Each glucose unit joins the growing chain by losing an –OH from one carbon and an –H from the neighboring glucose's –OH group, releasing H₂O and forming an α-1,4-glycosidic bond. Repeat this thousands of times and you get amylose, a storage polysaccharide. The same monomer, glucose, linked with a different bond geometry (β-1,4) produces cellulose — a structural polysaccharide that most animals cannot digest because they lack the enzyme to cleave that particular bond. This illustrates a key problem-solving principle: a macromolecule's function is determined not just by which monomers compose it, but by the specific bond type and three-dimensional arrangement linking them.

This principle has direct diagnostic value. If you're given an unknown macromolecule and told it is broken down by amylase but not cellulase, you can infer it contains α-linkages, not β-linkages, even without seeing its full structure. Similarly, when analyzing a protein's function, you must look beyond "it's made of amino acids" to the specific sequence and resulting folded shape, since a single amino acid substitution (as in sickle-cell hemoglobin) can alter an entire macromolecule's behavior despite negligible change in composition.
