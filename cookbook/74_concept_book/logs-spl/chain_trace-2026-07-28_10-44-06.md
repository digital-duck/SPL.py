### mean (0.0s)

## Mean

**Definition.** Let $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ be a finite sequence of real numbers. The *arithmetic mean* (or *sample mean*) of this sequence is the value

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i.$$

The mean is the unique real number that minimises the sum of squared deviations: $\bar{x} = \arg\min_{c \in \mathbb{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - c)^2$. Geometrically, it is the balance point of the data — the centre of mass if each observation is assigned equal weight.

**Worked example.** A student records the following five quiz scores: $72, 85, 90, 68, 95$. The arithmetic mean is

$$\bar{x} = \frac{72 + 85 + 90 + 68 + 95}{5} = \frac{410}{5} = 82.$$

Now suppose one score is changed from $68$ to $168$ (a data-entry error). The new mean becomes $\frac{510}{5} = 102$, which exceeds all legitimate scores. This illustrates that the arithmetic mean is sensitive to outliers — a single extreme value can shift it substantially, motivating robust alternatives such as the median.

**Key theorem (Linearity of the sample mean).** For two sequences $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ and $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ and constants $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, let $z_i = a x_i + b y_i$. Then

$$\bar{z} = a\,\bar{x} + b\,\bar{y}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply the definition directly: $\bar{z} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (ax_i + by_i) = a \cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i + b \cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i$. $\square$

This linearity makes the mean uniquely tractable for combining scaled or shifted datasets without recomputing from raw observations.

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import Rational

scores = [72, 85, 90, 68, 95]
sample_mean = Rational(sum(scores), len(scores))
print("Sample mean:", sample_mean)          # 82

# Linearity check: scale each score by 1.1 and add a 5-point bonus
adjusted = [Rational(11, 10) * s + 5 for s in scores]
mean_adjusted = Rational(sum(adjusted), len(adjusted))
expected = Rational(11, 10) * sample_mean + 5
print("Mean of adjusted scores:", mean_adjusted)   # 95.2
print("Computed via linearity:  ", expected)        # 95.2
```

Running this cell confirms that scaling each score by $1.1$ and adding $5$ points raises the mean from $82$ to $\frac{476}{5} = 95.2$, in exact agreement with the linearity theorem applied to $\bar{x}$.

### random_variable (0.0s)

## Random Variable

**Definition.** A *random variable* is a measurable function $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, where $\Omega$ is the sample space, $\mathcal{F}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of events, and $P$ is a probability measure. For every Borel set $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, we require $X^{-1}(B) = \{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \in B\} \in \mathcal{F}$, ensuring that the probability $P(X \in B)$ is well-defined. Informally, a random variable assigns a real number to each outcome of an experiment, translating abstract events into numerical quantities amenable to analysis.

Random variables are classified by their range. A *discrete* random variable takes values in a countable set $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots\}$ and is characterized by its *probability mass function* (PMF) $p(x_k) = P(X = x_k)$, satisfying $\sum_k p(x_k) = 1$. A *continuous* random variable takes values in an interval and is described by a *probability density function* (PDF) $f$, where $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$.

**Worked Example.** Roll a fair six-sided die. Set $\Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ with $P(\{\omega\}) = 1/6$ for each outcome. Define $X(\omega) = \omega$. Then $X$ is a discrete random variable with PMF $p(k) = 1/6$ for $k \in \{1,\ldots,6\}$. Its *expected value* is
$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{k=1}^{6} k \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5,$$
and its *variance* is $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = \frac{91}{6} - \frac{441}{36} = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.92$.

**Key Theorem (Law of the Unconscious Statistician).** If $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is measurable and $X$ is a discrete random variable, then
$$\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_k g(x_k)\, p(x_k),$$
without needing to derive the distribution of $g(X)$ explicitly. The analogous statement for continuous $X$ replaces the sum with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) f(x)\,dx$.

**Lab Cell.**
```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational, summation, simplify

k = symbols('k', integer=True)
pmf = Rational(1, 6)

E_X  = summation(k * pmf, (k, 1, 6))
E_X2 = summation(k**2 * pmf, (k, 1, 6))
Var_X = simplify(E_X2 - E_X**2)

print(f"E[X]   = {E_X}")      # 7/2
print(f"Var(X) = {Var_X}")    # 35/12
```

### sampling_distribution (0.0s)

## Sampling Distribution

**Definition.** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables drawn from a population with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. A *sampling distribution* is the probability distribution of a statistic $T = g(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ computed across all possible samples of size $n$. The most important instance is the sampling distribution of the sample mean

$$\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i.$$

Because $\bar{X}$ is itself a random variable, it possesses an expected value and variance:

$$E[\bar{X}] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.$$

The quantity $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ is called the *standard error* of the mean; it measures how tightly sample means cluster around the true population mean.

**Worked Example.** Suppose examination scores in a large course are distributed with $\mu = 72$ and $\sigma = 10$. A professor repeatedly draws random samples of $n = 25$ students and records each sample mean. The standard error of these means is

$$\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{25}} = 2.$$

Thus the sample means vary with a spread of 2 points around 72, far narrower than the individual score spread of 10 points. Increasing the sample size to $n = 100$ would further reduce the standard error to 1, illustrating the fundamental principle that larger samples yield more precise estimates.

**Key Theorem (Central Limit Theorem).** For i.i.d. random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$, the standardized sample mean

$$Z_n = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

converges in distribution to the standard normal $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n \to \infty$. Formally,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(Z_n \leq z) = \Phi(z),$$

where $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal CDF. In practice, the normal approximation is considered reliable for $n \geq 30$ when the population distribution is not severely skewed.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy import symbols, sqrt, simplify

# Symbolic standard error
sigma, n = symbols('sigma n', positive=True)
standard_error = sigma / sqrt(n)
print("Standard error:", standard_error)
print("SE at sigma=10, n=25:", standard_error.subs({sigma: 10, n: 25}))

# Numerical simulation of sampling distribution
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
population = rng.normal(loc=72, scale=10, size=100_000)
sample_means = [population[rng.choice(len(population), 25, replace=False)].mean()
                for _ in range(5_000)]
print(f"Simulated mean of sample means: {np.mean(sample_means):.3f}")
print(f"Simulated std of sample means:  {np.std(sample_means):.3f}")
```

### standard_deviation (0.0s)

## Standard Deviation

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a random variable with expected value $\mu = E[X]$. The *standard deviation* of $X$, denoted $\sigma$ (or $\sigma_X$), is defined as

$$\sigma = \sqrt{E\!\left[(X - \mu)^2\right]}.$$

The quantity inside the square root, $\sigma^2 = E[(X-\mu)^2]$, is called the *variance*. For a finite dataset $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$, the population standard deviation is

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2}, \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i.$$

When the dataset is treated as a *sample* drawn from a larger population, the denominator is replaced by $n-1$ (Bessel's correction), yielding the sample standard deviation $s$.

**Worked Example.** Consider the dataset $\{2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9\}$, which has $n = 8$ values.

1. Compute the mean: $\mu = \dfrac{2+4+4+4+5+5+7+9}{8} = \dfrac{40}{8} = 5$.

2. Compute squared deviations:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{8}(x_i - 5)^2 = 9 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 4 + 16 = 32.$$

3. Population standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{32/8} = \sqrt{4} = 2$.

Intuitively, the data points lie, on average, two units away from the mean of 5.

**Key Theorem (Chebyshev's Inequality).** For *any* distribution with finite mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and for every $k > 1$,

$$P(|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma) \leq \frac{1}{k^2}.$$

This guarantees, for example, that at least $1 - 1/4 = 75\%$ of any distribution's probability mass lies within two standard deviations of the mean — no assumptions on the shape of the distribution are required. For the normal distribution the corresponding figure is approximately 95.4%, a much tighter bound arising from its specific analytic form.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

data = [2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9]
n = len(data)
mu = sp.Rational(sum(data), n)
variance = sum((sp.Rational(x) - mu)**2 for x in data) / n
sigma = sp.sqrt(variance)
print(f"mean={mu}, variance={variance}, sigma={sigma}")
# mean=5, variance=4, sigma=2
```

### normal_distribution (0.0s)

## Normal Distribution

**Definition.** A continuous random variable $X$ is said to follow a *normal distribution* with mean $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ and variance $\sigma^2 > 0$, written $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if its probability density function (PDF) is

$$f(x;\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad x \in \mathbb{R}.$$

The parameter $\mu$ controls the center of the symmetric bell-shaped curve, while $\sigma$ (the standard deviation) controls its spread. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t;\mu,\sigma^2)\,dt$; because the integral has no closed form in elementary functions, probabilities are read from precomputed tables or computed numerically.

**Worked Example.** Suppose the heights of adult males in a population are modeled as $X \sim \mathcal{N}(175, 49)$ (centimeters), so $\mu = 175$ and $\sigma = 7$. Find the probability that a randomly selected male is taller than $182\,\text{cm}$.

Because every normal CDF can be expressed in terms of the $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ table, set $z = (182 - 175)/7 = 1$ and look up $F(182) = F_{\mathcal{N}(0,1)}(1) \approx 0.8413$. Therefore

$$P(X > 182) = 1 - F(182) \approx 1 - 0.8413 = 0.1587.$$

Approximately $15.9\%$ of males exceed $182\,\text{cm}$.

**Key Theorem (68–95–99.7 Rule).** If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$, then

$$P(\mu - k\sigma \le X \le \mu + k\sigma) \approx \begin{cases} 0.6827 & k=1,\\ 0.9545 & k=2,\\ 0.9973 & k=3. \end{cases}$$

This follows from evaluating $2F(\mu+k\sigma)-1$ at $k=1,2,3$, and it provides a rapid sanity-check for whether observed data are plausibly normal.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, exp, pi, sqrt, integrate, oo, simplify

x = symbols('x', real=True)
sigma_val = 7
mu_val = 175

f = (1 / (sigma_val * sqrt(2 * pi))) * exp(-(x - mu_val)**2 / (2 * sigma_val**2))

# P(X > 182)
prob = integrate(f, (x, 182, oo))
print("P(X > 182) =", simplify(prob))  # expects (1 - erf(1/sqrt(2))) / 2
```

### standard_error_of_mean (0.0s)

## Standard Error Of Mean

**Definition.** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables drawn from a population with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. The sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i$ is itself a random variable. By linearity of expectation and independence, $\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_n] = \mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2/n$. The **standard error of the mean** (SEM) is the standard deviation of this sampling distribution:

$$\operatorname{SEM} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

When $\sigma$ is unknown, it is replaced by the sample standard deviation $s$, giving the estimated SEM $\hat{\sigma}_{\bar{X}} = s/\sqrt{n}$. The SEM quantifies how precisely the sample mean estimates the population mean; it is the single new quantity this section introduces.

**Worked Example.** A quality-control engineer measures the fill volume (in mL) of 25 bottles sampled from a production line. The sample yields $\bar{x} = 500.4$ mL and $s = 2.0$ mL. The estimated SEM is

$$\hat{\sigma}_{\bar{X}} = \frac{2.0}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{2.0}{5} = 0.40 \text{ mL}.$$

A 95% confidence interval for the true mean fill volume is approximately $\bar{x} \pm 1.96\,\hat{\sigma}_{\bar{X}} = 500.4 \pm 0.78$ mL, so the engineer is 95% confident the population mean lies between $499.62$ mL and $501.18$ mL. Critically, the SEM is four times smaller than $s$: precision improves with the *square root* of sample size, not linearly.

**Key Theorem.** The Central Limit Theorem (assumed known) states that for sufficiently large $n$,

$$\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1).$$

The denominator in this standardization is precisely the SEM. This connection explains why the SEM appears in every $z$- or $t$-based confidence interval for $\mu$: it is the natural scale of fluctuation of $\bar{X}_n$ around $\mu$, regardless of the underlying population distribution.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, sqrt

sigma, n = symbols('sigma n', positive=True)
SEM = sigma / sqrt(n)
print("SEM =", SEM)

# Numerical example
s_val, n_val = 2.0, 25
sem_est = s_val / n_val**0.5
print(f"Estimated SEM = {sem_est:.4f} mL")
print(f"95% CI half-width ≈ {1.96 * sem_est:.4f} mL")
```

### central_limit_theorem_means (0.0s)

## Central Limit Theorem Means

**Definition.** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables drawn from any population with finite mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2 > 0$. Define the sample mean

$$\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i.$$

The *Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for means* states that as $n \to \infty$, the standardized sample mean

$$Z_n = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

converges in distribution to the standard normal:

$$Z_n \xrightarrow{d} Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1).$$

Equivalently, $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\, \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$.

**Proof sketch.** Center and scale each observation by setting $Y_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$, so each $Y_i$ has mean $0$ and variance $1$. Then $Z_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i$. Because the $Y_i$ are independent, variances add: $\mathrm{Var}(Z_n) = 1$ for every $n$. A Taylor expansion of the characteristic function $\varphi_{Y}(t) = E[e^{itY}]$ about $t = 0$ yields $\varphi_{Y}(t/\sqrt{n}) = 1 - t^2/(2n) + o(1/n)$, so $\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = [1 - t^2/(2n) + o(1/n)]^n \to e^{-t^2/2}$ as $n \to \infty$. This is precisely the characteristic function of $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and pointwise convergence of characteristic functions implies convergence in distribution. $\square$

**Worked example.** Suppose the time a student spends on a single homework problem follows an exponential distribution with rate $\lambda = 2$ (minutes$^{-1}$), so $\mu = 0.5$ min and $\sigma^2 = 0.25$ min$^2$. For a sample of $n = 50$ problems, the CLT guarantees

$$\bar{X}_{50} \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(0.5,\;\frac{0.25}{50}\right) = \mathcal{N}(0.5,\;0.005).$$

The probability that the mean time exceeds $0.6$ minutes is

$$P(\bar{X}_{50} > 0.6) \approx P\!\left(Z > \frac{0.6-0.5}{\sqrt{0.005}}\right) = P(Z > 1.414) \approx 0.079.$$

**Key theorem (Berry–Esseen bound).** The CLT gives no rate on its own. If the third absolute moment $E[|X_i|^3]$ is finite, then

$$\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}}\left|F_{Z_n}(x) - \Phi(x)\right| \leq \frac{C\,E[|X_i - \mu|^3]}{\sigma^3\sqrt{n}},$$

where $C \leq 0.4748$ and $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. The error shrinks at rate $O(n^{-1/2})$, confirming that larger samples make the normal approximation more reliable.

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, sqrt, exp, pi, integrate, oo, Rational, N

t = symbols('t')
mu, sigma_sq, n = Rational(1, 2), Rational(1, 4), 50
se = sqrt(sigma_sq / n)                     # standard error
z_val = (Rational(3, 5) - mu) / se         # z-score for 0.6
phi = exp(-t**2 / 2) / sqrt(2 * pi)
prob = integrate(phi, (t, z_val, oo))
print("P(X̄ > 0.6) ≈", N(prob, 4))
```

## Cookie Recipes Simulation

### Definition

A **cookie recipes simulation** models a constrained resource-allocation problem in which a finite set of ingredients must be distributed across multiple recipe variants to maximize a scalar objective — typically yield (number of cookies) or profit. Formally, let $\mathbf{r} = (r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_m)^{\top}$ denote $m$ recipe types, and let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}_{\geq 0}$ be the ingredient matrix, where $A_{ij}$ is the quantity of ingredient $i$ required per unit of recipe $j$. Given an ingredient supply vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}$ and a revenue vector $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}$, the simulation seeks a batch vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}$ satisfying

$$A\mathbf{x} \leq \mathbf{b}, \quad \mathbf{x} \geq \mathbf{0},$$

while maximising $\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$. This is a standard linear programme, but embedding it inside a Monte Carlo loop over uncertain supply or demand transforms it into a **stochastic simulation**.

### Worked Example

Suppose a bakery stocks $b_1 = 500\,\text{g}$ of flour and $b_2 = 200\,\text{g}$ of sugar. Two recipes are available: a sugar cookie ($r_1$) requires $(100\,\text{g flour},\,80\,\text{g sugar})$ per batch, and a shortbread ($r_2$) requires $(200\,\text{g flour},\,40\,\text{g sugar})$ per batch. Revenue per batch is $c_1 = 4$ and $c_2 = 5$. The feasible region is determined by

$$100x_1 + 200x_2 \leq 500, \quad 80x_1 + 40x_2 \leq 200, \quad x_1, x_2 \geq 0.$$

Solving at the vertices, $(x_1, x_2) = (2, 0.5)$ yields revenue $\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = 4(2) + 5(0.5) = 10.5$, which is the optimum. If flour supply is drawn from $\mathcal{U}[400, 600]$, running $N = 10{,}000$ replications and averaging optimal revenues yields the **expected maximum revenue** $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{*}]$.

### Key Theorem

**Theorem (Linearity of expectation under LP feasibility).** Let $\mathbf{b}(\omega)$ be a random supply vector with finite mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{b}]$. If the constraint matrix $A$ is fixed and the LP is feasible almost surely, then for large $N$,

$$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{*}(\mathbf{b}^{(k)}) \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{*}(\mathbf{b})],$$

by the Strong Law of Large Numbers, provided the value function $V(\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{*}(\mathbf{b})$ is integrable. Because $V$ is piecewise linear and convex in $\mathbf{b}$, Jensen's inequality further guarantees $\mathbb{E}[V(\mathbf{b})] \geq V(\boldsymbol{\mu})$: stochastic supply is worth at least as much as its mean in expectation.

```python
    80*x1  + 40*x2  - 200
], x1, x2)

```
