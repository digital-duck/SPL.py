### 木 (8.0s)

## 木

Look outside at a tree. It has one thick trunk standing up, and branches spreading out to the sides. Now imagine drawing that tree as fast as you can, using only a few strokes. You'd probably draw a line straight down for the trunk, and two lines crossing it for the branches. That's exactly what someone did thousands of years ago — and it became the character 木.

Here's the fun part: this little tree-drawing doesn't just mean "tree." It can lend its *meaning* to other characters, like two trees standing together making 林 (a forest), or a person leaning against 木 to rest, making 休. But sometimes 木 shows up just to lend its *sound*, like in 沐 (meaning "to bathe"), where it has nothing to do with trees at all!

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = 林 \text{ (forest)}$$

**Now you try:** Can you think of a picture-symbol that could mean two different things depending on where you use it — like a wheel that sometimes means "circle" and sometimes just sounds like "round"?

### 女 (8.1s)

## 女

Imagine you had to draw a picture of "a person" using only three quick strokes — no time for fingers, hair, or clothes. What's the one shape that instantly says "person"? Ancient scribes chose a kneeling figure, arms curved gently forward. That simple, folded shape became 女, the character for woman.

Look at the strokes again: they curve like someone sitting back on their heels, hands resting in front — a pose of stillness, often linked in old pictures to daily life at home. You don't need to know anyone's name to recognize the shape as "a person, here."

What makes 女 extra special is that it doesn't just stand alone — it becomes a building block. Many characters about mothers, sisters, marriage, and family borrow this same kneeling-figure shape as their anchor, the way a root word can hide inside many bigger words.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{好 (good)}
$$

Now you try: draw the kneeling shape yourself, then look for it hiding inside 妈 (mother) or 姐 (older sister). Can you spot it?

### 人 (6.0s)

## 人

Stand up and look at your shadow. Two legs, a little lean forward — that's a person walking! Long, long ago, people who invented Chinese writing looked at a walking figure from the side and drew exactly that: two legs, caught mid-step, leaning like they're about to go somewhere.

That simple sketch became 人, the character for "person." But 人 didn't stop being just one word — it became a building block, showing up inside hundreds of other characters whenever the meaning has something to do with people: a person resting against a tree, two people standing together, a person following another. Whenever you spot 人 tucked inside a bigger character, you can guess: "this is probably about a human being."

$$
\underbrace{丿}_{leaning\ leg} + \underbrace{㇏}_{trailing\ leg} = \underbrace{人}_{person}
$$

Now you try: draw 人 with just two strokes, like two legs mid-stride. Then look around your room — can you find three other characters that might be hiding a tiny walking person inside them?

### 子 (6.6s)

## 子

Imagine a tiny baby wrapped snugly in a blanket, only its round little head peeking out. That's exactly what the character 子 was drawn to show — a swaddled infant, arms tucked in, safe and small. Long ago, someone looked at a baby like that and turned its shape into a picture, and the picture became this character.

Why does it feel right? Because the character still looks like what it means — a small bundle with a head on top and little wrapped legs below. You can almost see the baby if you squint.

But 子 grew beyond just "baby." Over time, people started tacking it onto the end of other words, like a friendly little tag that says "this is a thing, a noun!" It's a bit like adding "-let" in English, as in "book" becoming "booklet."

**Now you try:** Look at 桌子 (table) and 椅子 (chair). Can you spot 子 hiding at the end of both?

### 宀 (7.0s)

## 宀

Imagine a tiny drawing of a roof — just two slanted strokes meeting at a peak, with a little wall on each side like the eaves of a house. That's it: no walls, no door, no floor. Just the roof. But a roof is a powerful thing. It's the part of a house that says "this is a safe place — you can stop wandering now."

In Chinese characters, 宀 rarely stands alone. It shows up on *top* of other characters, like a hat, telling you: whatever is under this roof is protected, gathered in, or settled down. A pig under a roof becomes "home." A woman under a roof becomes "peace." The roof itself doesn't do much — but it changes the meaning of everything beneath it, the way putting a lid on a pot turns loose ingredients into a meal.

$$
\underbrace{\text{宀}}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{\text{X}}_{\text{something}} = \text{something sheltered}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw a roof shape with two slanted lines. Then imagine putting your favorite toy under it — does it feel safer already?

### 马 (11.2s)

## 马

Imagine a word-builder who keeps a box of picture cards. One card shows a horse — four legs, a mane, a galloping shape. That card is 马. But this word-builder has a clever trick: sometimes he doesn't use the horse card for its *picture* at all — he uses it just for its *sound*, the way you might borrow the word "bee" to write "be-lieve," not because believing has anything to do with bees.

That's exactly what happens with 马. On its own, it means horse. But tucked inside other characters, it often forgets all about horses and just lends its sound, "ma."

$$
\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{马}_{sound: ma} = 妈 \text{ (mom)}
$$

"Mom" has nothing to do with horses — 马 just came along for its sound, like a costume the character wears, while 女 (woman) points to who mom is.

**Now you try:** Next time you see 马 inside a character, ask yourself — is it galloping in for its *meaning*, or just lending its *voice*?

### 从 (6.4s)

## 从

Imagine you're walking down the street, and your little brother is right behind you, matching your steps. That's the whole story of this character — someone following someone.

Look at the shape: two of the same little person-mark, one behind the other, both walking the same direction. There's no trick to it — the picture just *is* the meaning. A person, then another person, trailing along. That's what "follow" looks like when you draw it.

Here's how the character is built:

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{a person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{a person, trailing}} = 从 \;(\text{"follow"})
$$

One person leads, one follows — stack them, and you get the verb itself. No extra symbols needed, because the picture already tells the story.

**Now you try:** Next time you walk somewhere with a friend or a pet trailing behind you, picture 从 in your head — two people, one behind the other. Can you draw your own version of "follow" using two simple stick figures, one right behind the other?

### 口 (5.5s)

## 口

Draw a little square. Not a fancy one — four straight lines meeting at corners, leaving a small open space in the middle. That open space is the whole idea. What do you have on your face that is basically an opening? Your mouth! And that's exactly what this character shows: a mouth, drawn simply as the shape of an opening.

Think about everything your mouth does. It eats an apple. It says "hello." It asks "why?" That's why 口 shows up not just for the body part, but for anything that involves an opening or an act of speaking — like a doorway, a gate, or even a question mark's job of turning a statement into a question.

Notice the shape does the explaining for you — no need to memorize a random squiggle, just remember: a mouth is an opening, so it's drawn as one.

**Now you try:** Draw 口 in the air with your finger. Then open your own mouth and say a word out loud — you just did what the character shows!

### 日 (6.9s)

## 日

Long ago, someone looked up at the bright circle in the sky and wanted to draw it. A circle is easy to imagine but hard to carve or brush quickly, so early writers squared it off — round became boxy, and a small dot or line was added inside to show it wasn't just any circle, but a special, glowing one.

Think of a stop sign: it isn't a perfect circle either, but you still recognize what it means. That's what happened to the sun here. The character kept the *feeling* of roundness — a shape with no sharp starting point, something whole — while becoming easy to write with a brush in four quick strokes.

This character stands for the sun itself, and also for "day," since a day is simply one trip of the sun across the sky.

$$\underbrace{\bigcirc}_{sun's\ shape} + \underbrace{-}_{a\ mark\ of\ light} = 日$$

**Now you try:** Draw a circle, then flatten its sides into a box. Add one line inside. Does it still feel like the sun to you?

### 月 (6.4s)

## 月

Look up at the night sky when the moon is thin — just a curved sliver of light, like a bent boat floating in the dark. Long ago, someone looked at that same curved shape and drew it on a piece of bone or bamboo: two curved strokes forming a little box, with two short lines inside like the shadows we imagine on its surface. That drawing became 月.

But here's a surprising twist: the very same shape, 月, also hides inside many characters about the human body — like 肝 (liver), 胃 (stomach), and 脸 (face). Why would "moon" show up in body parts? It doesn't! Thousands of years ago, "flesh" was drawn as a slightly different shape. Over time, as writing simplified, the "flesh" shape merged into looking exactly like "moon."

So 月 is really two ancestors wearing one disguise: sometimes it means the moon in the sky, and sometimes it whispers "this is about the body."

**Now you try:** Next time you see 月 in a character, ask — is this word about the sky, or about you?

### 林 (5.8s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing alone in a field. Nice, but quiet. Now imagine a second tree right next to it. Suddenly it feels different — cozier, shadier, a little wild. That's the idea behind 林 (lín), the word for "woods."

Chinese characters love a simple trick: draw something twice, and you get *more of it*. One tree (木) is just a tree. Two trees side by side aren't "two trees" anymore — they become a whole new idea: a small woods, a grove, a cluster of green.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{林}_{\text{woods}}
$$

It's like stacking LEGO bricks — one brick is just a brick, but two together start forming a shape you can actually build a fort out of. Repetition isn't just "more" — it's a new meaning altogether.

**Now you try:** Draw one tree. Then draw it again right beside it. Does it start to feel like a little forest already?

### 水 (5.5s)

## 水

Imagine you're standing by a stream, watching the water wiggle and bend around every rock in its path. It never goes in a straight line — it curves, splits, and flows around whatever it meets. That wiggling, splitting motion is exactly what the character 水 draws!

Look closely: there's a tall stroke down the middle, like the main current of the stream, and little strokes branching off to the sides, like tiny splashes or droplets breaking away. The whole shape feels like water caught mid-motion — never still, always moving.

Water shows up everywhere in writing, and when it does, it often shrinks down into a simpler form: three small drops on the left side of a character, written as 氵. Think of it as water's "nickname" — smaller, but still recognizably watery.

**Now you try**: Trace 水 slowly with your finger, imagining the stream flowing downward and outward. Then look for 氵 in other characters — can you guess which ones might mean something wet, like rain, rivers, or oceans?

### 休 (8.0s)

## 休

Imagine you have been running and playing all day. Your legs feel heavy, your eyes feel sleepy. What do you do? You find a tree, and you lean your back against it, and you just... stop.

That is exactly what this character shows you. Look closely: on the left is a little person 人, standing up straight. On the right is a tree 木, with branches reaching up. When you put them side by side, the person leans against the tree to rest.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{休}_{\text{rest (xiū)}}
$$

Two simple pictures snap together, and suddenly you can *see* the meaning before anyone tells you the word. That's the magic — the character isn't just a symbol you memorize, it's a tiny drawing of a person taking a break under a tree.

**Now you try:** Cover the character with your hand. Can you rebuild it from just 人 and 木? Then look around — is there a tree nearby you could lean on for a real rest?

### 众 (5.1s)

## 众

Imagine one person standing alone on a hill. Now imagine two more people walk up and stand beside them. Suddenly it doesn't feel like "a person" anymore — it feels like a *crowd*, a *bunch of people together*. That's exactly the idea behind 众 (zhòng).

You already met this trick with 森, where three 木 trees piled up became a whole forest. 众 does the same thing, but with people instead of trees. One 人 is just one person walking. Two 人 side by side could still just be two friends. But three 人 stacked together? That's not "some people" anymore — that's *everyone*, *the crowd*, *the masses*.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = \underbrace{众}_{crowd}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw one stick-figure person, then two more standing close together underneath. Does it start to feel like a group instead of individuals? That feeling — that's exactly what 众 means.

### 吗 (12.9s)

## 吗

Imagine asking your friend, "You like ice cream." Now imagine asking, "You like ice cream?" In English your voice goes up at the end, and maybe you add a little word. In Mandarin, there's a tiny word that does this job every time: 吗 (ma). Just drop it at the end of any sentence, and — like magic — a statement becomes a question!

Notice something fun: 吗 is built from two pieces you may already know. On the left, 口 (mouth) reminds us this word is about *speaking* — questions come out of a mouth! On the right, 马 (horse) is just there to sound like "ma."

$$\underbrace{口}_{\text{mouth, spoken sound}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sounds like "ma"}} = 吗 \text{ (question word)}$$

This is a handy trick to remember: whenever you see 马 tucked inside a character, it's often just borrowed for its sound, not its meaning.

**Now you try:** Take the statement "你是学生" (You are a student) and add 吗 at the end. What question did you just make?

### 好 (6.2s)

## 好

Imagine a mother holding her child close. Long, long ago, someone looked at this scene and thought: this is what goodness looks like. So they drew a picture — a woman on one side, a child on the other — and put them together to make one word: 好 (hǎo), meaning "good."

Why does this feel right? Think about the safest, warmest moment you can picture — someone who cares for you, right beside you. That feeling of things being okay, of being cared for, is exactly what the ancient picture-makers were trying to capture. A family together, safe and whole — that felt like the very idea of "good."

The character is built from two simpler pictures placed side by side:

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 好 \ (\text{good})$$

Now you try: Next time you see 好, picture a mother and child standing together. Can you think of another pair of things that, side by side, would also feel like "good" to you?

### 妈 (5.0s)

## 妈

Imagine you're building a word like a little machine, out of two parts that each bring their own job. One part, 女, means "a female person" — it's the same piece you find in words for girl, woman, sister. The other part, 马, doesn't mean "horse" here at all — it's just lending its *sound*, "ma," the way a nickname might borrow a sound without the meaning. Put them together, and you get 妈 (mā): mother. The female part tells you *what kind* of word this is, and the sound part tells you *how to say it*.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{female person}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sounds like "ma"}} = 妈 \ (\text{mother})$$

This is a common trick in Chinese: one piece for meaning, one piece for sound, glued into a single character.

Now you try: can you think of another word where one part tells you the meaning and another part just lends its sound?

### 字 (7.3s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny child, tucked safely under a roof, warm and cared for. That's the picture hiding inside this very character — because 字 *is* that picture.

Long ago, people needed a word for "character" itself — for the little symbols they were writing. So they built one the same way they built everything else: by combining pieces that already meant something. They took 宀, a roof, and 子, a child, and put the child under the roof. A character, they were saying, is something raised and cared for, the way a family raises a child — grown from older pieces into something new.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \underbrace{字}_{\text{character}}$$

That's why the whole study of how characters connect and grow from one another is called **ZiNets** — "zì networks" — named after this very character. The writing system used its own word for "character" to describe itself!

**Now you try:** Can you think of something else that gets "raised" over time, growing from small pieces into something bigger — like a word learning new meanings, or a skill built from smaller habits?

### 安 (6.0s)

## 安

Picture a little girl coming inside just as the sky turns dark and windy. Her mother pulls her under the roof, out of the wind, and she curls up safe and warm. That feeling — the storm outside, the calm inside — is exactly what this character captures.

Look at the shape: a roof sits over a woman. Not because only women belonged at home, but because in the old picture-story of this character, a woman settled quietly inside a house was the clearest image of calm the writer could draw — nothing rushing, nothing worried, just stillness under shelter.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = 安 \text{ (peace, settled)}$$

That's why 安 shows up in 平安 (safe and sound) and 安全 (safety) — anywhere something is protected and at rest.

**Now you try**: Draw a roof, then put someone you love resting underneath it. That drawing *is* 安.

### 明 (6.3s)

## 明

Imagine the sky can only hold one light at a time. During the day, the sun rules alone. At night, the moon takes over. But what if, just for a moment, you could have both? That's the idea packed into this character.

Picture the sun blazing outside your window, and picture the full moon glowing over a quiet lake. Now put them side by side, shining together. The room wouldn't just be lit — it would be *dazzling*. That doubled brightness, that feeling of everything lit up at once, is exactly what this character captures.

It's built from two pieces standing next to each other, each one already a giver of light:

$$
\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} = 明 \; (\text{bright})
$$

Sun plus moon isn't just "more light" — it's the whole idea of brightness itself, as if someone asked "what are ALL the ways something can shine?" and the answer was simply: put the two great lights together.

**Now you try:** Next time you see both the sun and moon in the sky at once — it happens! — say "明" and notice how bright the moment feels.

### 森 (6.8s)

## 森

Imagine one tree standing alone — just one. Now imagine two trees together — that's already a little group, a "grove" (林). But what happens when you keep adding trees, more and more, until they crowd together, tall and thick and a little mysterious? That's a forest — 森 (sēn).

Here's the fun part: the character doesn't just mean forest, it *shows* it. One tree (木) is a tree. Two trees stacked make 林, a grove. Three trees stacked make 森, a forest. The more trees piled into the character, the bigger and denser the place feels — almost like the character is growing right in front of you.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \text{ (forest)}
$$

$$
木 \xrightarrow{+木} 林 \xrightarrow{+木} 森
$$

Notice something: as the tree count climbs, so does the size of the idea — one, a pair, a crowd.

**Now you try:** Can you think of another word where "more of the same thing" makes something bigger or wilder — like raindrops becoming a storm?

### 沐 (7.2s)

## 沐

Imagine you're washing your hands, and someone asks, "What sound does washing make?" You'd probably say "swish" or "splash" — not "tree"! But in the character 沐, something surprising happens: it borrows its sound from a tree.

Here's the trick: 沐 means "to wash" (like washing your hair or body), and it's built from two familiar friends — 水 (water) and 木 (tree). Water makes total sense for washing — water is *what you wash with*. But 木 isn't sitting there because washing has anything to do with trees. It's there because "tree" is pronounced "mù" — and so is 沐! The tree lent its sound, not its meaning.

$$
\underbrace{水}_{\text{washing (meaning)}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{"mù" (sound)}} = 沐 \ (\text{"mù," to wash})
$$

This is a whole new trick! Before, pictures gave meaning. Now, one piece can just whisper its pronunciation.

**Now you try:** Cover 沐 and rebuild it — water for meaning, tree for sound. Can you think of another character where one part might just be "borrowing a voice"?

### phonetic_borrowing (7.8s)

## Phonetic Borrowing

Imagine you already own a toy brick shaped like a tree — you call it "mù." One day you need a brick that sounds like "mù" but means "to wash your hair." Instead of inventing a brand-new shape, you grab your tree brick and just glue a water symbol next to it. The tree isn't doing tree-work anymore — it's only lending its sound!

That's the trick: a familiar piece gets hired for a second job, not for its meaning, but for its pronunciation. The character 木 (tree, sounds like "mù") shows up inside 沐 (to bathe), still sounding like "mù," even though bathing has nothing to do with trees.

The same brick, 马 (horse, "mǎ"), gets hired twice more — in 妈 (mom) and 吗 (a question word) — always bringing its "mǎ" sound along.

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{sound: mù}} + \underbrace{water}_{\text{meaning}} = 沐$$
$$\underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: mǎ}} + \underbrace{female}_{\text{meaning}} = 妈$$
$$\underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: mǎ}} + \underbrace{mouth}_{\text{meaning}} = 吗$$

**Now you try:** Find another character containing 马. Does it still sound like "mǎ"?

### semantic_composition (10.0s)

## Semantic Composition

Imagine building a sentence out of tiny pictures instead of words. A person leaning against a tree means "rest." A mother next to her child means "good." This is 会意, the meaning-stacking principle: instead of drawing one complicated picture, you combine two or more simple pictures, and their meanings blend into something new.

Why does this feel right? Because it's how we think in metaphors already — "tree-person" really does feel restful, like someone finally sitting down after a long walk.

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \underbrace{休}_{rest}$$

Even the word for "character" itself, 字, is built this way — a child (子) sheltered under a roof (宀), like a new idea growing up inside a home.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = \underbrace{字}_{character}$$

**Now you try:** Can you guess what "many trees together" (森) or "many people together" (众) might mean?

### phono_semantic_principle (6.8s)

## Phono Semantic Principle

Imagine you had to invent a brand-new word for every single thing in the world — a totally unique squiggle for "river," another for "lake," another for "ocean." Your brain would explode! Ancient Chinese scholars found a shortcut, almost like a LEGO trick.

They noticed: why not build a character from two smaller pieces? One piece hints at the *meaning* — like a clue about the category. The other piece hints at the *sound* — like a clue about how to say it out loud. Put them together, and you get a brand-new character that almost decodes itself!

$$\underbrace{水 (\text{water})}_{\text{meaning clue}} + \underbrace{青 (qīng)}_{\text{sound clue}} = 清 (qīng, \text{"clear"})$$

The water radical tells you it's about liquid; the sound piece tells you how to pronounce it. This single trick built over 80% of all Chinese characters!

**Now you try:** Take the water radical (水) and pair it with a sound piece you already know. Can you guess what category the new character might belong to — even before knowing its exact meaning?

### zinets_portal (7.1s)

## Zinets Portal

Imagine a giant spiderweb made of Chinese characters, glowing on your screen. Each thread connects a character to the smaller pieces that built it. You tap one glowing dot — say, 明 (bright) — and suddenly a mini-lesson appears, made just for you, tracing back through its ancestors: 日 (sun) and 月 (moon).

Why does this feel right? Because nobody explains a character the same way twice — a total beginner needs the whole family tree, while someone who already knows "sun" and "moon" just needs the last step. The portal notices what you already know and skips it, so your lesson is never too easy or too hard.

Under the hood, tapping a character asks: "show me everything above this dot, minus what this learner has already met." The network remembers the family tree; the gap-check remembers you.

$$
\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} = \text{明 (bright)}
$$

**Now you try**: Tap 明 in the portal. If you already learned 日 and 月, does your lesson start there — or does it jump straight to how they combine?
