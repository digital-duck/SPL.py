### term (12.1s)

## Term

Think of a big box of LEGO labeled "numbers." Every single brick in that box — 3, 17, 100 — is a *term*: an actual, particular thing living inside the box. The box itself, "numbers," is what we call its *type*. A brick is a term of that type.

Here's the twist: it's not just numbers that live in boxes like this. A function, like "double a number," is a term too — it lives in a box called "numbers $\to$ numbers." And soon we'll meet an even stranger kind of brick: a *proof* is also a term, living inside a box whose label is the statement it proves!

Formally: if $t$ is a term of type $T$, we write $t : T$. So $3 : \mathbb{N}$, and a doubling function $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$.

When you write `def two := 2`, you're not creating something new — you're just giving an existing brick, `2`, a nametag: `two`. Now `two : \mathbb{N}` too.

**Now you try:** Name three terms of type "animals," then name one term of type "animals $\to$ sounds."

### type (10.3s)

## Type

Imagine a giant sorting machine at a toy factory. Every toy that rolls by gets stamped with a label: "Car," "Doll," "Block." Workers never have to guess what a toy is — the label tells them instantly, so they know exactly what to do with it.

Lean works the same way. Every single object — a number, a shape, a proof, even a true-or-false statement — carries a label called its **type**. The number $3$ has type $\mathbb{N}$ (natural number). The idea "the sky is blue" has type $\text{Prop}$ (proposition). You can ask Lean for any object's label using the command `#check`.

We write $x : T$ to mean "$x$ has type $T$." So $3 : \mathbb{N}$ and $(2 + 2 = 4) : \text{Prop}$. Types aren't decoration — Lean *refuses* to combine things whose types don't match, the same way you'd never mix a car's engine into a doll's tea party. That's how Lean catches nonsense before it happens.

**Now you try:** Pick three things — a number, a word, and a true/false sentence. Guess each one's type, then check with `#check` to see if you were right!

### proposition (18.2s)

## Proposition

Imagine you tell a friend: "I bet this bridge can hold a thousand cars." Your friend says, "Prove it." You can't just *say* it's true — you need something they can check: an engineer's calculation, a stress test, a signed certificate. That certificate isn't the bridge. It's a *reason to believe* the bridge works.

In this way of thinking, a mathematical claim — a proposition — is treated like a container. Not a container for numbers, but a container for reasons. The claim "$2+2=4$" is a box, and a valid proof is the thing sitting inside that box, filling it up.

Formally: a proposition $P$ comes paired with its proof $p$, written $p : P$ — read as "$p$ is a proof of $P$." A proposition with no proof is an empty box; nobody can open it and pull anything out. A proposition with a proof is a box holding real evidence.

**Now you try:** Pick a claim you believe, like "my plant needs water daily." What would count as evidence living *inside* that claim's box?

### function_types (7.4s)

## Function Types

Imagine a vending machine. You put in a coin (that's the *type* of thing it accepts), and out comes a snack (the *type* it gives back). You never see inside the machine — you just know: coin in, snack out. That's a function.

In our language, we write this machine's label as $A \to B$: "give me an $A$, I'll give you a $B$." The actual machine — the recipe for turning coins into snacks — is written as $\mathtt{fun}\ x \Rightarrow \ldots$, where $x$ is the coin you feed in, and the $\ldots$ describes what happens next.

Every function has two parts, like a toy with its box: the **type** ($A \to B$, the label on the box) and the **term** (the toy itself, $\mathtt{fun}\ x \Rightarrow \ldots$). Nothing happens on its own — you must *apply* the function to an input to get a result. Applying is the only way anything gets built or computed.

**Now you try:** Picture a machine labeled $\text{Number} \to \text{Number}$ that doubles whatever you give it. What comes out if you feed in 5? What if you feed in 100?

### curry_howard (16.2s)

## Curry Howard

Imagine a locksmith who notices something strange: every key she has ever made is secretly the same shape as the lock it opens. Not similar — identical, if you squint the right way. A key and a lock, two things that seemed totally different, turn out to be one idea wearing two costumes.

That is the aha of Curry-Howard. Here is the one new idea: a claim (like "if it rains, the ground is wet") and a description of a shape (like "something that turns Rain into WetGround") can be the very same thing, just described two ways. Once you accept that, everything else follows for free: proving the claim is exactly building the shape, and a computer checking your shape is valid is checking your proof is valid. Same lock, same key.

Minimal formalization: proposition $P$ corresponds to type $P$; a proof of $P$ corresponds to a term $p : P$.

Now you try: think of a claim you believe is true. Could you describe it instead as a shape — "a recipe that turns one thing into another"?

### proof (12.0s)

## Proof

Imagine a courtroom. You can't just say "the defendant is guilty" — you have to build an argument, step by step, where each step follows undeniably from the last, until you reach the verdict. A mathematical proof is exactly this: a chain of small, honest steps connecting what you already know to what you want to show.

Here's the surprising twist: in this book's world, a proof isn't just an argument you write in words — it's a *thing*, an object you can hold, like a number or a shape. A proof $p$ of a proposition $P$ is written $p : P$, the same way you'd write $3 : \mathbb{N}$ for a number. Checking whether an argument is valid becomes checking whether $p$ really carries that label — a mechanical check, not a judgment call. No separate "truth department" is needed.

**Now you try**: think of a proof as a *recipe* that, when followed exactly, always produces the claimed result — never a maybe.

### theorem_statements (6.3s)

## Theorem Statements

Imagine a locksmith and a promise-keeper meet. The locksmith says, "I promise: give me any key of type A, and I will hand you a door of type B." That promise — "if you give me A, I give you B" — is a theorem. But a promise without action is just words. The proof is the locksmith actually building the mechanism that takes the key and opens the door.

This is the beautiful secret at the heart of Curry-Howard: a theorem's *statement* is really a question about a shape, and the *proof* is a working machine of exactly that shape. We write it as:

$$\text{theorem\_name} : \text{statement} := \text{proof}$$

The statement is like a blueprint (a function's type signature — "input A, output B"). The proof is the actual working gadget matching that blueprint. If the gadget runs without breaking, the promise is trustworthy.

**Now you try:** Write your own promise: "theorem sun\_rises : night → day := ???" What proof (machine) would you build to fill in that `???`?

### tactic_mode (6.2s)

## Tactic Mode

Imagine assembling furniture, but instead of following a fixed instruction sheet, you look at what's in front of you *right now* and pick the next tool — a screwdriver here, a wrench there — until nothing's left unbuilt. That's tactic mode.

A theorem prover keeps track of a **goal**: the thing you still need to show. Each **tactic** is a small move — "split this into two cases," "apply this known fact," "simplify the algebra" — that transforms the goal into a simpler one, or several smaller ones. You keep firing tactics until the goal reads "done," meaning nothing is left to prove.

Here's the elegant part: every tactic you typed gets recorded, in order. That list *is* the proof — a transcript of your reasoning, replayable and checkable by the machine step by step.

$$\text{Goal}_0 \xrightarrow{\text{tactic}_1} \text{Goal}_1 \xrightarrow{\text{tactic}_2} \cdots \xrightarrow{\text{tactic}_n} \square$$

where $\square$ marks no goals remaining.

**Now you try:** if your goal is "prove $2+2=4$," what's the very first tactic — the first honest move — you'd reach for?

### core_tactics (7.8s)

## Core Tactics

Imagine you're solving a maze, but instead of walking through it, you're proving a path exists. You don't need to actually walk it — you just need a rock-solid argument that a way through is guaranteed. Proof assistants like Lean give you five basic "moves" to build that argument, like tools in a toolbox.

`intro` says: "Let's just assume the starting condition and see where it leads." `exact` says: "I already know the precise answer — here it is." `apply` reaches for a known rule and fits your goal into it, like snapping a puzzle piece into place. `constructor` builds something new piece by piece, the way you'd assemble a toy from parts. `cases` says: "This could go one of several ways — let's check each one."

Formally, each tactic transforms a goal $G$ into zero or more subgoals $G_1, \dots, G_n$ such that proving all $G_i$ proves $G$.

Now you try: given a hint that a number is either even or odd, which tactic lets you handle both possibilities separately?

### equality_rfl (14.3s)

## Equality Rfl

Imagine you have two names for your pet — "Buddy" and "the dog who sleeps on my bed." Different words, same animal. You don't need a vet to prove they're the same; you just look and see it's obviously true. That's the feeling behind $\text{rfl}$.

In Lean, some equalities are true for a boring, wonderful reason: both sides simplify down to the exact same thing. Take $2 + 2 = 4$. If you compute $2+2$, you get $4$. Now both sides of the equals sign are literally identical. There's nothing left to argue — the proof is simply:

$$2 + 2 = 4 \quad \text{proved by} \quad \text{rfl}$$

$\text{rfl}$ works whenever Lean can crunch both sides down to identical results. It's the simplest, laziest, most satisfying kind of proof — you don't build an argument, you just let the computer notice they match.

**Now you try:** Would $\text{rfl}$ prove $3 + 1 = 4$? What about $x + 1 = 1 + x$ for an unknown $x$?

### inductive_types (7.5s)

## Inductive Types

Imagine you have a box of building blocks, and someone tells you: "Every tower is either an empty platform, or a platform with one more block stacked on a tower." That's the *whole* rulebook. No exceptions, no secret extra towers. If you can build something by following just those two rules, it's a tower. If you can't, it isn't.

This is exactly how mathematicians define numbers in Lean, a computer proof-checker. They write:

```
inductive Nat
  | zero
  | succ (n : Nat)
```

which says: a natural number is either $\text{zero}$, or the successor of another natural number, written $\text{succ}(n)$. So $3$ is really $\text{succ}(\text{succ}(\text{succ}(\text{zero})))$ — three blocks stacked up!

The magic: nothing about numbers, lists, or true/false is secretly built into the computer. They're all defined this same tiny way, from their constructors up.

**Now you try:** Using only "empty" and "one more," describe how you'd build the number 2 — how many "one mores" do you need?

### rewriting_simp (8.7s)

## Rewriting Simp

Imagine your math homework says $3 + 4 = 7$. If a problem later has "$3+4$" sitting in it, you know you can cross it out and write $7$ instead — same value, cleaner look. That swap is called *rewriting*.

In Lean, the tactic `rw` does exactly this: given a proven equality $a = b$, it hunts for $a$ inside your goal and replaces it with $b$. It's like using a fact you already trust to simplify a puzzle you're still solving.

Now imagine you had a *whole toolbox* of such facts — hundreds of them, like "$n+0=n$" or "double negation cancels" — and instead of applying them one at a time, a helper robot tries all of them, again and again, until nothing more can be simplified. That's `simp`: a fleet of community-approved rewrite rules, fired automatically until your goal is as tidy as possible.

Formally, `rw` performs one substitution using $a=b$; `simp` performs repeated substitution using a curated rule set until reaching a normal form.

**Now you try:** if you know $x + 0 = x$, what would `rw` turn "$x+0=5$" into?

### typeclasses_structures (8.6s)

## Typeclasses Structures

Imagine a toolbox. You don't ask "does this hammer feel like a Tuesday hammer or a Friday hammer?" You ask "does it hit nails?" If yes, it works with every nail-related job in your house — automatically. You never re-explain hammering for each nail.

Mathematicians face the same puzzle: many different things — whole numbers, clock times, shuffles of a deck of cards — all "combine and undo" the same way. Rather than proving the same theorem over and over for each one, mathematicians build one packet called a *group structure*: a carrier (the set of things) plus its rules (how to combine, how to undo, which one does nothing). Anything carrying that packet automatically gets every group theorem for free — a computer can even detect which packet an object has and hand over the right rules by itself.

Formally, a group is a pair $(G, \cdot)$ satisfying: closure, associativity $ (a\cdot b)\cdot c = a\cdot(b\cdot c)$, an identity $e$, and inverses $a^{-1}$.

**Now you try**: Check — do clock hours (add, wrap at 12) form a group?

### mathlib (5.6s)

## Mathlib

Imagine a library with a million books, but no librarian — and no card catalog. If you wanted the book "every even number bigger than 2 is a sum of two primes," you'd have to search shelf by shelf. Mathematicians building **mathlib** faced exactly this problem: they'd proved a million lines of facts, but proving something new meant first checking nobody had proved it already.

Their solution wasn't a smarter search engine — it was a *naming law*. Every fact's name must describe its shape: what it starts with, what it concludes, in a fixed order. A fact about "if $a$ is less than $b$, then $a+c$ is less than $b+c$" is always named the same predictable way, no matter who wrote it.

So finding a fact isn't luck — it's translation. You picture the fact, write its name using the rules, and it's usually already there.

**Now you try:** Suppose the naming rule is "conclusion first, condition after." Name a fact that says "if it rains, the ground is wet."

### formalization_gap (8.7s)

## Formalization Gap

Imagine you tell a friend, "Every kid in class has a pet." You really mean it — but what you *typed* into your notebook was "Some kid in class has a pet." Both sentences look tidy. Both could even be true at the same time! But they say very different things, and nobody checking your spelling would catch the mix-up.

That's the formalization gap: the space between what you *meant* and what you *wrote*, when writing turns ideas into a formal language a computer can check. A proof checker (like Lean) is a fantastic spelling-and-grammar checker — it verifies that every logical step follows from the last. But it never asks, "Is this the sentence you meant to write?" A flipped quantifier ($\forall$ swapped for $\exists$) or a hidden always-false assumption can make a statement provable and *still* wrong about the world.

Formally: a proof of $\phi$ guarantees only that $\phi$ follows from the axioms — not that $\phi$ matches the informal claim $\phi'$ it was meant to encode.

**Now you try**: Rewrite "Every kid has a pet" so it's *impossible* to accidentally flip into "Some kid has a pet" — how would you make the meaning unmistakable?

### verified_mathematics (6.4s)

## Verified Mathematics

Imagine two friends arguing about whether a huge, tricky puzzle has been solved correctly. One says "trust me, I checked it." The other says "prove it — to a robot who accepts nothing on faith." That robot is a proof assistant called Lean.

A mathematical proof is really a chain of small logical steps, each one following from the last by a rule everyone agrees on. Normally, human reviewers read the whole chain and check every link — slow, and sometimes a tiny broken link slips through unnoticed. A *formal proof* writes every single step in a language so precise that a computer can check each link itself. If the computer accepts it, no step was skipped, guessed, or wrong.

Big teams now build these formal proofs together, like assembling a giant machine from many small verified parts, for results once considered too enormous for any one person to fully check. Peer review becomes mechanical: not "do I trust you?" but "did the checker say yes?"

**Now you try:** Write three tiny steps proving "if a cookie jar has cookies, and you take none, it still has cookies" — precise enough that a robot with no common sense could follow each one.
