## Parent Structure

Imagine you find a mysterious number: 7. Is it a whole number? A fraction? Part of an equation with letters in it? You can't tell just by looking — you need to know where it *lives*.

In Sage, every single object has a home address called its **parent**. The number 7 might live in $\mathbb{Z}$ (the integers), or in $\mathbb{Q}$ (the fractions), or even in a strange finite world like $GF(7)$, where numbers wrap around like a clock. Same digit, different neighborhoods, different rules!

Why does this matter? Because the neighborhood decides what math is even allowed. In $\mathbb{Z}$, dividing 7 by 2 doesn't give a whole number back — it breaks the rules. In $\mathbb{Q}$, it's perfectly fine.

To find any object's address, just ask Sage:

$$x.\texttt{parent}() \;\longrightarrow\; \text{the mathematical world } x \text{ belongs to}$$

**Now you try:** Type `7.parent()` and `(7/2).parent()` in Sage. Do you get the same neighborhood both times?

### symbolic_variable (0.0s)

## Symbolic Variable

Imagine a mystery box. You don't know what's inside — could be $3$, could be $100$, could be $-7$. You can't add "the box" to $5$ and get one fixed number, because the box isn't holding still. But you *can* still reason about it: "the box plus five" is a perfectly good thought, even before you peek inside.

That mystery box is what a symbol like $x$ does in math. Writing `var('x')` doesn't create *a* number — it creates a placeholder that stands for *every possible number at once*. This is the floor of generality: instead of asking "what is $3+5$?" we ask "what is $x+5$?" and the answer, $x+5$, works no matter what $x$ turns out to be.

This is why algebra comes before arithmetic in power, even if it comes after in school. Arithmetic answers one question at a time. Algebra answers all of them simultaneously, by naming the unknown instead of guessing it.

$$x + 5$$

**Now you try:** Pick your own mystery box, call it $n$. Write down "$n$ plus $n$" — you've just built a rule that works for every number in the universe, without ever opening the box.

### symbolic_expression (0.0s)

## Symbolic Expression

Imagine you write $x + 2$ on a piece of paper, but $x$ is a mystery number — nobody has said what it is yet. In SageMath's Symbolic Ring, $SR$, that's exactly what a **symbolic expression** is: a little frozen sentence built out of symbols and operations, kept exactly as written until *you* decide to do something with it.

Why does this feel right? Because it's how algebra actually works in your head — you don't need to know $x$ to know that $x + x = 2x$, or that $(x+1)^2$ "wants to become" $x^2 + 2x + 1$. The computer holds that sentence the same patient way, refusing to guess a value too early.

Formally, an expression in $SR$ is a tree: symbols like $x, y$ are leaves, and operations like $+, \times, \sin(\cdot)$ are branches combining them:

$$e = \sin(x) + x^2 \cdot y$$

Nothing here is a number — it's a *structure*, exact and symbolic, until you substitute values or ask Sage to simplify it.

**Now you try:** type `x + x` in Sage. Does it say $2x$, or does it wait for you to ask?

### exact_integer (0.0s)

## Exact Integer

Imagine counting grains of sand, one by one, forever — never rounding, never estimating, never losing track. That's what an ordinary computer number *can't* do. A regular "float" number is like a ruler with only so many tick marks: stretch it too far, and it starts skipping between marks, quietly losing precision. But a Sage `Integer` is different — it's like an infinitely stretchy ruler with no ticks skipped at all.

Here's the key idea: $2^{100}$ isn't approximated as some rounded decimal — it's stored as the *actual, exact* whole number, all 31 digits of it, with nothing lost. Sage integers have arbitrary precision: they grow as large as needed and never overflow or round off.

$$2^{100} = 1267650600228229401496703205376$$

That's one single exact number — not a float pretending to be close.

**Now you try:** Ask Sage for `2^200` and then `3^300`. Notice: no error, no rounding, no "too big" — just the exact answer, every digit intact.

### substitution_evaluation (0.0s)

## Substitution Evaluation

Imagine a vending machine with a slot labeled $x$. You can drop in a $3, a $5, or a $100 — the machine doesn't care, it just waits. The expression $x^2 + 1$ is like that machine before anything is dropped in: a *rule*, not a number.

The moment you say "let $x = 4$," something special happens in two separate steps. First, **substitution** swaps the symbol for the number everywhere it appears: $x^2+1 \to 4^2+1$. This is `subs()` — pure replacement, no arithmetic yet, like literally taping a "4" over every "$x$" sign. Second, **evaluation** actually does the math: $4^2+1 = 17$. This is `n()` — turning that taped-up expression into an actual answer.

Why split them? Because sometimes you want to *see* the substituted expression before crunching it — to check your work, or to substitute again with a different rule.

**Now you try:** Take $y = 2t + 5$. Substitute $t=3$ (don't compute yet). Then evaluate. What did each step give you?

### exact_rational (0.0s)

## Exact Rational

Cut a pizza into 3 equal slices and eat one. You ate exactly $\frac{1}{3}$ of the pizza — not 0.333, not "about a third," but *exactly* one part out of three. If you tried to write that as a decimal, you'd be writing 3's forever: $0.3333\ldots$ The decimal never finishes the job. But $\frac{1}{3}$ finishes instantly — it's already the whole truth, frozen as a fraction.

That's the idea behind an **exact rational**: a number kept as a pair, $\frac{a}{b}$ (a numerator and a denominator, with $b \neq 0$), rather than converted into a decimal. It never rounds, never approximates, never loses a digit. Two rationals like $\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{2}{6}$ are secretly the same number — they represent equal division, just sliced differently. Decimals only show up when *you* ask "what does this look like written out?" — and even then, it's just a picture of the fraction, not a replacement for it.

**Now you try:** Take $\frac{2}{7}$. Don't convert it to a decimal. What is $\frac{2}{7} + \frac{3}{7}$, kept exact?

### plotting_visualization (0.0s)

## Plotting Visualization

Imagine you solve a puzzle, get an answer, and someone asks, "Are you sure?" What do you do? You check it — maybe by drawing it. That's what plotting does for math.

Say you find that a curve is $y = x^2$. You could trust the algebra... or you could draw it and *see* the smiling U-shape appear. `plot` does exactly that: it takes an expression, picks many values of $x$, computes $y$ for each, and connects the dots into a picture. `plot3d` does the same trick for surfaces with two variables — like drawing a hill from a formula. `implicit_plot` handles trickier equations like $x^2 + y^2 = 4$, where $y$ isn't alone on one side — it hunts for every point that makes the equation true and shades them in, revealing a circle.

The picture isn't proof, but it's a powerful sanity check: if your equation says "circle" and the drawing shows a squished oval, something's wrong.

**Now you try:** Plot $y = x^3$. Does the picture match the shape you imagined before drawing it?

### preparser (6.2s)

## Preparser

Imagine you and a robot both read the sentence "cut the cake in half." You know exactly what that means. But if the robot takes it too literally, it might try to cut the cake into the letters "h-a-l-f"! Sage has a similar problem: mathematicians write $x^2$ to mean "$x$ squared," but in plain computer language, the symbol $\char`^$ usually means something totally different (a logic operation, not power).

So before Sage runs your code, a helper called the **preparser** quietly rewrites it — like a translator standing between you and the computer. It turns $\char`^$ into "raise to a power," turns the numbers you type into exact whole numbers instead of fuzzy decimals, and turns $1/2$ into a precise fraction instead of an approximate answer.

If you ever want to skip the translator and speak the computer's native language directly, you can turn it off with `preparser(False)`.

**Now you try:** predict what $2\char`^3$ becomes after the preparser translates it — is it "2 XOR 3" or "2 to the power of 3"?

### exact_vs_float (10.1s)

## Exact Vs Float

**Picture this**: You cut a pizza into 3 equal slices and eat 1. How much did you eat? Your calculator says $0.3333333...$ and keeps going forever, never quite finishing. But you *know* the answer: exactly one slice out of three.

**Intuition**: A calculator's decimal is like a photograph of a number — a snapshot that's close enough for most purposes, but sometimes blurry at the edges. The fraction $\frac{1}{3}$ isn't a snapshot; it *is* the number, perfectly, with nothing lost. Computers that store $\frac{1}{3}$ as a decimal have to round it somewhere, and rounding is a tiny lie that can pile up if you're not careful.

**Minimal formalisation**: We keep numbers as exact fractions or symbols all the way through our reasoning: $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$, never $0.333... + 0.333... \approx 0.667$. Only at the very last step, when we want to *read* the answer, do we ask for a decimal: $n\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = 0.667$. Converting early throws away truth; converting late keeps it.

**Now you try**: Add $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4}$ exactly first. Then turn just that final answer into a decimal. Which felt more trustworthy?

## Interactive Notebooks

Imagine a lab notebook that doesn't just record what you did — it *redoes* it, right in front of you, the moment you ask. That's a Jupyter notebook running the SageMath kernel: a mix of writing, math, and living code in one document.

The magic ingredient is the `@interact` slider. Attach it above a function, and Sage builds a little control panel — drag a slider, and every plot, number, or shape on the page instantly recomputes. Want to see how a parabola changes as you stretch it? Don't retype the formula twenty times — slide a number and *watch* it happen.

This turns math into something you can play with, not just look at. Under the hood, the preparser quietly translates the friendly math you type (like `2x` or `x^2`) into precise code Sage understands — you get natural notation, but rigorous computation.

**Now you try:** Build a slider for $a$ in $y = a x^2$, and describe how the curve's shape changes as $a$ grows.

## Stem Classroom

Imagine two students in different countries, one whose family can pay for expensive software, one who can't. If their math tools give different answers to the same problem, something has gone very wrong. A Computer Algebra System (CAS) with no license fee fixes this instantly: every student, everywhere, runs the exact same mathematics — same symbolic simplifications, same numerical results, down to the last digit.

Why does this matter? Mathematics itself doesn't care who owns the computer. A proof is true or it isn't; an equation simplifies the same way whether you're in a village school or a university lab. Free, open tools let the *math* be the great equalizer, not the wallet.

Formally: a CAS manipulates symbolic expressions like $\int x^2\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3} + C$ exactly, not by approximation — and an `@interact` widget lets a student drag a slider and *watch* a formula like $$f(x) = a\sin(bx)$$ reshape itself live, turning a static lesson into an experiment.

**Now you try**: pick any formula from your textbook and imagine turning one number in it into a slider. What would change as you dragged it?
