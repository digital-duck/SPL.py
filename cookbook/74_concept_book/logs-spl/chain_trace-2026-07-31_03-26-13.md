### sequence (17.0s)

## Sequence

**Definition.** A *sequence* is a function $a : \mathbb{N} \to X$, where $X$ is a set (typically $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, or a metric space $(X, d)$). We write $(a_n)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ and say $(a_n)$ *converges* to $L \in X$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $n > N$ implies $d(a_n, L) < \varepsilon$. The limit, when it exists, is unique in a Hausdorff space.

**Theorem (Completeness and Cauchy Criterion).** In a complete metric space, $(a_n)$ converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that $m, n > N$ implies $d(a_m, a_n) < \varepsilon$.

*Proof sketch.* Convergence implies Cauchy by the triangle inequality: $d(a_m, a_n) \leq d(a_m, L) + d(L, a_n) < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2$. The converse is the definition of completeness; it fails in $\mathbb{Q}$ (where $a_n = \lfloor n\sqrt{2}\rfloor / n$ is Cauchy but has no rational limit), motivating the construction of $\mathbb{R}$ via Dedekind cuts or Cauchy equivalence classes. $\square$

**Literature context.** The Cauchy criterion is classical (Rudin, *Principles of Mathematical Analysis*, §3), but its generalisation to Banach and Fréchet spaces underpins functional analysis and numerical methods for PDEs. Open questions arise in non-Archimedean and $p$-adic settings, where the ultrametric inequality strengthens convergence dramatically (Robert, *A Course in $p$-adic Analysis*).

**Suggested investigation.** Construct explicit Cauchy sequences in $\mathbb{Q}$ converging to $\sqrt{2}$, $e$, or a root of $x^5 - x - 1 = 0$. Verify numerically that the sequence is Cauchy yet has no rational limit. Extend to the $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$: show that $\sum_{k=0}^{n} p^k$ is Cauchy under $|\cdot|_p$ and identify its limit.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the metric space explicitly, prove both directions of the Cauchy criterion, exhibit a concrete non-convergent Cauchy sequence over $\mathbb{Q}$, and identify what structural property of $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{Z}_p$) is responsible for completing the space — distinguishing the algebraic from the topological content of the argument.

### time_series (46.0s)

## Time Series

**Definition.** A *weakly stationary time series* is a stochastic process $\{X_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu$ for all $t$ and $\text{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) = \gamma(h)$ for a function $\gamma$ depending only on the lag $h \in \mathbb{Z}$, not on $t$. This single condition—that the second-order structure is time-invariant—is the operative primitive: it licenses both Fourier-analytic tools and linear prediction theory without requiring the stronger assumption of strict stationarity.

**Theorem (Wold Decomposition).** Every zero-mean purely nondeterministic weakly stationary process $\{X_t\}$ admits the representation
$$X_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j}, \quad \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j^2 < \infty,$$
where $\{\varepsilon_t\}$ is white noise with variance $\sigma^2$ and $\psi_0 = 1$. The coefficients $\{\psi_j\}$ are unique. *Proof sketch:* Project $X_t$ onto the closed linear span $\overline{\text{sp}}\{X_s : s < t\}$; the one-step prediction errors $\varepsilon_t = X_t - \hat{X}_t$ are mutually orthogonal, and successive Gram–Schmidt steps recover the $\psi_j$ uniquely. $\square$

**Literature context.** Wold's theorem establishes that weak stationarity is precisely the condition under which linear prediction is well-posed; the non-uniqueness that arises in non-stationary settings (unit roots, structural breaks) is an active research area. Brockwell and Davis (1991) give the canonical treatment; Anderson (1971) covers the multivariate extension. Open questions concern whether weak stationarity is the right regularity class for neural sequence models—Transformers and state-space models implicitly learn $\psi_j$ weights without enforcing the $\ell^2$ summability constraint.

**Suggested investigation.** Generate sample paths from a weakly stationary AR(2) process with roots near but strictly inside the unit circle. Estimate $\psi_j$ via the Durbin–Levinson recursion and verify empirically that the estimated coefficients lie in $\ell^2$. Then perturb one root to land on the unit circle and document precisely where the recursion breaks down and why.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should establish: the parameter configuration and its distance from the unit circle, the estimated $\psi_j$ sequence and its partial sums against the theoretical $\ell^2$ bound, and a precise statement of which step in the Wold proof fails when weak stationarity is violated.

### trend (30.6s)

## Trend

**Definition.** Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^T$ be a discrete-time stochastic process. Its *trend component* $\tau_t$ is the low-frequency, slowly-varying systematic movement obtained via the additive decomposition $y_t = \tau_t + s_t + \varepsilon_t$, where $s_t$ denotes seasonality and $\varepsilon_t$ is a zero-mean irregular component. The critical distinction is whether $\tau_t$ is *deterministic* — $\tau_t = \alpha + \beta t$ for fixed $\alpha, \beta$ — or *stochastic*, governed by $\tau_t = \tau_{t-1} + \eta_t$ with $\eta_t \overset{iid}{\sim} (0, \sigma_\eta^2)$ (a random walk, hence $I(1)$). These regimes are not aesthetically equivalent: conflating them yields spurious regression, a pathology rigorously established by Phillips (1986) showing that OLS $t$-statistics diverge when both $y_t$ and $x_t$ are independent $I(1)$ processes.

**Theorem (Beveridge–Nelson Decomposition).** Any $I(1)$ process with Wold representation $\Delta y_t = C(L)\varepsilon_t$, $C(1) \neq 0$, admits a unique decomposition into a random walk and a stationary remainder: $y_t = C(1)\sum_{s=1}^t \varepsilon_s + \tilde{y}_t$, where $\tilde{y}_t$ is $I(0)$. The permanent component $C(1)\sum \varepsilon_s$ *is* the stochastic trend; no signal-extraction oracle separates it cleanly from the cycle. The Hodrick–Prescott filter instead solves
$$
\min_{\{\tau_t\}} \sum_{t=1}^T (y_t - \tau_t)^2 + \lambda \sum_{t=2}^{T-1}(\Delta^2 \tau_{t+1})^2,
$$
trading fidelity against smoothness via hyperparameter $\lambda$. Hamilton (2018, *ReStat*) shows the HP filter introduces spurious periodicity when applied to unit-root data — an unresolved methodological controversy.

**What is known; what is open.** The Augmented Dickey–Fuller and KPSS tests offer complementary null hypotheses (unit root vs. trend-stationarity) but have low power in small samples and near-unit-root DGPs. Structural-break-robust tests (Perron 1989, Zivot–Andrews 1992) remain active research: consistent break-point selection with trend estimation is not settled when the number of breaks is unknown.

**Suggested investigation.** Generate paired series — one trend-stationary ($y_t = 0.1t + \varepsilon_t$, $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$), one random-walk ($y_t = y_{t-1} + \eta_t$) — for $T \in \{50, 200, 1000\}$. Apply ADF, KPSS, and HP extraction at $\lambda \in \{6.25, 100, 1600\}$. Record rejection rates across 500 Monte Carlo replications. Extend by injecting a structural break at $t = T/2$ and assessing whether the Zivot–Andrews procedure recovers the break date.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: the empirical size and power of each test under each DGP; how $\lambda$ choice distorts HP trend estimates when the true DGP is $I(1)$; and whether break-detection materially improves size control. Tables should report rejection rates with 95% Monte Carlo confidence intervals; figures should overlay estimated and true trends to visualize HP distortion.

### differencing (24.9s)

## Differencing

**Definition.** Let $\{X_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}$ be a discrete-time stochastic process. The *lag-$d$ difference operator* $\Delta^{(d)}$ is defined recursively by $\Delta^{(0)} X_t = X_t$, $\Delta^{(1)} X_t = X_t - X_{t-1}$, and $\Delta^{(k)} X_t = \Delta^{(1)}(\Delta^{(k-1)} X_t)$ for $k \geq 2$. In terms of the backshift operator $B$ (where $B X_t = X_{t-1}$), one writes $\Delta^{(k)} = (1 - B)^k$, a polynomial in $B$ of degree $k$.

**Theorem (Stationarity via differencing).** If $\{X_t\}$ is an $\mathrm{I}(d)$ process — integrated of order $d$, meaning $\Delta^{(d)} X_t$ is covariance-stationary but $\Delta^{(d-1)} X_t$ is not — then $Y_t = \Delta^{(d)} X_t$ has a well-defined, time-invariant autocovariance function $\gamma_Y(h) = \mathrm{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-h})$.

*Proof sketch.* Expand $\Delta^{(d)} X_t = \sum_{j=0}^{d} \binom{d}{j} (-1)^j X_{t-j}$ via the binomial theorem applied to $(1-B)^d$. Stationarity of this linear combination follows from the assumption that $\Delta^{(d)} X_t$ has finite, shift-invariant second moments, verifiable via spectral density arguments: the spectral density of $\Delta^{(d)} X_t$ is $f_Y(\lambda) = |1 - e^{-i\lambda}|^{2d} f_X(\lambda)$, which is integrable when $f_X$ has the characteristic $O(|\lambda|^{-2d})$ pole at the origin. $\square$

**Literature context.** The integration order framework originates with Box and Jenkins (1976) and was formalized by Engle and Granger (1987) in the context of cointegration (Nobel Prize, 2003). The unit-root testing literature — Dickey-Fuller, Phillips-Perron, KPSS — addresses the empirical challenge of determining $d$ from finite samples, where over- and under-differencing both incur bias-variance costs. Fractional differencing ($d \in \mathbb{R}$) extends the framework; Granger and Joyeux (1980) and Hosking (1981) established that $\mathrm{ARFIMA}(p,d,q)$ models capture long-memory processes with $0 < d < 1/2$, a regime inaccessible to integer differencing. The determination of $d$ via maximum likelihood or wavelet estimation remains an active area, particularly for non-Gaussian or nonlinear processes.

**Suggested investigation.** Simulate $\mathrm{I}(1)$ and $\mathrm{I}(2)$ series; apply Augmented Dickey-Fuller tests before and after each round of differencing to confirm theoretical integration orders. Then fit an $\mathrm{ARIMA}(p,d,q)$ model via AIC/BIC grid search, and compare forecast RMSE against a naïve benchmark on a held-out window. Extend to a real financial or macroeconomic series (e.g., log GDP, log equity index) and replicate the Engle-Granger cointegration test for a pair of $\mathrm{I}(1)$ series.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the integration order hypothesis, justify the chosen unit-root test and its power limitations under finite $T$, present ADF statistics with lag selection rationale, and report the spectral density estimate before and after differencing to visualize the pole removal. Discuss whether over-differencing introduces a unit root in the MA polynomial and how model identification criteria resolve the trade-off.

### error (80.7s)

## Error

**Definition.** Given a true quantity $x^* \in \mathcal{X}$ and an approximation $\hat{x} \in \mathcal{X}$, the *absolute error* is
$$e = \|\hat{x} - x^*\|$$
under a chosen norm $\|\cdot\|$. In statistical learning, this extends naturally to the expected squared deviation of an estimator $\hat{f}$ from the Bayes-optimal predictor $f^*$, averaged over the randomness of the training sample.

**Theorem (Bias–Variance Decomposition).** For the squared loss $\ell(a,b) = (a-b)^2$ and any estimator $\hat{f}$,

$$\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f}(x) - y)^2] = \underbrace{\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f^*(x)\bigr)^2}_{\mathrm{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}(x))}_{\mathrm{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\mathrm{irreducible}},$$

where $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(y \mid x)$. *Proof sketch:* Decompose $\hat{f}(x) - y = [\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}\hat{f}(x)] + [\mathbb{E}\hat{f}(x) - f^*(x)] + [f^*(x) - y]$, square, and take expectations; cross terms involving $\epsilon = y - f^*(x)$ vanish by independence of $\hat{f}$ from the noise. $\square$

**Literature context.** The tradeoff is classical (Geman et al., 1992), yet its behavior under overparameterization is not settled: Belkin et al. (2019) showed that test error can *decrease again* past the interpolation threshold — the double-descent phenomenon — contradicting the standard U-shaped intuition. Decompositions for non-squared losses (cross-entropy, hinge) lack closed forms and remain active research territory (Neal et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020).

**Suggested investigation.** Fit polynomial regressors of degree $d = 1, \ldots, n-1$ to a synthetic dataset with known $f^*$ and $\sigma^2$; estimate the three components empirically via 500 bootstrap resamples. Plot each against $d$ and locate the double-descent regime near $d = n-1$. Replicate on a neural-network width sweep on MNIST to observe interpolation-threshold behavior in practice.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should specify the norm and loss governing the decomposition, provide analytic bias and variance expressions under the chosen model family, report confidence intervals on all empirical estimates, and connect observed regimes (underfitting, overfitting, interpolation) to theoretical bounds from VC dimension or Rademacher complexity.

### lag (28.8s)

## Lag

**Definition.** A *lag* of magnitude $\tau \geq 0$ is a pure temporal displacement between a cause and its observable effect. In continuous time, a linear time-invariant system with output delay satisfies

$$y(t) = f\bigl(u(t - \tau)\bigr),$$

whose Laplace-domain representation is $Y(s) = e^{-\tau s} G(s) U(s)$, where $e^{-\tau s}$ is the *delay operator*. In discrete time, a lag-$k$ relationship is encoded by the backshift operator $B^k$: $y_t = B^k u_t \equiv u_{t-k}$. Lag also appears in statistical settings as the argument of an autocovariance function $\gamma(k) = \mathrm{Cov}(X_t, X_{t-k})$, bridging signal processing and time-series econometrics.

**Stability and the transcendental characteristic equation.** Consider the scalar delay differential equation $\dot{x}(t) = ax(t) + bx(t-\tau)$. Substituting $x(t) = e^{\lambda t}$ yields the characteristic quasi-polynomial

$$\lambda = a + b e^{-\lambda \tau},$$

which has infinitely many roots $\{\lambda_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ in $\mathbb{C}$. This infinite spectral dimension is the central analytical obstacle: unlike ODE systems, stability cannot be verified by checking finitely many eigenvalues. The *Pontryagin criterion* and the *D-subdivision method* (Sipahi et al., 2011, *IEEE Trans. Autom. Control*) characterize the stability boundary as a curve in the $(a, b)$ plane parametrized by $\omega \in \mathbb{R}$. A key result: for $a < 0$ and $|b| < |a|$, the system is stable for all $\tau \geq 0$; outside this cone, there exists a critical delay $\tau^*$ beyond which stability is lost.

**Literature context.** Gu, Kharitonov, and Chen (2003, *Stability of Time-Delay Systems*, Birkhäuser) is the standard reference for Lyapunov–Krasovskii functional methods. Current open problems include tight stability conditions for coupled delay networks (relevant to Momagrid-style distributed inference pipelines, where round-trip communication introduces heterogeneous node-level lags) and data-driven identification of $\tau$ when the delay is unknown or state-dependent (see Brunton & Kutz, 2022, *Data-Driven Science and Engineering*, Chapter 7).

**Suggested investigation.** Fix $a = -1$ and sweep $(b, \tau)$ over a grid; numerically locate roots of $\lambda - a - be^{-\lambda\tau} = 0$ using the Lambert $W$ function, and verify agreement with the Pontryagin boundary. Then introduce a Smith predictor and measure the reduction in effective lag in closed-loop step response.

**Report note.** A write-up should state the characteristic equation explicitly, prove at least one stability boundary case analytically, compare the analytic boundary against numerical root-locus plots, and report the critical delay $\tau^*$ with a sensitivity analysis to parameter uncertainty in $b$.

### autoregressive_model (21.0s)

## Autoregressive Model

**Definition.** An autoregressive (AR) model of order $p$ represents a discrete-time stochastic process $\{X_t\}$ as a linear combination of its own past values plus white noise:

$$X_t = \sum_{k=1}^{p} \phi_k X_{t-k} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2).$$

The coefficient vector $\boldsymbol{\phi} = (\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_p)^\top$ fully characterizes the model's short-range dependence structure. In the language of probability, $X_t$ is conditionally Gaussian given its history: $p(X_t \mid X_{t-1}, \ldots, X_{t-p}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^\top \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \sigma^2)$.

**Theorem (Stationarity condition).** The AR($p$) process is covariance-stationary if and only if all roots of the characteristic polynomial $\Phi(z) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^p \phi_k z^k$ lie strictly outside the unit disk in $\mathbb{C}$. Under stationarity, the autocorrelation function satisfies the Yule–Walker equations $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_p \boldsymbol{\phi} = \boldsymbol{\gamma}_p$, where $\Gamma_p$ is the Toeplitz autocorrelation matrix and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_p = (\gamma(1), \ldots, \gamma(p))^\top$.

**Proof sketch.** Write the AR($p$) in companion form $\mathbf{Y}_t = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{Y}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{e}_t$. Stationarity requires that all eigenvalues of $\mathbf{F}$ lie within the unit disk, which is equivalent to the root condition on $\Phi(z)$.

**Literature context.** Box and Jenkins (1970) established AR models as the foundation of time-series analysis; Hamilton (1994) remains the canonical reference for macroeconomic applications. The deep connection between AR models and neural language models — where the joint probability $p(x_1, \ldots, x_T) = \prod_t p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ is factored autoregressively — has revived theoretical interest (Bengio et al., 2003; Brown et al., 2020). Open questions include: optimal order selection under model misspecification, AR approximation of long-memory (ARFIMA) processes, and the statistical efficiency gap between least-squares and maximum-likelihood estimators in finite samples.

**Suggested investigation.** Fit AR($p$) models of varying order to a real-world time series (e.g., log-returns of an asset or a macroeconomic indicator). Estimate $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ via ordinary least squares and compare with the Yule–Walker estimator. Verify the stationarity condition numerically by plotting the characteristic roots, and use AIC/BIC to perform order selection. Then generate synthetic data from the fitted model and evaluate one-step-ahead forecast intervals.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should establish: the estimated coefficient vector with standard errors, a root plot confirming stationarity, a diagnostic of residual autocorrelation (Ljung–Box test), and a table comparing forecast RMSE across orders. Connecting the classical AR framework to the autoregressive factorization used in transformer language models makes for a compelling theoretical bridge.

### integrative_component (25.2s)

## Integrative Component

**Definition.** In a closed-loop control system, the *integrative component* (I-component) of the control signal is defined as

$$u_I(t) = K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau,$$

where $e(\tau) = r(\tau) - y(\tau)$ denotes the tracking error between reference $r$ and plant output $y$, and $K_i > 0$ is the integral gain. In the frequency domain this reduces to multiplication by $K_i/s$, introducing a pole at the origin — a structural zero in the open-loop sensitivity function.

**Theorem (Steady-State Error Elimination).** Consider a linear time-invariant plant $G(s)$ with unity feedback and an integral controller $C(s) = K_i/s$. If the closed-loop system $T(s) = C(s)G(s)/[1+C(s)G(s)]$ is internally stable, then for any step reference $R(s) = A/s$ the steady-state error satisfies $\lim_{t\to\infty} e(t) = 0$.

*Proof sketch.* By the Final Value Theorem, $\lim_{t\to\infty} e(t) = \lim_{s\to 0} s E(s)$. The error transfer function is $E(s) = R(s)/[1+C(s)G(s)]$, so $s E(s) = sA/\{s[1 + K_i G(s)/s]\} = A/[1 + K_i G(0)/0^+] \to 0$, provided $G(0)$ is finite and nonzero. $\square$

**Literature Context.** The internal model principle (Francis & Wonham, 1976, *Automatica*) generalizes this: a controller achieves asymptotic tracking of a class of reference signals if and only if it internally replicates the signal's generator dynamics. Integration is the canonical instance for step inputs. Open problems include robust integral action under plant uncertainty (see Morari & Zafiriou, 1989) and the interaction between integrator windup and nonlinear saturation, which remains an active area in anti-windup synthesis (Zaccarian & Teel, 2011).

**Suggested Investigation.** Implement a first-order plant $G(s) = 1/(s+a)$ in simulation and sweep $K_i$ and $a$ jointly. Record the gain and phase margins as a function of $K_i$, the transient overshoot under step inputs, and the onset of windup when the actuator saturates at $\pm u_{\max}$. Compare a naive I-controller with a clamping anti-windup scheme (back-calculation).

**Report-Writing Note.** A write-up should establish: (i) analytic stability bounds on $K_i$ as a function of $a$; (ii) empirical transient metrics (rise time, overshoot, settling time) against theoretical predictions from the dominant-pole approximation; and (iii) a quantitative account of windup onset and how the anti-windup modification restores nominal transient performance.

### moving_average_model (53.1s)

## Moving Average Model

**Definition.** A *moving average model of order $q$*, denoted $\mathrm{MA}(q)$, is the process
$$X_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}, \qquad \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),$$
where $\{\theta_j\}_{j=1}^q \subset \mathbb{R}$ are the MA coefficients. In backshift notation, $X_t - \mu = \Theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t$ with $\Theta(z) = 1 + \theta_1 z + \cdots + \theta_q z^q$.

**Theorem (Stationarity).** *Every $\mathrm{MA}(q)$ process is weakly stationary regardless of the values of $\{\theta_j\}$.* The autocovariance function satisfies
$$\gamma(h) = \sigma^2 \sum_{j=0}^{q-|h|} \theta_j \theta_{j+|h|}, \quad |h| \leq q; \qquad \gamma(h) = 0, \quad |h| > q,$$
with $\theta_0 \equiv 1$. *Proof sketch.* Since $X_t - \mu$ is a finite linear combination of i.i.d. zero-mean variables, $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu$ and $\gamma(h)$ depends only on $|h|$; direct substitution yields the formula. $\square$

A structural caveat: the coefficients $\theta$ and $\theta^{-1}$ produce identical $\{\gamma(h)\}$, so the MA polynomial $\Theta(z)$ is not identifiable from second-order structure alone. Restricting all roots of $\Theta(z)$ to lie outside the unit disk selects the canonical *invertible* representative; non-invertibility degrades forecasting even when autocovariances match.

**Literature context.** The MA model originates in Yule (1927) and was systematized by Box and Jenkins (1970). The classical identification strategy examines the ACF for a hard cutoff at lag $q$, which is theoretically exact but brittle in finite samples. Open problems include: MLE for MA parameters yields a non-convex likelihood surface with local optima near the invertibility boundary; model selection criteria (AIC, BIC) degrade under heavy-tailed noise.

**Suggested investigation.** Simulate $\mathrm{MA}(1)$ processes at $\theta = 0.8$ and its dual $\theta^{-1} = 1.25$ for $n \in \{50, 200, 1000\}$. Fit both candidates by conditional and exact MLE using `statsmodels` or R's `arima()`. Measure parameter recovery bias and forecasting RMSE, then repeat with Student-$t_3$ noise to test Gaussian MLE robustness near the unit-root boundary.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should establish: the theoretical ACF cutoff, empirical ACF plots confirming or contradicting it, a comparison table of estimates and forecast errors across $\theta/\theta^{-1}$ pairs and sample sizes, and a discussion of whether enforcing invertibility during optimization consistently selects the correct branch. The investigation is complete when it can articulate why invertibility matters for forecasting even when $\{\gamma(h)\}$ is identical between dual solutions.

### arima (20.4s)

## Arima

**Definition.** An ARIMA$(p, d, q)$ process is a discrete-time stochastic process $\{X_t\}$ such that the $d$-th difference $W_t = \Delta^d X_t = (1-B)^d X_t$ satisfies the ARMA$(p,q)$ equation

$$
\phi(B)\,W_t = \theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
$$

where $\phi(B) = 1 - \phi_1 B - \cdots - \phi_p B^p$ and $\theta(B) = 1 + \theta_1 B + \cdots + \theta_q B^q$ are the AR and MA polynomials in the backshift operator $B$, and $d \geq 0$ is the integration order.

**Key result (Wold decomposition).** If $\phi(z) \neq 0$ for all $|z| \leq 1$ (invertibility of AR part) and $\theta(z) \neq 0$ for $|z| \leq 1$ (invertibility of MA part), then the ARMA component $W_t$ admits the unique causal representation $W_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j}$, where $\psi(B) = \phi(B)^{-1}\theta(B)$ and $\sum_j \psi_j^2 < \infty$. Differencing $d$ times maps a unit-root nonstationary process to a covariance-stationary one, underpinning the "I" component. The Ljung–Box statistic $Q(m) = n(n+2)\sum_{k=1}^m \hat{\rho}_k^2/(n-k)$ under $\chi^2_{m-p-q}$ provides a standard residual diagnostic.

**Literature context.** Box and Jenkins (1970) introduced ARIMA as a systematic identification–estimation–diagnostic cycle; Hamilton (1994, *Time Series Analysis*) provides the canonical treatment. Open questions include: optimal order selection under model misspecification (AIC vs. BIC in finite samples with structural breaks), and whether differencing versus fractional integration ($d \in (0,1)$, ARFIMA) better captures long-memory phenomena in macroeconomic and climate series (Granger & Joyeux, 1980; Hosking, 1981).

**Suggested investigation.** Select a nonstationary economic series (e.g., monthly CPI or equity price index). Apply the augmented Dickey–Fuller and KPSS tests to determine $d$, use ACF/PACF plots to hypothesize $(p,q)$, estimate via exact maximum likelihood, validate with Ljung–Box and recursive residuals, and compare one-step-ahead forecast RMSE against a seasonal SARIMA$(P,D,Q)_s$ baseline.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up should state the unit-root test statistics and critical values, report parameter estimates with standard errors and likelihood ratio tests for order reduction, present ACF plots of raw and differenced series alongside model residuals, and include a rolling-window out-of-sample forecast evaluation with confidence intervals.
