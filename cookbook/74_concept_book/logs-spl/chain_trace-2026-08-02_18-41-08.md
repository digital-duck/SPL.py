### derivative (0.2s)

## Derivative

The derivative of a function measures how fast its output changes relative to its input at any given point. More precisely, the derivative of $f$ at $x = a$ is the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to zero:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

Geometrically, this limit is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$. When the limit exists, $f$ is said to be **differentiable** at $a$. The derivative function $f'(x)$ gives you that slope at every point simultaneously.

**Worked Example.** Find the derivative of $f(x) = x^2$ at $x = 3$.

Apply the definition directly:

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(6 + h) = 6$$

So the slope of $y = x^2$ at $x = 3$ is $6$, and the tangent line there is $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$.

In practice, the power rule $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$ lets you skip the limit algebra: $f'(x) = 2x$, so $f'(3) = 6$ immediately.

**Problem-Solving Application.** A company's daily revenue (in thousands of dollars) is modeled by $R(x) = -2x^2 + 80x$, where $x$ is the number of units sold. To maximize revenue, you need the value of $x$ where the rate of change flips from positive to negative — that is, where $R'(x) = 0$.

Differentiate: $R'(x) = -4x + 80$. Setting this equal to zero gives $x = 20$. Since $R''(x) = -4 < 0$, the function is concave down, confirming this is a maximum. Selling 20 units yields peak daily revenue of $R(20) = -2(400) + 80(20) = \$800{,}000$.

This pattern — differentiate, set equal to zero, check concavity — is the core algorithm for optimization problems across physics, economics, and engineering. The derivative transforms a vague question ("where is the peak?") into a precise algebraic equation you can solve.

### differentials (18.4s)

## Differentials

When you compute a derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$, the symbols $dy$ and $dx$ look like a fraction but are treated as a single object. **Differentials** give those symbols independent meaning: $dx$ is a small (but finite) change in $x$ that you choose, and $dy$ is the corresponding change in $y$ that the tangent line predicts.

If $y = f(x)$ is differentiable, the differential of $y$ is defined as

$$dy = f'(x)\, dx.$$

This is simply the derivative times your chosen step size. The equation says: if you move $dx$ units along the $x$-axis, the tangent line rises (or falls) by exactly $dy$ units. The actual function changes by $\Delta y = f(x + dx) - f(x)$, which differs from $dy$ only by a tiny error that shrinks to zero much faster than $dx$ does. For small $dx$, $dy \approx \Delta y$, which is the linear approximation you use in practice.

**Worked example.** Estimate $\sqrt{4.06}$ without a calculator.

Let $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, so $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$. Choose the base point $x = 4$ (where the square root is exact) and set $dx = 0.06$.

$$dy = f'(4)\cdot dx = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4}}\cdot 0.06 = \frac{1}{4}\cdot 0.06 = 0.015.$$

Therefore $\sqrt{4.06} \approx \sqrt{4} + dy = 2 + 0.015 = 2.015$. The true value is $2.01494\ldots$, so the error is less than $0.0001$.

**Problem-solving application.** Differentials appear throughout applied work whenever you need to propagate uncertainty or estimate sensitivity. Suppose the radius of a sphere is measured as $r = 5$ cm with a possible error of $\pm 0.1$ cm. How much uncertainty does this introduce into the volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$?

Compute $dV$:

$$dV = 4\pi r^2\, dr = 4\pi (25)(0.1) = 10\pi \approx 31.4 \text{ cm}^3.$$

So a $2\%$ error in $r$ ($0.1/5$) produces roughly a $6\%$ error in $V$ ($31.4/523.6$) — matching the rule that a $p\%$ error in radius causes approximately $3p\%$ error in volume, because the exponent is 3. Differentials make that scaling law precise and quantitative without requiring you to evaluate the function at two different radii.

### propagated_error (18.9s)

## Propagated Error

When you measure a physical quantity, you always carry some uncertainty—a ruler might be precise to ±1 mm, a stopwatch to ±0.01 s. The trouble begins when you *combine* measurements in a formula. Each individual uncertainty bleeds into the final result, and the total uncertainty in that result is called the **propagated error** (or **propagation of uncertainty**).

The core rule: if a quantity $Q$ depends on independently measured variables $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, each with uncertainty $\delta x_i$, then the uncertainty in $Q$ is

$$\delta Q = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x_1}\delta x_1\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x_2}\delta x_2\right)^2 + \cdots + \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x_n}\delta x_n\right)^2}$$

Each partial derivative captures how sensitive $Q$ is to a small change in that variable. The square-root-of-sum-of-squares form assumes the errors are independent and random; it prevents worst-case pessimism from simply adding all errors together.

**Worked example.** You measure a rectangular table to find its area $A = L \times W$. You get $L = 2.00 \pm 0.02\ \text{m}$ and $W = 1.50 \pm 0.01\ \text{m}$.

The partial derivatives are $\frac{\partial A}{\partial L} = W = 1.50$ and $\frac{\partial A}{\partial W} = L = 2.00$. Plugging in:

$$\delta A = \sqrt{(1.50 \times 0.02)^2 + (2.00 \times 0.01)^2} = \sqrt{(0.030)^2 + (0.020)^2} = \sqrt{0.0013} \approx 0.036\ \text{m}^2$$

So the area is $A = 3.00 \pm 0.04\ \text{m}^2$ (rounded to one significant figure in the uncertainty).

**Problem-solving application.** A practical shortcut for products and quotients: the *relative* uncertainty in $Q = x^a y^b$ is

$$\frac{\delta Q}{Q} = \sqrt{\left(a\frac{\delta x}{x}\right)^2 + \left(b\frac{\delta y}{y}\right)^2}$$

Suppose you compute kinetic energy $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ with $m = 5.0 \pm 0.1\ \text{kg}$ and $v = 10.0 \pm 0.3\ \text{m/s}$. The relative uncertainties are $\frac{0.1}{5.0} = 2\%$ for mass and $2 \times \frac{0.3}{10.0} = 6\%$ for velocity (the exponent 2 doubles $v$'s contribution). Combined: $\sqrt{(2\%)^2 + (6\%)^2} \approx 6.3\%$. With $K = 250\ \text{J}$, the propagated error is about $\pm 16\ \text{J}$.

The key insight: variables that appear with higher powers—or that already carry large relative uncertainties—dominate the final error budget. Identifying that dominant term tells you exactly where to invest effort in taking more careful measurements.
