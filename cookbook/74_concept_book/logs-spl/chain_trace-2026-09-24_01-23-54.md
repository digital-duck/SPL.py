## Parent Structure

Imagine you find a mysterious number: 7. Is it a whole number? A fraction? Part of an equation with letters in it? You can't tell just by looking — you need to know where it *lives*.

In Sage, every single object has a home address called its **parent**. The number 7 might live in $\mathbb{Z}$ (the integers), or in $\mathbb{Q}$ (the fractions), or even in a strange finite world like $GF(7)$, where numbers wrap around like a clock. Same digit, different neighborhoods, different rules!

Why does this matter? Because the neighborhood decides what math is even allowed. In $\mathbb{Z}$, dividing 7 by 2 doesn't give a whole number back — it breaks the rules. In $\mathbb{Q}$, it's perfectly fine.

To find any object's address, just ask Sage:

$$x.\texttt{parent}() \;\longrightarrow\; \text{the mathematical world } x \text{ belongs to}$$

**Now you try:** Type `7.parent()` and `(7/2).parent()` in Sage. Do you get the same neighborhood both times?

### symbolic_variable (7.9s)

## Symbolic Variable

Imagine a mystery box. You don't know what's inside — could be $3$, could be $100$, could be $-7$. You can't add "the box" to $5$ and get one fixed number, because the box isn't holding still. But you *can* still reason about it: "the box plus five" is a perfectly good thought, even before you peek inside.

That mystery box is what a symbol like $x$ does in math. Writing `var('x')` doesn't create *a* number — it creates a placeholder that stands for *every possible number at once*. This is the floor of generality: instead of asking "what is $3+5$?" we ask "what is $x+5$?" and the answer, $x+5$, works no matter what $x$ turns out to be.

This is why algebra comes before arithmetic in power, even if it comes after in school. Arithmetic answers one question at a time. Algebra answers all of them simultaneously, by naming the unknown instead of guessing it.

$$x + 5$$

**Now you try:** Pick your own mystery box, call it $n$. Write down "$n$ plus $n$" — you've just built a rule that works for every number in the universe, without ever opening the box.

### symbolic_expression (6.6s)

## Symbolic Expression

Imagine you write $x + 2$ on a piece of paper, but $x$ is a mystery number — nobody has said what it is yet. In SageMath's Symbolic Ring, $SR$, that's exactly what a **symbolic expression** is: a little frozen sentence built out of symbols and operations, kept exactly as written until *you* decide to do something with it.

Why does this feel right? Because it's how algebra actually works in your head — you don't need to know $x$ to know that $x + x = 2x$, or that $(x+1)^2$ "wants to become" $x^2 + 2x + 1$. The computer holds that sentence the same patient way, refusing to guess a value too early.

Formally, an expression in $SR$ is a tree: symbols like $x, y$ are leaves, and operations like $+, \times, \sin(\cdot)$ are branches combining them:

$$e = \sin(x) + x^2 \cdot y$$

Nothing here is a number — it's a *structure*, exact and symbolic, until you substitute values or ask Sage to simplify it.

**Now you try:** type `x + x` in Sage. Does it say $2x$, or does it wait for you to ask?

### exact_rational (7.8s)

## Exact Rational

Cut a pizza into 3 equal slices and eat one. You ate exactly $\frac{1}{3}$ of the pizza — not 0.333, not "about a third," but *exactly* one part out of three. If you tried to write that as a decimal, you'd be writing 3's forever: $0.3333\ldots$ The decimal never finishes the job. But $\frac{1}{3}$ finishes instantly — it's already the whole truth, frozen as a fraction.

That's the idea behind an **exact rational**: a number kept as a pair, $\frac{a}{b}$ (a numerator and a denominator, with $b \neq 0$), rather than converted into a decimal. It never rounds, never approximates, never loses a digit. Two rationals like $\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{2}{6}$ are secretly the same number — they represent equal division, just sliced differently. Decimals only show up when *you* ask "what does this look like written out?" — and even then, it's just a picture of the fraction, not a replacement for it.

**Now you try:** Take $\frac{2}{7}$. Don't convert it to a decimal. What is $\frac{2}{7} + \frac{3}{7}$, kept exact?

### simplification (8.5s)

## Simplification

Imagine you write "one half plus one half" on a scrap of paper, and your friend writes "one." You both wrote the *same* number — just dressed differently. Math expressions are like that too: $\frac{2x}{2}$ and $x$ are twins wearing different clothes.

A computer that manipulates symbols faces this constantly: is $\sin^2(x) + \cos^2(x)$ secretly the number $1$? Is some tangled expression secretly zero? These aren't always obvious — sometimes the disguise is very good!

`simplify_full()` is the tool that strips away disguises, rewriting an expression into its plainest form, often using known patterns called *trig identities* — trusted rules like $\sin^2(x)+\cos^2(x)=1$ that always hold.

There's a subtlety worth noticing: writing `expr == 0` doesn't check anything — it just builds a *question* (an equation object) waiting to be answered. The tool `is_zero()` is what actually answers it, using simplification behind the scenes.

$$\sin^2(x) + \cos^2(x) - 1 \;\xrightarrow{\text{simplify}}\; 0$$

**Now you try:** Take $\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$. What simpler expression is it secretly equal to?

### calculus_sage (8.1s)

## Calculus Sage

Imagine you have a magic ruler for slopes. You draw the wiggliest curve, and instead of guessing, the ruler tells you the exact steepness at every single point — instantly, with a formula, not just a number.

That is what a symbolic calculus engine does. "Symbolic" means it works with the *expression itself* — $x^2$, $\sin(x)$, $e^x$ — not a graph or a table of numbers. It knows the rules (like "the slope-finder of $x^2$ is $2x$") and applies them exactly, the way you'd do algebra on paper, but faster and without mistakes.

The core moves are: differentiating (finding steepness, $\frac{d}{dx}$), integrating (finding accumulated area, $\int f(x)\,dx$), taking a limit (what a function approaches), and summing a series. The engine has a built-in honesty check called the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int f(x)\,dx = f(x)$$

Every time it finds an antiderivative, it differentiates its own answer to confirm it lands back on the original function — proof, not trust.

**Now you try:** Pick $f(x) = x^3$. Guess an antiderivative, then differentiate your guess. Does it return to $x^3$?

### exact_linear_algebra (8.5s)

## Exact Linear Algebra

Picture a recipe that calls for exactly $\frac{1}{3}$ cup of sugar. If your measuring cup only shows decimals, you'd pour $0.333...$ and never quite finish the job — always a tiny bit missing. Numerical linear algebra, run on decimals, has this same worry: round off enough times and small errors sneak in, until you're not sure if your answer is really zero or just very close to it.

Exact linear algebra refuses to guess. When we compute over the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ — fractions of whole numbers — every step stays perfectly exact, no leftover crumbs. Row-reduce a matrix to its reduced row echelon form, and the pivots are exact fractions. Ask for a kernel (the vectors a matrix sends to zero), and you get an exact answer, not "basically zero." Ask for eigenvalues, and you can get exact algebraic numbers, like $\sqrt{2}$, rather than $1.41421356...$

The gain: no "condition number" anxiety — no worrying whether your answer is trustworthy or corrupted by rounding.

**Now you try:** take the fraction $\frac{1}{7}$. Add it to itself seven times by hand, exactly. Do you get exactly $1$? A calculator using decimals might not!

### manifolds_geometry (7.4s)

## Manifolds Geometry

Imagine an ant living on the surface of a giant beach ball. To the ant, the ground looks flat — it can walk forward, turn, measure small squares. But if it walks far enough in a "straight line," it ends up back where it started! The ant's world is curved, even though up close it feels flat.

That's the big idea of a manifold: something that looks flat if you zoom in close enough (a "chart," like a little flat map of the ant's neighborhood), but can be curved or twisted globally. A metric tells you how to measure distances on this surface, and curvature tells you how much the surface bends away from flat.

The exciting part: computers can now carry out this geometry *exactly*, symbol by symbol, using tools like SageManifolds. You define charts and formulas for distance, and the software computes curvature precisely — no rounding, no guessing.

$$ds^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta \, d\phi^2$$

Now you try: pick two cities on a globe. Is the shortest path between them a straight line on a flat map, or a curve? Why?

### general_relativity (7.1s)

## General Relativity

Imagine standing on a giant trampoline while a bowling ball rests in the middle. Roll a marble nearby, and it doesn't go straight — it curves toward the ball, following the dip in the fabric. Einstein's big idea was that massive objects like stars and planets do something similar to space and time itself: they bend it. What we call "gravity" isn't a force pulling things — it's objects following the straightest possible path through curved space-time.

To describe this bending precisely, mathematicians use an object called the **metric tensor**, written $g_{\mu\nu}$, which measures distances in curved space-time. From it, we compute **Christoffel symbols** $\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}$, which tell a moving object how to "steer" as space curves. The full curvature is captured by the **Riemann tensor**, and Einstein's field equations,

$$
G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}
$$

link curvature ($G_{\mu\nu}$) directly to matter and energy ($T_{\mu\nu}$).

**Now you try:** Picture the trampoline again — a heavier ball makes a deeper dip. What happens to the marble's path if you make the ball heavier?
