## Usa 1845 Annexation Texas

**Definition.** The Annexation of Texas refers to the 1845 act by which the United States Congress admitted the Republic of Texas—an independent nation since its 1836 secession from Mexico—as the 28th U.S. state. The decision was politically explosive for two intertwined reasons. First, Mexico had never recognized Texan independence and warned that annexation would be treated as an act of war. Second, Texas permitted slavery, and its admission threatened to upset the fragile balance between free and slave states in Congress that had been maintained since the Missouri Compromise of 1820. Annexation was accomplished not by treaty (which required a two-thirds Senate vote and had failed in 1844) but by a joint resolution of Congress, requiring only simple majorities in both chambers—a procedural workaround that itself became a precedent for later territorial acquisitions, including Hawaii in 1898.

**Worked example: reading the border dispute as a decision problem.** Consider the annexation not just as a historical event but as a strategic choice with a disputed parameter: where was the Texas–Mexico border? The Republic of Texas claimed the Rio Grande as its southern boundary; Mexico insisted the border was the Nueces River, roughly 150 miles farther north. This was not a minor cartographic quibble—the disputed strip contained land and pride, and each government had incentive to assert the claim that maximized its territory. When the U.S. annexed Texas in 1845, it inherited the Rio Grande claim, and President Polk deployed troops into the disputed zone in early 1846. Mexico interpreted this as an invasion of its own territory; the U.S. interpreted a subsequent skirmish as an attack on U.S. soil. This is a classic case of two parties each acting rationally within their own boundary definition, producing a conflict (the Mexican-American War) that neither side needed to "want" for it to become nearly inevitable once the ambiguous border went unresolved by treaty.

**Problem-solving application.** When analyzing any territorial annexation, ask three diagnostic questions: (1) Does a third party (here, Mexico) dispute the sovereignty being transferred? (2) Does the annexation shift an internal political balance (here, free vs. slave states)? (3) What procedural mechanism was used, and did it bypass a higher bar (treaty ratification) for a lower one (joint resolution)? Applying this framework to Texas predicts exactly what followed: a war with Mexico (1846–48) and an intensified sectional crisis over slavery's expansion, culminating in the Wilmot Proviso debates and, eventually, the Compromise of 1850.
