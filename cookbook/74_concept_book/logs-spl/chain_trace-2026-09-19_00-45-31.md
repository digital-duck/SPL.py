## Electrical Potential

Every membrane in a living cell is a tiny battery. On one side sits a solution rich in some ion — say, potassium — and on the other, a solution poor in it. Left alone, those ions would diffuse down their concentration gradient, spreading out until evenly mixed. But ions carry charge, and a membrane restricts their movement to specific channels. As positive potassium ions leak out through open channels, they leave behind unbalanced negative charge inside the cell, and the resulting electrostatic pull drags the escaping ions back. The system settles into a standoff: chemical diffusion pushing ions one way, electrostatic attraction pulling them the other. The "pressure" stored in that standoff — the work required to move a unit of charge from one side of the membrane to the other — is the electrical potential, measured in volts (joules per coulomb).

This potential is not abstract; it is directly measurable and directly consequential. A resting neuron holds its interior at about $-70\ \text{mV}$ relative to the outside, meaning roughly 70 millivolts of pent-up driving force sit ready to move charge the instant a channel opens. That voltage is set by the balance of ion gradients, described quantitatively by the Nernst equation:

$$E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{outside}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{inside}}}\right)$$

Here $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is temperature in kelvin, $z$ is the ion's charge, $F$ is Faraday's constant, and the bracketed terms are concentrations on each side. This equation is unavoidable, not decorative: it is the only way to predict the equilibrium voltage for a given ion gradient, since it captures the exact point where the electrical force balances the diffusional one.

Problem-solving application: suppose extracellular potassium concentration is 5 mM and intracellular is 140 mM. At body temperature ($T \approx 310\ \text{K}$), with $z = +1$, plugging into the Nernst equation yields $E_K \approx -91\ \text{mV}$ — close to a real neuron's resting potential, confirming potassium is the dominant driver at rest. The same equation, applied to any other permeant ion, gives that ion's own equilibrium voltage from its own inside/outside concentration ratio — a single calculation you repeat, plugging in different concentrations and charges, to characterize any ion gradient across any membrane. Computing $E_{\text{ion}}$ this way is the standard first step in modeling any excitable-cell circuit.
