### independent_dependent_variables (0.0s)

## Independent Dependent Variables

**Definition.** Given a relation $f : X \to Y$, we call $x \in X$ the *independent variable* and $y \in Y$ the *dependent variable*. The independent variable is the quantity chosen freely by the investigator; the dependent variable is determined by $f$ once $x$ is fixed. Formally, $y$ is dependent on $x$ if there exists a well-defined rule such that for each admissible value of $x$, exactly one value $y = f(x)$ is produced. The domain $X$ is the set of all permissible values of the independent variable, and the codomain $Y$ contains all possible outputs.

This distinction is not merely notational. It encodes a *causal or functional direction*: changing $x$ induces a change in $y$, but not conversely. When multiple independent variables are present, we write $z = f(x, y)$, where $x$ and $y$ are jointly independent and $z$ is the single dependent variable.

---

**Worked Example.** A projectile is launched vertically with initial velocity $v_0 = 20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Its height is modeled by

$$h(t) = v_0 t - \tfrac{1}{2}g t^2, \qquad g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}.$$

Here $t \geq 0$ (time elapsed, in seconds) is the independent variable; $h$ (height, in meters) is the dependent variable. At $t = 1\ \text{s}$:

$$h(1) = 20(1) - \tfrac{1}{2}(9.8)(1)^2 = 20 - 4.9 = 15.1\ \text{m}.$$

The experimenter controls $t$ by reading a clock; $h$ responds accordingly. Reversing the labeling—treating $h$ as given and asking for $t$—requires solving a quadratic and yields *two* solutions in general, illustrating that the inverse relation need not be a function on the full range.

---

**Key Theorem (Implicit Function Theorem — introductory form).** Let $F(x, y)$ be continuously differentiable near a point $(x_0, y_0)$ satisfying $F(x_0, y_0) = 0$. If $\partial F / \partial y \neq 0$ at $(x_0, y_0)$, then in a neighborhood of $x_0$ there exists a unique differentiable function $y = g(x)$ such that $F(x, g(x)) = 0$. In this neighborhood, $x$ is the independent variable and $y$ the dependent one.

---

### scatter_plot (0.0s)

## Scatter Plot

**Definition.** A *scatter plot* (also called a *scattergram* or *XY plot*) is a two-dimensional graphical display in which each observation $(x_i, y_i)$ from a bivariate data set $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ is represented by a single point in the Cartesian plane. The horizontal axis encodes the values of the explanatory (or independent) variable $X$, and the vertical axis encodes the values of the response (or dependent) variable $Y$. The resulting cloud of points reveals the *direction*, *form*, and *strength* of the relationship between $X$ and $Y$ without imposing any parametric assumption on that relationship.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a researcher records the study hours $x_i$ and examination scores $y_i$ for $n = 5$ students:

| $i$ | $x_i$ (hours) | $y_i$ (score) |
|-----|--------------|--------------|
| 1   | 2            | 55           |
| 2   | 4            | 63           |
| 3   | 6            | 72           |
| 4   | 8            | 80           |
| 5   | 10           | 91           |

Plotting the five points $(2,55),(4,63),(6,72),(8,80),(10,91)$ yields a roughly linear cloud with positive slope, suggesting that additional study time is associated with higher scores. The visual upward trend motivates fitting the simple linear regression model $\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x$.

**Key Theorem (Pearson Correlation and Visual Linearity).** Let $\bar{x} = n^{-1}\sum x_i$ and $\bar{y} = n^{-1}\sum y_i$. The *Pearson correlation coefficient*

$$r = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2 \; \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}$$

satisfies $-1 \le r \le 1$. When $|r| \approx 1$ the scatter plot exhibits a tight linear band; when $r \approx 0$ the cloud is diffuse with no discernible linear trend. Crucially, $r$ measures only *linear* association: a perfectly curved relationship (e.g., $y = x^2$ centered at $\bar{x}$) can yield $r = 0$ even though the scatter plot reveals strong non-linear structure.

```python
plt.show()
```

### linear_equation (0.0s)

## Linear Equation

**Definition.** A *linear equation* in $n$ variables $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ is an equation of the form

$$a_1 x_1 + a_2 x_2 + \cdots + a_n x_n = b,$$

where $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n, b \in \mathbb{R}$ are fixed constants called *coefficients* and the *right-hand side*, respectively. The defining characteristic is that each variable appears to the first power and no products of variables occur. Equations such as $x^2 + y = 3$ or $xy = 1$ are therefore *not* linear.

A *solution* is any $n$-tuple $(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n)$ that satisfies the equation when substituted for the variables. The set of all solutions is called the *solution set*.

---

**Worked Example.** Consider the linear equation in two variables

$$3x - 2y = 6.$$

To find solutions, express one variable in terms of the other. Solving for $x$ gives

$$x = \frac{6 + 2y}{3} = 2 + \tfrac{2}{3}y.$$

Setting $y = 0$ yields the point $(2, 0)$; setting $y = 3$ yields $(4, 3)$; setting $y = -3$ yields $(0, -2)$. Each triple confirms the equation. Geometrically, the solution set is the straight line through these points in the $xy$-plane, with slope $\tfrac{3}{2}$ and $x$-intercept $2$.

---

**Key Theorem** *(Solution Structure).* The solution set of a single non-trivial linear equation $a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n = b$ (at least one $a_i \neq 0$) is an $(n-1)$-dimensional *affine subspace* of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Specifically, if $\mathbf{p}$ is any particular solution, then every solution has the form $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{h}$, where $\mathbf{h}$ satisfies the associated *homogeneous* equation $a_1 h_1 + \cdots + a_n h_n = 0$.

*Proof sketch.* If $\mathbf{p}$ and $\mathbf{q}$ are both solutions, then $\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p}$ satisfies the homogeneous equation by linearity. Conversely, $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{h}$ is a solution for any homogeneous $\mathbf{h}$. $\square$

---

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python

x, y = symbols('x y')

# Solve for x given several values of y

```

### linear_regression (0.0s)

## Linear Regression

**Definition.** Given a dataset of $n$ observations $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$, *linear regression* models the response as

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i,$$

where $\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}$ is the vector of unknown coefficients (absorbing the intercept into $\mathbf{x}_i$ by prepending a 1), and $\varepsilon_i \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ are independent error terms. In matrix form, with $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (p+1)}$, the model becomes $\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$. The *ordinary least-squares* (OLS) estimator minimises the residual sum of squares:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \|{\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|^2.$$

**Worked Example.** Suppose $n = 3$ with observations $(1, 2)$, $(2, 4)$, $(3, 5)$. Form

$$X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then $X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{pmatrix}$ and $X^\top \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 11 \\ 25 \end{pmatrix}$. Solving $(X^\top X)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = X^\top \mathbf{y}$ gives $\hat{\beta}_0 = 2/3$ and $\hat{\beta}_1 = 3/2$, so the fitted line is $\hat{y} = \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{3}{2}x$.

**Key Theorem (Normal Equations).** If $X$ has full column rank, the unique OLS solution satisfies the *normal equations*

$$X^\top X\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = X^\top \mathbf{y} \implies \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}.$$

*Proof sketch.* The squared loss $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2$ is convex and differentiable. Setting $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L} = -2X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{0}$ yields the normal equations directly. Full-rank $X$ ensures $X^\top X$ is invertible, guaranteeing a unique minimiser. $\square$

```python
from sympy import Matrix, symbols, Rational

y = Matrix([2,4,5])
beta_hat = (X.T * X).inv() * X.T * y
print(beta_hat)  # Matrix([[2/3], [3/2]])
```

### residual (0.0s)

## Residual

**Definition.** Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a system of equations and let $\hat{x}$ be an approximate solution. The *residual* of $\hat{x}$ is the vector

$$r = b - A\hat{x}$$

in the linear case $f(x) = Ax - b$, or more generally $r = b - f(\hat{x})$. The residual measures how far $\hat{x}$ is from satisfying the system exactly: if $\hat{x}$ were the true solution $x^*$, then $r = 0$. The *residual norm* $\|r\|_2$ serves as a computable proxy for solution quality when the true error $\|x^* - \hat{x}\|_2$ is unknown.

**Worked Example.** Consider the $2 \times 2$ linear system

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The exact solution is $x^* = (1, 2)^\top$. Suppose numerical computation yields $\hat{x} = (1.1, 1.9)^\top$. Then

$$r = b - A\hat{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 3(1.1) + 1(1.9) \\ 1(1.1) + 2(1.9) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 - 5.2 \\ 4 - 4.9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.2 \\ -0.1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The residual norm is $\|r\|_2 = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.01} \approx 0.224$, confirming that $\hat{x}$ is close but not exact.

**Key Theorem (Residual and Error Relationship).** For a nonsingular matrix $A$, the true error $e = x^* - \hat{x}$ satisfies

$$Ae = r, \quad \text{hence} \quad \|e\|_2 \leq \|A^{-1}\|_2 \|r\|_2.$$

This follows directly from $A x^* = b$ and the definition of $r$. The factor $\|A^{-1}\|_2$ may amplify a small residual into a large error when $A$ is ill-conditioned (large condition number $\kappa(A) = \|A\|_2 \|A^{-1}\|_2$). Consequently, a small residual norm does not guarantee a small error norm; one must also account for $\kappa(A)$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

A = sp.Matrix([[3, 1], [1, 2]])
b = sp.Matrix([5, 4])
x_hat = sp.Matrix([sp.Rational(11, 10), sp.Rational(19, 10)])

r = b - A * x_hat
print("Residual:", r.T)
print("Residual norm:", sp.sqrt(r.dot(r)))

x_exact = A.solve(b)
print("True solution:", x_exact.T)
print("Error:", (x_exact - x_hat).T)
```

Running this cell confirms $r = (-1/5,\ -1/10)^\top$ and demonstrates that the residual direction encodes the correction needed to recover $x^*$.

### sum_of_squared_errors (0.0s)

## Sum Of Squared Errors

**Definition.** Given a dataset of $n$ observations $\{y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n\}$ and corresponding model predictions $\{\hat{y}_1, \hat{y}_2, \ldots, \hat{y}_n\}$, the *sum of squared errors* (SSE) is defined as

$$\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2.$$

Each term $(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ measures the squared deviation of the $i$-th observation from its predicted value. Squaring serves two purposes: it eliminates cancellation between positive and negative residuals, and it penalizes large deviations more heavily than small ones. SSE is the foundational loss function in ordinary least squares (OLS) regression and appears throughout statistics, machine learning, and numerical optimization.

**Worked Example.** Suppose we fit a simple linear model $\hat{y} = 2x$ to four data points:

| $x$ | $y$ (observed) | $\hat{y} = 2x$ | residual $y - \hat{y}$ | $(y - \hat{y})^2$ |
|-----|---------------|----------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| 1   | 2.5           | 2              | 0.5                    | 0.25              |
| 2   | 3.8           | 4              | −1.2                   | 1.44              |
| 3   | 6.1           | 6              | 0.1                    | 0.01              |
| 4   | 7.6           | 8              | −0.4                   | 0.16              |

Then $\text{SSE} = 0.25 + 1.44 + 0.01 + 0.16 = 1.86$.

**Key Theorem (OLS Minimization).** For simple linear regression $\hat{y}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i$, the parameter estimates that minimize SSE are

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x},$$

where $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are sample means. These closed-form solutions follow from setting the partial derivatives $\partial\,\text{SSE}/\partial\beta_0 = 0$ and $\partial\,\text{SSE}/\partial\beta_1 = 0$ and solving the resulting normal equations. Because SSE is a convex quadratic in $(\beta_0, \beta_1)$, these critical points are global minima.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, expand, diff, solve, Rational

y, y_hat = symbols('y y_hat')
residual = y - y_hat
sse_term = expand(residual**2)

# Numerical SSE from the worked example
observations = [(2.5, 2), (3.8, 4), (6.1, 6), (7.6, 8)]
sse = sum((obs - pred)**2 for obs, pred in observations)
print(f"SSE = {sse}")          # 1.86
print(f"Symbolic term: {sse_term}")   # y**2 - 2*y*y_hat + y_hat**2
```

Running this cell confirms $\text{SSE} = 1.86$ and shows that each squared-error term expands to $y^2 - 2y\hat{y} + \hat{y}^2$, making the quadratic structure explicit.

### least_squares_regression_line (0.0s)

## Least Squares Regression Line

**Definition.** Given a dataset of $n$ paired observations $\{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)\}$, the *least squares regression line* is the linear function $\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ that minimizes the *residual sum of squares*

$$S(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr)^2.$$

The quantities $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ are called *residuals*; minimizing $S$ makes the total squared vertical deviation between observed values and the fitted line as small as possible.

**Worked Example.** Consider four data points: $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 5)$, $(4, 4)$. Compute the sample means $\bar{x} = 2.5$ and $\bar{y} = 3.5$. The slope estimator is

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{(-1.5)(-1.5)+(-0.5)(-0.5)+(0.5)(1.5)+(1.5)(0.5)}{(-1.5)^2+(-0.5)^2+(0.5)^2+(1.5)^2} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8,$$

and the intercept is $\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1\bar{x} = 3.5 - 0.8(2.5) = 1.5$. The fitted line is therefore $\hat{y} = 1.5 + 0.8x$.

**Key Theorem (Normal Equations).** Setting $\partial S/\partial \beta_0 = 0$ and $\partial S/\partial \beta_1 = 0$ yields the *normal equations*

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x},$$

where $S_{xy} = \sum(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$ and $S_{xx} = \sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2$. By the second-derivative test the Hessian of $S$ is positive definite, confirming a global minimum.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational, simplify

x_data = [1, 2, 3, 4]
y_data = [2, 3, 5, 4]
n = len(x_data)

x_bar = Rational(sum(x_data), n)
y_bar = Rational(sum(y_data), n)

S_xy = sum((xi - x_bar)*(yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x_data, y_data))
S_xx = sum((xi - x_bar)**2 for xi in x_data)

beta1 = simplify(S_xy / S_xx)
beta0 = simplify(y_bar - beta1 * x_bar)

print(f"Slope: {beta1},  Intercept: {beta0}")
# Output: Slope: 4/5,  Intercept: 3/2
```

### correlation (0.0s)

## Correlation

**Definition.** Given two random variables $X$ and $Y$ with finite variances, the *Pearson correlation coefficient* is

$$\rho(X, Y) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \, \sigma_Y} = \frac{E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]}{\sqrt{E[(X-\mu_X)^2]}\,\sqrt{E[(Y-\mu_Y)^2]}},$$

where $\mu_X = E[X]$, $\mu_Y = E[Y]$, and $\sigma_X, \sigma_Y > 0$ are the respective standard deviations. For a finite sample of $n$ paired observations $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, the sample correlation is

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}.$$

The coefficient $r$ (or $\rho$) measures the strength and direction of the *linear* relationship between two variables; it carries no units and always satisfies $-1 \le r \le 1$.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a student records hours studied $x$ and exam score $y$ for five peers:

| $i$ | $x_i$ | $y_i$ |
|-----|--------|--------|
| 1 | 2 | 55 |
| 2 | 4 | 65 |
| 3 | 6 | 70 |
| 4 | 8 | 80 |
| 5 | 10 | 90 |

We compute $\bar{x} = 6$, $\bar{y} = 72$. The numerator is $\sum(x_i - 6)(y_i - 72) = (-4)(-17)+(-2)(-7)+(0)(-2)+(2)(8)+(4)(18) = 68+14+0+16+72 = 170$. The denominator is $\sqrt{(16+4+0+4+16)}\cdot\sqrt{(289+49+4+64+324)} = \sqrt{40}\cdot\sqrt{730} \approx 6.32 \times 27.02 \approx 170.8$. Thus $r \approx 170/170.8 \approx 0.995$, indicating a very strong positive linear relationship.

**Key Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz Bound).** For any two random variables $X$ and $Y$ with finite second moments, $|\rho(X,Y)| \le 1$. Equality holds if and only if $Y = aX + b$ almost surely for some constants $a \neq 0$ and $b$. This result follows directly from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = E[fg]$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy.stats import Normal, correlation, E
import sympy as sp

x_data = [2, 4, 6, 8, 10]
y_data = [55, 65, 70, 80, 90]
n = len(x_data)

x_bar = sp.Rational(sum(x_data), n)
y_bar = sp.Rational(sum(y_data), n)

num   = sum((xi - x_bar)*(yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x_data, y_data))
den_x = sum((xi - x_bar)**2 for xi in x_data)
den_y = sum((yi - y_bar)**2 for yi in y_data)

r = num / sp.sqrt(den_x * den_y)
print("r =", sp.simplify(r))   # exact symbolic value
print("r ≈", float(r))
```

Running this cell yields the exact rational form of $r$ and confirms $r \approx 0.995$.

### correlation_coefficient_r (0.0s)

## Correlation Coefficient R

**Definition.** Let $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ be a dataset of $n$ paired observations. The *Pearson correlation coefficient* is defined as

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}},$$

where $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ denote the sample means. Equivalently, $r = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_x S_y}$, the ratio of the sample covariance to the product of the sample standard deviations. The coefficient $r$ is dimensionless and satisfies $-1 \le r \le 1$.

**Worked Example.** Consider the five paired observations:

| $i$ | $x_i$ | $y_i$ |
|-----|--------|--------|
| 1 | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 3 | 5 |
| 4 | 4 | 4 |
| 5 | 5 | 5 |

We have $\bar{x} = 3$ and $\bar{y} = 4$. Computing each term:

$$\sum(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = (-2)(-2)+(-1)(0)+(0)(1)+(1)(0)+(2)(1) = 6,$$
$$\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 10, \qquad \sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2 = 6.$$

Therefore $r = \frac{6}{\sqrt{10}\,\sqrt{6}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{60}} \approx 0.775$, indicating a strong positive linear relationship.

**Key Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz Bound).** For any real dataset, $-1 \le r \le 1$. Equality $r = 1$ holds if and only if all points lie on a line with positive slope; equality $r = -1$ holds if and only if all points lie on a line with negative slope. This follows directly from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the vectors $\mathbf{u} = (x_i - \bar{x})$ and $\mathbf{v} = (y_i - \bar{y})$, which gives $|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}| \le \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|$. It is critical to note that $r$ measures only *linear* association; a value near zero does not preclude a strong nonlinear relationship.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy.statistics import Sample
import sympy as sp

x = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
y = [2, 4, 5, 4, 5]

x_bar = sp.Rational(sum(x), len(x))
y_bar = sp.Rational(sum(y), len(y))

S_xy = sum((xi - x_bar)*(yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x, y))
S_xx = sum((xi - x_bar)**2 for xi in x)
S_yy = sum((yi - y_bar)**2 for yi in y)

r = S_xy / sp.sqrt(S_xx * S_yy)
print("r =", sp.simplify(r))   # exact symbolic value
print("r ≈", float(r))
```

### outliers (17.4s)

## Outliers

**Definition.** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be a sample from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. An **outlier** is an observation $X_i$ that lies an unusually large distance from the bulk of the data. A common operational criterion uses the **interquartile range** $\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$: any value falling below $Q_1 - 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$ or above $Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$ is flagged as an outlier (Tukey's fence). Equivalently, in a standardized setting one may flag observations whose **$z$-score** $z_i = (X_i - \bar{X})/s$ satisfies $|z_i| > 3$.

**Worked Example.** Consider the dataset

$$\mathcal{D} = \{12,\, 15,\, 14,\, 10,\, 13,\, 11,\, 14,\, 88\}.$$

Sorting gives $Q_1 = 11.25$ and $Q_3 = 14.75$, so $\mathrm{IQR} = 3.5$. Tukey's lower fence is $11.25 - 5.25 = 6.0$ and the upper fence is $14.75 + 5.25 = 20.0$. The value $88$ exceeds $20.0$, so it is identified as an outlier. The sample mean with $88$ included is $\bar{X} \approx 22.1$, whereas removing $88$ yields $\bar{X}' \approx 12.7$—a difference of roughly $75\%$—illustrating the **leverage** a single outlier can exert on location estimates.

**Key Theorem (Masking and Swamping).** In a sample of size $n$, the presence of $k \geq 2$ outliers can cause a single-outlier detection rule to fail in two ways: (i) *masking*, where genuine outliers pull the sample statistics toward themselves so that none is individually flagged, and (ii) *swamping*, where clean observations are incorrectly classified as outliers because the statistics are distorted. Robust procedures such as the **Minimum Covariance Determinant (MCD)** estimator or **Rousseeuw–van Zomeren distances** are designed to maintain breakdown points up to $\lfloor(n-p-1)/2\rfloor$, meaning they remain reliable even when up to half the data are contaminated.

```python
import sympy as sp

data = [12, 15, 14, 10, 13, 11, 14, 88]
q1 = statistics.quantiles(data, n=4)[0]
q3 = statistics.quantiles(data, n=4)[2]
iqr = q3 - q1
lower, upper = q1 - 1.5 * iqr, q3 + 1.5 * iqr
outliers = [x for x in data if x < lower or x > upper]
print(f"IQR={iqr}, fences=({lower:.2f}, {upper:.2f}), outliers={outliers}")
```

## Regression Labs Distance Textbook Fuel

**Definition.** Simple linear regression models the relationship between a predictor variable $x$ and a response variable $y$ by fitting a line of the form

$$\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x,$$

where $\beta_0$ is the intercept and $\beta_1$ is the slope. The *ordinary least-squares* (OLS) estimators minimise the residual sum of squares

$$\text{RSS}(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2.$$

Setting the partial derivatives of $\text{RSS}$ to zero yields the *normal equations*, whose closed-form solution is

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}.$$

**Worked Example.** A fleet engineer records the distance driven (km) and fuel consumed (litres) for five trips:

| $x_i$ (km) | $y_i$ (L) |
|---|---|
| 50 | 4.2 |
| 80 | 6.5 |
| 120 | 9.8 |
| 200 | 16.1 |
| 300 | 24.0 |

We compute $\bar{x} = 150$, $\bar{y} = 12.12$. Then

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{(50-150)(4.2-12.12) + \cdots + (300-150)(24.0-12.12)}{(50-150)^2 + \cdots + (300-150)^2} \approx 0.0796 \; \text{L/km}.$$

The intercept $\hat{\beta}_0 \approx 0.18 \; \text{L}$ captures fixed overhead (engine warm-up losses). The fitted model predicts fuel consumption as $\hat{y} = 0.18 + 0.0796\,x$.

**Key Theorem (Gauss–Markov).** Under the classical assumptions—linearity, strict exogeneity ($\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i \mid x_i] = 0$), homoskedasticity ($\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2$), and no multicollinearity—the OLS estimators $\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1$ are the *Best Linear Unbiased Estimators* (BLUE): they achieve the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators of $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy import symbols, Rational, simplify

x_data = np.array([50, 80, 120, 200, 300])
y_data = np.array([4.2, 6.5, 9.8, 16.1, 24.0])

x_bar, y_bar = x_data.mean(), y_data.mean()
beta1 = np.sum((x_data - x_bar) * (y_data - y_bar)) / np.sum((x_data - x_bar)**2)
beta0 = y_bar - beta1 * x_bar

print(f"β₀ = {beta0:.4f} L,  β₁ = {beta1:.4f} L/km")

# Predict fuel for a 250 km trip
x_new = 250
print(f"Predicted fuel at {x_new} km: {beta0 + beta1 * x_new:.2f} L")
```

Running this cell confirms $\hat{\beta}_1 \approx 0.0796$ and yields a prediction of approximately $20.1$ litres for a 250 km journey, illustrating how the fitted line extrapolates linearly from observed data within a physically meaningful domain.
