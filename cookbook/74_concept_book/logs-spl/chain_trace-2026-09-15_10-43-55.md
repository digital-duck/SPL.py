### 木 (7.8s)

## 木

Picture a tree standing alone in a field. A straight trunk rises up, and branches spread out on either side like arms reaching for the sky. That simple shape — trunk plus branches — is exactly what the character 木 draws for you. Look at it: one line standing tall, with strokes branching off left and right. It is a picture of a tree, drawn so simply that you can almost feel the shade it makes.

But here is the surprising part: this little tree-brick gets used in two very different jobs. Sometimes it lends its *meaning* — think of trees, wood, nature. Sometimes it only lends its *sound*, forgetting its meaning entirely and just showing up because it sounds right.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林 \;(\text{forest — meaning})
$$
$$
\underbrace{木}_{sound\ "mu"} + \underbrace{水}_{water} = 沐 \;(\text{to bathe — sound only})
$$

**Now you try:** Can you find another character with 木 hiding inside it? Is it there for its *meaning*, or just for its *sound*?

### 林 (5.3s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing alone. Now imagine a second tree standing right next to it. What do you have? Not just "two trees" — you have the beginning of something new: a little cluster of green, a hint of shade, a place where birds might gather.

That's the idea behind 林 (lín), meaning "woods" or "grove." It's built from two copies of the character for tree, 木, standing side by side like trunks in a forest.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \underbrace{林}_{woods}
$$

This is the simplest trick a character can play: just repeat a piece, and the meaning grows bigger. One tree is a tree. Two trees together become something more — a woods. The character doesn't need a new picture; it just doubles up what it already has.

**Now you try:** If one 木 is a tree, and two 木 make 林 (woods), what do you imagine three trees stacked together might mean? Sketch it and guess before you look it up!

### 森 (7.4s)

## 森

Imagine one tree standing alone. Now imagine two trees together — that's already a little woods, 林. But what if there are so many trees you can't even count them, trees stretching in every direction as far as you can see? That's not a woods anymore. That's a forest — 森.

Here's the trick this character plays: it just keeps stacking the same brick. One tree, 木. Two trees, 林. Three trees, 森. The character doesn't need a brand-new picture for "forest" — it just adds one more tree on top, and suddenly the meaning grows from "tree" to "small woods" to "big, thick forest." More bricks, bigger idea!

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \ (forest)
$$

Notice how 木 → 林 → 森 is like a staircase: each step adds one tree, and each step feels like a bigger, denser place.

**Now you try:** Draw three tiny trees in a triangle shape. That's basically what 森 looks like — trees everywhere you look!
