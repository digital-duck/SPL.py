### prefix (28.9s)

## Prefix

A prefix is the steerer: it attaches before a root or stem and points the stem's meaning in a direction — inward, backward, across, before, or together — without changing what part of speech the word is. Where a suffix often converts a verb into a noun or an adjective into an adverb, a prefix leaves that grammar alone and instead redirects the action or modifies the degree of the base meaning. This is why prefixes are so reliable for vocabulary-building: once you know the direction a prefix points, you can predict the meaning of dozens of unfamiliar words built on different stems, as long as the prefix stays the same.

Consider the prefix *trans-* ("across"). Attach it to different Latin stems and the same directional idea — moving across something — carries over every time:

$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = transport \quad\text{(carry across)}$$
$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{fer}_{bring} = transfer \quad\text{(bring across)}$$
$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{form}_{shape} = transform \quad\text{(shape across, i.e. change shape)}$$
$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{scribe}_{write} = transcribe \quad\text{(write across, i.e. from one form to another)}$$

In every case the word stays a verb — the prefix never touched the part of speech — and *trans-* contributes the same "across" meaning no matter which stem it lands on.

This gives you a problem-solving strategy for decoding unfamiliar words on a test or in a dense reading passage. Suppose you meet the word *transnational* for the first time. Rather than guessing blindly, isolate the prefix first: *trans-* means "across." Even without knowing every nuance of *national*, you can infer the word describes something that crosses national boundaries — and confirm it means "extending across or beyond national borders." The same move works on *transatlantic* (across the Atlantic) and *translucent* (light passing across, i.e. partly transparent): different stems, same prefix, predictably related meanings.

When you're unsure of a word, don't try to memorize it whole. Split it: identify the prefix, name the direction it points, then reason about how that direction modifies the stem's core meaning. This decompositional habit turns an unfamiliar vocabulary word into a solvable puzzle rather than a fact to memorize — and it scales, since the same prefix will keep reappearing on stem after stem as your vocabulary grows.

### stem (23.5s)

## Stem

Every English word carries a semantic core — a piece that tells you, at a glance, what the word is about. This core is the **stem**: the part of a word that supplies its meaning, as opposed to the prefixes and suffixes that adjust that meaning. If a word were a sentence in miniature, the stem would be the subject; everything attached to it is commentary. English inherited its vocabulary from three main sources — Latin, Greek, and Germanic (Old English) roots — and because of this layered history, every English word has at least one identifiable stem, however disguised it may look after centuries of borrowing and spelling change.

Consider the word *transportation*. Strip away the prefix *trans-* ("across") and the suffix *-ation* (turns a verb into a noun), and you are left with *port*, from Latin *portare*, "to carry." That single syllable is doing the semantic heavy lifting — everything else is grammatical scaffolding:

$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} + \underbrace{ation}_{noun-forming} = transportation$$

Once you can isolate *port*, an entire family of words opens up: *import* (carry in), *export* (carry out), *support* (carry under, i.e., hold up), *portable* (able to be carried). None of these need to be memorized separately once the stem is recognized.

This is the practical payoff of stem-hunting: it turns vocabulary acquisition from rote memorization into pattern recognition. When you meet an unfamiliar word, the first move is not to reach for a dictionary but to ask, "What is the stem, and what does it mean?" The skill to practice is stem extraction: peel off recognizable affixes first, then check what remains against a short list of common Latin and Greek roots.

Try it yourself on a new set of words before checking any reference: *dictation*, *predict*, *contradict*, *verdict*, *dictator*. Remove the prefixes and suffixes from each — what single piece keeps showing up, and what might it mean? If your guess is close to "to say" or "to speak," you have just extracted a stem on your own, the same way *port* was extracted above. That is the transferable move: a student who can do this reliably effectively owns a dozen words at once for every one stem learned, because each new word is simply the same core meaning wearing a different prefix or suffix — a strategy far more durable than memorizing whole words in isolation.

### in- (13.7s)

## In-

**Definition.** The prefix *in-* means "in, into, on, or toward." It is one of English's most versatile Latin borrowings, but it also has a second, unrelated job: turning words negative, as in *inactive* or *incomplete*. This section focuses on the directional sense — the one that steers meaning inward. What makes *in-* distinctive is its habit of shape-shifting to match the consonant that follows it, a process called assimilation. Before *m*, *p*, or *b*, it becomes *im-* (as in *import*, *impose*); before *l*, it becomes *il-* (*illuminate*); before *r*, it becomes *ir-* (*irrigate*). The spelling changes, but the inward meaning stays constant.

$$
in\text{-} \xrightarrow{+p,b,m} im\text{-} \qquad in\text{-} \xrightarrow{+l} il\text{-} \qquad in\text{-} \xrightarrow{+r} ir\text{-}
$$

**Worked example.** Take *import*: $\underbrace{im\text{-}}_{into} + \underbrace{port}_{to\ carry} = import$, literally "to carry into" a country. Compare *illuminate*: $\underbrace{il\text{-}}_{into} + \underbrace{lumin}_{light} + \underbrace{ate}_{verb\ suffix} = illuminate$, "to bring light into" something. In both cases, the root itself never changes — only the prefix's final consonant bends to match it.

**Problem-solving application.** When you meet an unfamiliar word beginning with *im-*, *il-*, or *ir-*, the fastest way to unlock its meaning is to mentally restore the base form *in-* and ask what root it's attached to. Given *irradiate*, strip the assimilated prefix back to *in-* and identify the root *radi-* ("ray, beam") — the word means "to send rays into" something, hence exposure to radiation. Try the same move on *implant* (root *plant-*, "to set into") and *illustrate* (root *lustr-*, "to shine into," i.e., to make vivid). This restoration strategy — undoing the sound change to recover the true prefix — works because assimilation is entirely predictable: it never invents new meanings, it only smooths pronunciation. Once you can reverse it automatically, a whole family of *im-/il-/ir-* words becomes as transparent as if they'd kept their original spelling.

### port (11.7s)

## Port

The Latin verb *portare*, "to carry," gives English one of its most productive roots. Anything that moves — physically or figuratively — from one place to another tends to carry this root somewhere in its name.

$$
\underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} + \underbrace{-able}_{\text{capable of being}} = \text{portable}
$$

*Portable* names something capable of being carried — a laptop, a generator, a skill set. The same stem builds *transport* (carry across), *export* (carry out), *import* (carry in), *support* (carry from underneath, hence to hold up), and *report* (carry back, hence to bring back information).

**Worked example.** Consider *transportation*, *deportation*, and *portfolio*. Each uses the stem differently, and the prefix or ending tells you exactly what kind of carrying is happening.

$$
\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} + \underbrace{ation}_{\text{the act of}} = \text{transportation, the act of carrying across (distance)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{de}_{\text{away from}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} + \underbrace{ation}_{\text{the act of}} = \text{deportation, the act of carrying (someone) away from a country}
$$

A *portfolio* is literally a case for carrying loose sheets (*folio* = leaf/sheet) — which is why the word now also means a collection of an artist's work or a set of financial investments: the carrying case gave its name to what it carries.

**Problem-solving application.** When you meet an unfamiliar word with *port* buried inside it, isolate the prefix first, then ask "carry what, in which direction?" A computer *port* is an opening through which data is "carried" in or out of a device — the same logic as a harbor *port*, where ships carry goods in or out of a country. This is also why software gets *ported* to a new platform: developers carry the code from one system to another. Try it on *comport* (*com-* = together + *port* = carry → to carry oneself, i.e., behave) or *purport* (*pur-* from *pro-*, forward + *port* → to carry forward as claimed, i.e., to profess or claim). In each case, decomposing the word into stem and affix turns a guess into a derivation.

### con- (12.9s)

## Con-

**Con-** is a prefix meaning "together" or "with." Attached to a root, it turns an individual action or state into a shared, joint one — whatever the root does alone, *con-* makes it done in company. The prefix comes from Latin *cum* ("with"), and English absorbed it through centuries of borrowed Latin vocabulary, which is why it shows up constantly in formal and academic words rather than everyday Anglo-Saxon ones.

$$
\underbrace{con}_{together} + \underbrace{struct}_{build} = construct
$$

To construct is not simply to build — it is to build by putting pieces together, assembling parts into a whole. Compare this with a root that has no *con-*: *struct* alone doesn't function as an English word, but related forms like "structure" still carry the sense of parts arranged into a whole, confirming that *con-* is doing real work of "combination" rather than just decoration.

The prefix is remarkably stable in meaning across very different roots, which makes it a reliable tool for decoding unfamiliar words:

$$
\underbrace{con}_{together} + \underbrace{verse}_{turn} = converse
$$

$$
\underbrace{con}_{together} + \underbrace{sequence}_{following} = consequence
$$

In *converse*, two people "turn" toward each other in exchange — talking is a turning-together. In *consequence*, events "follow together," one bound to the next, which is why a consequence is never isolated but always tied to a cause.

**Problem-solving application:** When you meet an unfamiliar word beginning with *con-* (or its spelling variants *co-*, *col-*, *com-*, *cor-* — the prefix changes its final letter to match the sound that follows, as in *collect*, *compose*, *correlate*), isolate the root and ask what the root means alone, then add "together" or "jointly" to that meaning. Try it with *concur*: the root *cur* relates to "running" (as in *current*), so *concur* is literally "to run together" — which is exactly why it means to agree or coincide with someone else's opinion. This decomposition strategy works because Latin-derived prefixes like *con-* keep a consistent semantic contribution across hundreds of English words, even when the root itself is rare or archaic outside that combination.

### dict (11.9s)

## Dict

The English root *dict* comes from Latin *dicere*, "to say." Any word built on this root has something to do with speaking, stating, or authoritatively laying down words — from ordinary conversation to formal decrees. Because *dicere* sat at the center of Roman public life (edicts were read aloud, judges pronounced verdicts, orators were prized for what they said and how), the root spread into a wide family of English words covering speech, judgment, and the recording of language itself.

**Word formation**

The root combines with prefixes that specify *how* or *in what direction* the saying happens:

$$
\underbrace{pre}_{\text{before}} + \underbrace{dict}_{\text{to say}} = \text{predict (to say beforehand)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{contra}_{\text{against}} + \underbrace{dict}_{\text{to say}} = \text{contradict (to say against)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{ver}_{\text{truth}} + \underbrace{dict}_{\text{to say}} = \text{verdict (a true saying, a judgment)}
$$

Notice the pattern: the prefix supplies the *direction* or *quality* of the speech act, while *dict* supplies the core meaning of "declaring something in words."

**Worked example**

Take the word *dictionary*. Strip the noun-forming suffix *-ary* ("a place or collection of") and you are left with *diction*, itself built from *dict* + the noun suffix *-ion* ("act of"). So a dictionary is literally "a collection of the act of saying" — a book that records what words mean and how they are used. This is why *diction* (a person's choice and delivery of words) and *dictionary* (a reference for words) share a root even though one describes a skill and the other an object: both trace back to acts of speaking.

**Problem-solving application**

When you meet an unfamiliar word containing *dict*, isolate the prefix and ask what direction of "saying" it implies. *Dictate* (say directly, to be written down) and *edict* (say out, i.e., a proclamation issued publicly) both fit this test. This root-plus-prefix strategy lets you infer meaning on encountering *jurisdiction* (the authority to "say the law," *jus* + *dict*) or *indict* (to formally "say against," bringing a legal charge) even before checking a dictionary — itself, fittingly, a book named after the root you are using to decode it.

### ject (11.5s)

## Ject

The Latin root *ject* comes from *iacere*, "to throw." It appears at the end of many English words and always carries a sense of motion — something being thrown, cast, or forced in a particular direction. Because Latin verbs often took a preposition to specify *which way* the throwing happened, *ject* rarely stands alone. Instead, it pairs with prefixes like *pro-* (forward), *re-* (back), *in-* (into), *e-/ex-* (out), *sub-* (under), and *ob-* (against), each one steering the meaning of the throw.

$$
\underbrace{pro}_{forward} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = \text{project (to throw forward, as in a plan cast ahead in time)}
$$

Once you see *ject* as "throw," a whole family of words becomes transparent. *Reject* is to throw something back at the person who offered it. *Inject* is to throw a substance into something, as a nurse injects medicine into the body. *Eject* is to throw out, as a pilot ejects from a failing aircraft. *Subject* is to throw someone *under* — under a ruler's authority, or under a topic being discussed. *Object* (from *ob-*, against) is to throw something *against* — an argument thrown in opposition, or a physical thing that stands in your way.

Try applying the pattern yourself. The word *dejected* uses *de-* (down). Before checking a dictionary, predict the meaning: "thrown down" suggests a downcast, low spirit — and indeed, *dejected* means sad or discouraged, as if one's mood had been thrown down to the ground. Similarly, *trajectory* combines *trans-* (across) with *ject*, giving "thrown across" — the path an object follows as it moves through space, like the trajectory of a thrown ball.

This decode-the-prefix strategy is especially useful on standardized vocabulary tests, where an unfamiliar word like *interject* (to throw *between*, i.e., to interrupt a conversation) or *conjecture* (to throw *together*, i.e., to guess by putting ideas together) can often be solved on the spot rather than memorized in advance. Once you recognize *ject* as "throw," the prefix does the rest of the work — turning an unfamiliar word into a small, solvable puzzle of direction and motion.

### pre- (12.9s)

## Pre-

**Pre-** is a prefix that attaches to a root word to shift its action or state earlier in time — placing it before something else happens. As a beforehand steerer, pre- tells the reader: whatever the root describes, it happens in advance, ahead of a deadline, event, or expected sequence.

$$
\underbrace{pre}_{before} + \underbrace{view}_{see/watch} = preview
$$

A *preview* is a look at something before its official release — a film trailer, an advance chapter, a sneak peek at next year's curriculum. The prefix doesn't change what "view" means; it only relocates the action earlier on the timeline. Compare *pay* → *prepay* (paying before the bill is due), *heat* → *preheat* (warming an oven before you cook), and *requisite* → *prerequisite* (something required before a later requirement can be met).

**Worked example.** Take the root *caution*, meaning care or warning. Add pre-, and *precaution* names a measure taken *before* a risk materializes — a safeguard put in place ahead of potential harm, not a reaction to harm already done. This is the same logic as *prevent* (from Latin *venire*, "to come" — literally "to come before") and *predict* (from *dicere*, "to say" — "to say before it happens"). In each case, the timing shift is the entire meaning of the prefix; the root supplies the action, and pre- supplies the "when."

**Problem-solving application.** When you meet an unfamiliar word built with pre-, isolate the root first, translate it on its own, then apply the beforehand shift. Given *precede* (root *cede*, "to go/yield"), you can infer "to go before" without memorizing the word as a whole unit — useful for distinguishing it from its frequently confused near-homophone *proceed* ("to go forward"), where the prefix *pro-* signals forward motion rather than prior timing. This same strategy scales to technical vocabulary: a *preprocessor* in computing runs *before* the main program compiles, and a *precondition* in logic or programming is a requirement that must hold *before* a function executes. Recognizing pre- as a reliable timing marker — rather than treating each pre-word as a separate vocabulary item — turns prefix knowledge into a transferable tool: once you know the root, you can predict the meaning of the whole word, and often recognize related prefixes like *post-* (after) or *ante-* (before, as in *antebellum*) as timing markers working in the same family, just pointing in different directions along the timeline.

### re- (14.6s)

## Re-

**Definition.** *Re-* is a prefix meaning "again" or "back," attached to a root word to signal repetition of an action or a return to a prior state or location. It comes from Latin *re-*, carrying the same double sense in English: *rewrite* means to write again, while *return* means to turn back. Because *re-* almost never stands alone, every word it forms is a straightforward two-piece composition — the prefix plus a root that already carries independent meaning:

$$
\underbrace{re}_{\text{again/back}} + \underbrace{build}_{\text{construct}} = rebuild \;\; (\text{to construct again})
$$

$$
\underbrace{re}_{\text{again/back}} + \underbrace{call}_{\text{summon}} = recall \;\; (\text{to summon back, or remember})
$$

**Worked example.** Take the sentence: "After the storm damaged the bridge, engineers had to *reconstruct* it before traffic could *resume*." Break each word down: *reconstruct* = *re* ("again") + *construct* ("build"), so engineers built the bridge a second time. *Resume* = *re* ("again") + a root from Latin *sumere* ("to take up"), so traffic "took up again" — started moving once more. Notice that *resume* keeps the "again" sense even though the root *sume* isn't a free-standing English word; this is common with prefixes borrowed from Latin, where the root only survives inside compound words.

**Problem-solving application.** When you meet an unfamiliar word starting with *re-*, use the prefix as a first clue and test both senses — "again" and "back" — against context before settling on a meaning. Given *reimburse*, *retreat*, and *reinforce*: *reimburse* combines *re* + a root related to "purse/pay," yielding "to pay back"; *retreat* combines *re* + *treat* (from Latin "to draw"), yielding "to draw back," i.e., withdraw; *reinforce* combines *re* + *enforce* ("to strengthen"), yielding "to strengthen again," i.e., add extra support. Practice this two-step check — identify the root's core meaning, then apply "again" or "back" — and you can decode dozens of unfamiliar *re-* words on sight, from *reassign* to *revert*, without needing a dictionary each time.

### spect (13.6s)

## Spect

**Spect** comes from the Latin verb *specere*, "to look" or "to see." As a bound root, *spect* never stands alone as an English word; it always attaches to a prefix or suffix, and the resulting words all circle back to some act of seeing, watching, or checking. Once you spot this root, dozens of English words become transparent, because their meanings decompose cleanly into "look" plus a modifying prefix:

$$\underbrace{in}_{\text{into}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{inspect (to look into closely)}$$

$$\underbrace{ex}_{\text{out}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{expect (to look out for something coming)}$$

$$\underbrace{re}_{\text{back}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{respect (to look back at, i.e., regard highly)}$$

**Worked example.** Consider the word *retrospective*, often used to describe an art exhibit surveying an artist's whole career. Break it down: *retro-* ("backward") + *spect* ("look") + *-ive* (adjective-forming suffix) = "characterized by looking backward." A retrospective show literally looks back over decades of work. Compare this to *prospective*, where *pro-* ("forward") replaces *retro-*, giving "looking forward" — the word used for a *prospective* student or employee, someone being looked at, or looking ahead, before a decision is made. The prefix does all the directional work; *spect* supplies the constant meaning of "looking."

**Problem-solving application.** When you meet an unfamiliar word built on *spect*, isolate the prefix first and translate it literally, then attach "look" and test whether the combination makes sense in context. Try this with *circumspect*: *circum-* means "around," so *circumspect* should mean something like "looking around" — and indeed, a circumspect person is cautious, looking around at all sides of a situation before acting. Now try *perspective*: *per-* means "through," giving "a looking-through" — the vantage point or lens through which you view something, exactly matching its everyday use in phrases like "from her perspective." This decompose-and-test method works because *spect* words are compositional: master the root once, and every prefix you already know (*in-, ex-, re-, retro-, pro-, circum-, per-, in-*specific combinations like *inspector* or *spectator*) becomes a tool for decoding new vocabulary without a dictionary.

### struct (13.2s)

## Struct

*Struct* descends from the Latin verb *struere*, "to build," and it functions as the architectural root of English: whenever a word describes something erected, arranged, or organized — a physical building, a social system, or a sentence — *struct* is likely doing the work underneath.

**Worked example.** Consider *construct* and *destroy*'s more precise cousin, *destruct*. The prefix *con-* means "together," so *construct* literally means "to build together" — assembling pieces into a whole. The prefix *de-* means "down" or "apart," so *destruct* means "to unbuild" — pulling a structure apart. The pattern shows up cleanly:

$$
\underbrace{con}_{together} + \underbrace{struct}_{build} = construct
$$

$$
\underbrace{de}_{apart} + \underbrace{struct}_{build} = destruct
$$

Once you see *struct* as "build," a whole family becomes legible without a dictionary: *instruct* (build knowledge *into* someone, *in-* = "into"), *obstruct* (build something *against* a path, *ob-* = "against, in the way"), *infrastructure* (the *building underneath* — the base layer that supports everything else), and *restructure* (to *build again*, *re-* = "again").

**Problem-solving application.** This root's real power is diagnostic: when you meet an unfamiliar word containing *struct*, you can often infer its meaning by identifying the prefix and asking "what is being built, and in what direction?" Try it on *reconstruct*: *re-* ("again") plus *con-* ("together") plus *struct* ("build") yields "to build together again" — matching its actual sense of restoring something to a former state. Try *superstructure*: *super-* ("above") plus *struct* gives "the building above" — the visible part of a structure that rests on a foundation. This same logic extends beyond concrete buildings. A *social structure* is the "built" arrangement of roles and institutions in a society, and a sentence's *grammatical structure* is the "built" arrangement of its words and clauses. In each domain, *struct* signals the same underlying idea: component parts assembled according to a plan into an organized whole. Recognizing the root, then, is not just vocabulary — it is a transferable strategy for decoding new terms across science, sociology, and grammar alike, wherever something has been deliberately built.

### trans- (12.7s)

## Trans-

The prefix *trans-* marks crossing: movement over, across, or beyond a boundary. That boundary can be physical — a border, a river, an ocean — or abstract — a state of being, a category, a mode of existence. Whatever attaches after *trans-* names the boundary or the space being crossed; the prefix itself supplies the act of crossing.

$$\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = transport$$

*Transport* is literally "carry across" — moving people or goods from one place to another. *Transatlantic* is "across the Atlantic," describing a flight or cable that spans the ocean. *Transform* is "across form" — a change so thorough that the thing crosses into a new shape or identity, not merely adjusted but made structurally different. *Translate* is "across" plus a root meaning "carry" (from Latin *latus*, "carried") — taking meaning from one language and carrying it into another. In every case, *trans-* signals that something has moved from one side of a boundary to the other, and the second part of the word tells you what kind of boundary — physical distance, form, language, or category.

This makes *trans-* a useful diagnostic tool when you meet an unfamiliar word. If a word starts with *trans-*, ask two questions: what is the "thing" being carried or changed, and what is the boundary it's crossing? Take *transcend*: the root relates to climbing or scaling (compare *ascend*, *descend*), so *transcend* means to climb across or beyond a limit — to go past an ordinary boundary of experience or capability, as in "her courage transcended fear." Or take *transparent*: the root relates to appearing (compare *appear*, *apparent*), so *transparent* means something you can see across or through — light passes across the boundary of the material.

Compare *trans-* to two prefixes it's often confused with: *inter-* (between, among multiple things — *international* means "between nations") and *trans-* (across, through one boundary to the other side — *transnational* means "operating across national boundaries," not merely between two nations but cutting through the boundary itself). A corporation is *international* if it does business between several countries; it's *transnational* if it operates as though national borders barely apply, crossing through them freely. Recognizing this distinction sharpens your reading of any *trans-* word you meet for the first time.

### construct (12.1s)

## Construct

A **construct** is a whole built up from smaller pieces that were joined together — the term itself demonstrates the pattern it names.

$$\underbrace{con\text{-}}_{\text{together}} + \underbrace{struct}_{\text{build}} = \textit{construct}$$

The Latin prefix *con-* ("with, together") combines with *struct* (from *struere*, "to build, pile up") to give a word that literally means "to build together" or "pile up jointly." This same root *struct* appears across a family of English words, each adding a different prefix to shift the meaning: *instruct* (build knowledge *into* someone), *destruct* (build *apart*, i.e., tear down), *obstruct* (build *against*, blocking a path), and *infrastructure* (the underlying framework built *beneath* a system).

**Worked example.** Take the sentence: *"The committee will construct a new curriculum from the surveyed feedback."* Here, "construct" signals an assembly process — separate pieces (survey responses, learning goals, existing course materials) are gathered and organized into one coherent structure (the curriculum). Compare this to a near-synonym like *"build"*: both imply assembly, but *construct* carries a more deliberate, engineered connotation, often used for abstract or systematic creations (a construct can be a theory, an argument, or a grammatical construction — not just a physical object).

**Problem-solving application.** When you encounter an unfamiliar word containing *struct*, you can decompose it the same way to recover its meaning without a dictionary. Try *reconstruct*: the prefix *re-* means "again," so the word should mean "to build again" — and indeed, *reconstruct* means to rebuild or piece together something that existed before (e.g., reconstructing a historical event from fragments of evidence). Try *superstructure*: *super-* means "above," so this should name something built *on top of* a base — which matches its use for the above-ground portion of a building, resting on the foundation.

This decomposition strategy generalizes: whenever you meet a word with a recognizable root like *struct*, *scrib/script* ("write"), or *port* ("carry"), isolate the root's core meaning first, then interpret the prefix as a directional or relational modifier. This turns vocabulary acquisition from memorization into inference — a skill that scales far better than word-by-word recall, especially for the dense academic and technical vocabulary of college-level reading.

### import (11.6s)

## Import

**Composition**

$$\underbrace{in\text{-}}_{into} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = import$$

**Definition**

An *import* is something carried in from elsewhere — an object, a substance, or, most commonly in modern usage, a good bought from a foreign country and brought into the home market. The word is built from Latin *in-* ("into") plus *portare* ("to carry"), the same *port-* root that gives *transport* (carry across), *export* (carry out), and *report* (carry back). The prefix appears here as *im-*, not *in-*, because Latin regularly assimilates *in-* to match the sound that follows it — before a root starting with *p*, *b*, or *m*, the *n* shifts to *m* for ease of pronunciation. That is the same rule behind *im-* in *impossible* and *implant*, and the same brick you already met, in unshifted form, in *inspect* (*in-* + *spect*, "look into"). Recognizing this pattern lets you see *in-* hiding inside words that don't spell it *i-n*.

**Worked example**

Consider *importer*: strip the agent suffix *-er* ("one who does") and you are left with *import* itself, "carry-in." An *importer* is literally "one who carries [goods] into" a country. Compare this to *exporter*, built the same way but with *ex-* ("out of") replacing *in-*: the two words form a matched pair describing opposite directions of the same act of carrying goods across a border.

**Problem-solving application**

Suppose you meet the unfamiliar word *portfolio*. You already know *port-* means "carry"; the second piece, *-folio*, comes from Latin *folium*, "leaf" or "sheet of paper" (as in *foliage*). So *portfolio* literally means "a carrying-case for sheets of paper" — which explains both its original sense (a case for documents or artwork) and its financial sense (a "case" of investments one holds). Try the same move on *comport*: *com-* ("together") + *port* ("carry") — to "carry oneself together," i.e., to behave in a certain manner. Whenever you spot *port* buried inside a longer word, test whether "carry" fits the meaning; if it does, you can often decode the whole word from its prefix alone, without a dictionary.

### inspect (12.6s)

## Inspect

**Definition.** To *inspect* is to look at something with deliberate, focused attention in order to check its condition, quality, or correctness — not a passing glance, but a directed examination. The word's own structure enacts its meaning: the prefix *in-* points the gaze inward, toward the object under scrutiny, while the root supplies the act of looking itself.

$$\underbrace{in\text{-}}_{\text{into, toward}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{inspect}$$

The root *spect* (from Latin *specere*, "to look, to behold") also surfaces in *spectator*, *spectacle*, and *perspective* — each involving some form of looking. Prefixed with *in-*, the looking becomes directed and probing: you don't simply see an object, you look *into* it.

**Worked example.** Consider the sentence: "The mechanic inspected the engine before certifying the car as roadworthy." Decompose it: the mechanic doesn't merely glance at the engine (that would be closer to *observe* or *notice*); the mechanic examines it part by part, checking bolts, hoses, and fluid levels, looking for hidden faults. The *in-* signals that the examination goes beneath the surface — toward the internal workings, not just the external appearance.

**Problem-solving application.** Word-sum awareness like this helps you distinguish between near-synonyms that a thesaurus might lump together. Suppose you're revising an essay and choose between *inspect*, *examine*, *scrutinize*, and *survey*. Ask: does my context involve looking *into* something for hidden detail (*inspect*, *scrutinize*), or looking broadly *over* something (*survey*, from *sur-* "over" + *videre* "to see")? A customs officer who *inspects* luggage is checking contents item by item; a pilot who *surveys* the landscape is taking in the whole scene from above. Applying the prefix's directional meaning — *in-* versus *sur-* — lets you predict which verb fits a given scenario, even for words you haven't memorized before. This is the practical payoff of morphological analysis: once you recognize that *in-* consistently marks "inward" motion across a family of words (*inspect*, *inspire*, *invade*, *insert*), you can reason your way to a word's nuance rather than relying on rote definition.

### predict (11.1s)

## Predict

**Predict** means to say what will happen before it happens — to use present knowledge to make a claim about the future. It is composed of the prefix *pre-* ("before") and the root *dict* ("to say"), which also appears in *dictate*, *diction*, and *verdict*.

$$
\underbrace{pre}_{\text{before}} + \underbrace{dict}_{\text{say}} = \text{predict — to say beforehand}
$$

Unlike *dict* alone, which simply names the act of speaking or declaring, *predict* adds a temporal direction: the statement is aimed forward, at an event that has not yet occurred. This is why *dict* is sometimes called a "time-shifting" root when paired with prefixes like *pre-* — the same root can point backward (*edict*, from *ex-*, "spoken out") or forward (*predict*, "spoken before"), depending on which prefix steers it.

**Worked example.** Consider the sentence: "Meteorologists **predict** rain for tomorrow." The forecaster is not describing rain that is falling now, nor recalling rain that fell yesterday — the *pre-* fixes the statement in time, placing the act of speaking before the event it describes. Compare this to a weather *report*, which describes conditions already observed. The prefix is what distinguishes a forecast from a record.

**Problem-solving application.** When you meet an unfamiliar word built on *dict*, you can use the prefix to recover its meaning without a dictionary. Try *predetermine*, *precondition*, and *predict* side by side: in each case, *pre-* signals that something — a decision, a requirement, a statement — happens before the main event it modifies. Now apply the same strategy to *contradict* (*contra-*, "against" + *dict*, "say"): rather than shifting time, this prefix shifts direction, producing "to speak against." Practicing this way — isolating the root's stable meaning, then asking what job the prefix is doing — lets you decode new *dict*-family words like *indict*, *jurisdiction*, or *malediction* by reasoning through their parts rather than memorizing each one separately.

### reject (13.2s)

## Reject

**Definition**

To reject something is to throw it back — to refuse to accept, believe, use, or agree to it. The word preserves its physical origin with unusual clarity: it is built from a prefix meaning "back" attached to a root meaning "throw."

$$
\underbrace{re}_{\text{back}} + \underbrace{ject}_{\text{throw}} = \text{reject (throw back, refuse)}
$$

The root *ject*, from Latin *iacere* ("to throw"), is one of the most productive roots in English, generating an entire family of words that describe different directions or manners of throwing — literal or figurative. *Reject* specifically frames refusal as a return motion: something is offered or sent toward you, and you throw it back rather than keep it.

**Worked example**

Consider how *ject* words differ only by the directional prefix attached to them:

$$
ject \xrightarrow{+pro} project \xrightarrow{+re} reject \xrightarrow{+in} inject
$$

- *project* (pro- = forward): to throw forward, cast an image ahead, or propose a plan
- *reject* (re- = back): to throw back, refuse
- *inject* (in- = into): to throw into, force a substance inward

A magazine editor who **rejects** a manuscript is, in effect, sending it back the way it came — the opposite motion of an editor who *accepts* it and moves it forward into publication. A body that **rejects** a transplanted organ is likewise "throwing it back," refusing to absorb what was introduced.

**Problem-solving application**

Once you recognize *ject* as "throw," you can decode unfamiliar words on sight by asking: what direction is the throw?

- *e- (out)* + *ject* → **eject**: throw out (a pilot ejects from a cockpit)
- *sub- (under)* + *ject* → **subject**: thrown under, i.e., placed under control or discussion
- *ob- (against)* + *ject* → **object**: thrown against, i.e., something placed in opposition, or a physical thing

Try this yourself: a company that **rejects** a job applicant is sending their application back; a court that **rejects** an appeal is refusing to let the case move forward. In each case, substitute "throw back" for "reject" and the sentence still makes sense — a quick test for whether you've understood the root correctly. This decomposition strategy — isolate the prefix, isolate the root, combine their literal meanings — works across dozens of *ject* words and is a reliable shortcut for building vocabulary from Latin roots generally.

### transport (12.2s)

## Transport

**Definition.** *Transport* means to carry something across a boundary — from one location, medium, or condition to another. The word is built from two Latin morphemes: *trans-* ("across") and *port* ("carry"), the same root found in *portable*, *export*, and *import*. This composition is transparent and worth seeing directly:

$$\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = \text{transport}$$

The prefix specifies the *movement* (across a boundary), and the root specifies the *action* (carrying). Together they name any process in which a carrying agent — the "crossing steerer" — moves a payload from one side of a boundary to the other.

**Worked example.** Consider three uses of the word:
- *"The truck transports goods from the factory to the port."* Here the boundary is geographic — a distance between two physical locations.
- *"Oxygen is transported through the bloodstream to the muscles."* Here the boundary is physiological — from lungs to tissue, crossing cell membranes and vessel walls.
- *"The novel transports the reader to nineteenth-century Russia."* Here the boundary is imaginative — from the reader's present reality into a fictional world.

In each case, the *trans-* + *port* structure holds steady: something crosses (trans-) by being carried (port). The "carrying root" is the mechanism (truck, blood, narrative), and the boundary is whatever separates the starting point from the destination.

**Problem-solving application.** When you meet an unfamiliar word built on *port*, use the composition equation as a decoding tool rather than memorizing each word separately. Ask two questions: (1) What does the prefix say about *direction relative to a boundary*? (2) What is being carried? For *report* (re- + port, "carry back"), the answer is information carried back to a source. For *deport* (de- + port, "carry away/down"), it is a person carried away from a country. For *support* (sub- + port, "carry from underneath"), it is weight carried from below. Practice: before checking a dictionary, predict the meaning of *comport*, *purport*, and *extraport* using only the prefix's directional sense and the root's meaning "carry." This decomposition strategy generalizes to the entire Latinate vocabulary of English, where prefix + root analysis is often faster and more reliable than rote memorization.

### prefix_root_composition (13.4s)

## Prefix Root Composition

A Latinate English word is rarely a single unbreakable unit — it is usually a root carrying the core meaning, aimed in a particular direction by a prefix. The root supplies the action; the prefix supplies the trajectory: into, across, before, together, or back. Learning to read this composition turns unfamiliar vocabulary into a solvable puzzle rather than a memorization burden.

Consider the root *-spect-/-port-/-dict-/-struct-/-ject-/-port-* family, each meaning something concrete on its own: *spect* = look, *port* = carry, *dict* = say, *struct* = build, *ject* = throw. Prefixes then steer that meaning:

$$
\underbrace{in}_{into} + \underbrace{spect}_{look} = \text{inspect (look } \textit{into} \text{ something)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = \text{transport (carry } \textit{across} \text{ distance)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{pre}_{before} + \underbrace{dict}_{say} = \text{predict (say } \textit{before} \text{ it happens)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{con}_{together} + \underbrace{struct}_{build} = \text{construct (build } \textit{together, piece by piece)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{re}_{back} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = \text{reject (throw } \textit{back}\text{)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{im}_{into} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = \text{import (carry } \textit{into} \text{ a place)}
$$

Notice that *port* appears twice with two different prefixes, producing opposite-flow meanings: *transport* moves something across a space, while *import* moves something into a space. The root stays fixed; only the prefix changes the direction of the action. This is the same logic as a compass rose applied to meaning — the root is the needle, the prefix is the direction it points.

This decomposition becomes a practical decoding tool. Faced with an unfamiliar word like *deject* or *export*, a reader who knows *de-* (down/away) and *ject* (throw), or *ex-* (out) and *port* (carry), can assemble a working definition before ever reaching for a dictionary: *deject* = "throw down" (lower one's spirits), *export* = "carry out" (send goods abroad). The strategy generalizes: isolate the root's core action, identify the prefix's directional signal, then combine them literally as a first guess, refining for the word's actual usage. This is the practical payoff of prefix-root composition — not trivia about etymology, but a repeatable method for inferring meaning under uncertainty.
