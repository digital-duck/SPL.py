## Agri Rev 1740S

Between the 1740s and 1780s, British agriculture underwent changes that made the Industrial Revolution possible before a single factory opened. Two innovations mattered most: enclosure and the four-field crop rotation. Enclosure was the legal and physical consolidation of scattered open fields and common pasture — traditionally shared by villagers for grazing and subsistence plots — into large, fenced, privately owned farms. Parliament passed over 4,000 enclosure acts between 1750 and 1830, eventually enclosing roughly 6.8 million acres of English land. The four-field rotation, popularized by Norfolk farmers and associated with Charles "Turnip" Townshend, replaced the old three-field system (which required leaving one field fallow each year) with a cycle of wheat, turnips, barley, and clover. Turnips and clover restored soil nitrogen and could feed livestock through winter, so farmers no longer needed to slaughter most of their herds each autumn for lack of fodder.

The combined effect was a sharp rise in agricultural productivity. Selective livestock breeding — most famously Robert Bakewell's work with sheep and cattle in the 1760s — nearly doubled average animal weights by mid-century, producing more meat and wool per acre. Grain yields rose as fallow land came into continuous cultivation. Fewer farmworkers were needed to feed the same, or a growing, population.

This is the section's central problem-solving insight: enclosure and rotation did not merely make farming more efficient — they severed the economic link between rural people and the land. Villagers who had depended on common grazing rights lost their livelihoods when those commons were fenced off, and they could no longer support themselves through small-scale mixed farming. Historians estimate that between the 1750s and early 1800s, England's rural population share fell from roughly 65 percent to under 40 percent, while urban populations in Manchester, Birmingham, and Leeds multiplied several times over in the same period. That displaced labor force had nowhere to go but the growing textile mills and, later, factories.

The evidentiary case is testable by comparison: continental European regions that retained open-field systems and communal land tenure into the nineteenth century — much of France before its own revolution, for instance — industrialized markedly later than Britain. The correlation is not coincidental. A factory needs workers willing, and more importantly compelled, to sell their labor for wages; enclosure created exactly that population by removing the subsistence alternative. This is why the Agricultural Revolution functions as the hidden precondition beneath every subsequent thread of industrialization: the spinning jenny and the steam engine required hands to operate them, and those hands came from villages the enclosure acts had already emptied.
