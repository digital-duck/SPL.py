### enlightenment_philosophy_1748 (16.8s)

## Enlightenment Philosophy 1748

The Enlightenment was an intellectual movement, centered in eighteenth-century Europe, that applied reason and empirical observation—the methods already transforming natural science—to the study of government, law, and society. Its core claim was that political authority should be justified by rational argument and demonstrated benefit to the governed, not by inherited divine right. Two texts crystallized this shift into concrete institutional proposals. Charles-Louis de Secondat, Baron de Montesquieu, published *The Spirit of the Laws* in 1748, arguing that liberty survives only when legislative, executive, and judicial power are held by separate bodies that check one another—a direct response to the unchecked absolutism he observed under the French monarchy. Jean-Jacques Rousseau's *The Social Contract* (1762) went further, arguing that legitimate government exists only because individuals consent to be governed, surrendering some natural freedom in exchange for the protection and equality of collective law; a ruler who violates that compact forfeits authority.

**Worked example.** Consider the American Constitutional Convention of 1787. Delegates split the federal government into Congress (legislative), the presidency (executive), and the federal courts (judicial), each empowered to check the others—Congress can override a presidential veto; courts can rule laws unconstitutional. This is Montesquieu's separation of powers applied almost verbatim, a debt the framers acknowledged explicitly in the Federalist Papers. Rousseau's influence appears differently: the Declaration of Independence's claim that governments derive "just powers from the consent of the governed" restates the social contract in a single sentence.

**Problem-solving application.** When analyzing any revolution between 1776 and 1848, ask two diagnostic questions drawn from these texts: first, does the new regime disperse power across institutions (Montesquieu's test), or does it concentrate power under a new single authority claiming popular mandate? Second, does it ground legitimacy in demonstrated consent—elections, ratified constitutions—or merely in force? Applying this framework to the French Revolution reveals its instability: the National Assembly initially satisfied both tests, but the Terror (1793–94) and Napoleon's coup (1799) show consent invoked rhetorically while power re-concentrated, explaining why the Revolution's radical Enlightenment promise collapsed into dictatorship within a decade.

### seven_years_war_1756 (15.7s)

## Seven Years War 1756

The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) was the first conflict fought on a genuinely global scale, with battles in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and the Philippines. In Europe, Britain and Prussia faced an alliance of France, Austria, Russia, Sweden, and Saxony; in the colonies, Britain and France fought directly for control of North America and India, with the North American theater — known there as the French and Indian War — beginning two years earlier, in 1754. The war's outcome, sealed by the Treaty of Paris (1763), redrew the colonial map: Britain took all of French Canada and the territory east of the Mississippi, confirmed its dominance in India after Robert Clive's victory at Plassey (1757), and emerged as the world's leading colonial and naval power. France retained only small Caribbean sugar islands and fishing rights off Newfoundland, losing the bulk of its overseas empire in a single settlement.

A concrete way to see the war's stakes is the fighting in India, where the conflict was not simply Britain versus France but a proxy struggle waged through local rulers and the two nations' rival East India companies. The 1757 Battle of Plassey, in which Clive's roughly 3,000 troops defeated the far larger army of the Nawab of Bengal (aided by French artillery support to the Nawab), gave the British East India Company control of Bengal's revenue — a foothold that expanded into effective control of the subcontinent over the following decades. This one battle illustrates how the war's colonial theaters, though geographically distant from Europe, decided long-term imperial control just as decisively as the European campaigns did.

The war's financial legacy proved as consequential as its territorial one. Britain's war debt roughly doubled, prompting new colonial taxes (the Stamp Act, Townshend Acts) that fed resentment culminating in the American Revolution. France's debt was worse in relative terms: fighting an expensive war and losing its colonial revenue simultaneously left the French crown financially strained for the next quarter-century. That debt, compounded by France's later support for the American Revolution, became a root cause of the fiscal crisis that forced Louis XVI to convene the Estates-General in 1789 — the opening act of the French Revolution.

### american_revolution_1775 (14.5s)

## American Revolution 1775

By the 1770s, the thirteen British colonies in North America had developed a political culture that combined self-governing colonial assemblies with growing resentment of British taxation imposed without their consent. Britain's massive debt from the Seven Years' War — roughly £133 million by 1763 — led Parliament to impose a series of revenue measures on the colonies: the Stamp Act (1765), the Townshend Acts (1767), and the Tea Act (1773). Colonists, steeped in Enlightenment ideas about natural rights and government by consent, rejected these measures under the slogan "no taxation without representation," arguing that legitimate government required the consent of the governed, not mere subjecthood to a distant Parliament.

Tensions escalated from protest to armed conflict. The Boston Tea Party (December 1773) provoked the Coercive Acts, which colonists called the "Intolerable Acts." Fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. On July 4, 1776, the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, drafted principally by Thomas Jefferson, which opens by asserting that "all men are created equal" and are endowed with "unalienable Rights" — language drawn directly from John Locke's social contract theory. This was not merely a tax revolt; it was a claim that political authority derives from the people, not from monarchy or divine right.

Militarily, the odds favored Britain: it possessed the world's largest navy and a professional army against poorly supplied colonial militias. The war's turning point came at Saratoga (1777), a decisive American victory that persuaded France — eager to avenge its Seven Years' War losses — to enter the war as an American ally in 1778, followed by Spain and the Dutch Republic. French naval and financial support proved decisive at Yorktown (1781), where a combined Franco-American force trapped and forced the surrender of General Cornwallis. The Treaty of Paris (1783) recognized American independence.

The consequences extended far beyond North America. The war cost France an estimated 1 billion livres, deepening the fiscal crisis that would trigger the French Revolution six years later. More broadly, the American Revolution demonstrated that Enlightenment principles could be translated into a durable constitutional government — the United States Constitution (1787) — inspiring independence and republican movements across Latin America and Europe well into the nineteenth century.

### french_revolution_1789 (14.5s)

## French Revolution 1789

By 1789, the French monarchy was insolvent. Decades of war spending — including massive loans to support the American Revolution — had pushed the crown into debt equal to roughly the state's entire annual revenue several times over, while the tax burden fell almost entirely on commoners, since the nobility and clergy were largely exempt. When King Louis XVI convened the Estates-General in May 1789 to approve new taxes, the Third Estate (commoners, representing about 97% of the population) broke away, declared itself the National Assembly, and swore the Tennis Court Oath to write a constitution. On July 14, 1789, Parisians stormed the Bastille fortress, and in August the Assembly abolished feudal privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, asserting that sovereignty resides in the nation, not the king, and that liberty, property, and equality before the law are natural rights.

The Declaration's language directly echoed the American Declaration of Independence and the ideas of the Enlightenment, but its consequences went further and faster. Within four years France abolished the monarchy, executed Louis XVI (January 1793), and descended into the Terror, during which the Committee of Public Safety executed roughly 17,000 people (some estimates run higher when massacres are included) in the name of defending the revolution from internal and external enemies. This trajectory — from constitutional reform to radical republic to violent purge to eventual military dictatorship under Napoleon Bonaparte by 1799 — became the template historians use to analyze later revolutions: a moderate opening phase is often followed by radicalization when war, economic crisis, or elite resistance threatens the new order.

For problem-solving purposes, the useful analytical skill is comparative causation: identify why the American Revolution (1775) produced a stable constitutional republic while the French Revolution produced a decade of upheaval before Napoleon's coup. Key differences include France's existing rigid class hierarchy (versus colonial America's weaker aristocracy), a genuinely bankrupt treasury, and immediate war with hostile monarchies across Europe starting in 1792, which radicalized the revolution under the pressure of foreign invasion. This comparative method — weighing institutional starting conditions, fiscal pressure, and external threat — is the core analytic tool for evaluating any revolution's outcome, including the wave of 1848 revolutions and anti-colonial movements that later cited the Declaration of the Rights of Man as precedent.

### haitian_revolution_1791 (14.3s)

## Haitian Revolution 1791

The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was the uprising of enslaved Africans in Saint-Domingue, France's richest colony, which abolished slavery, defeated three European armies, and established Haiti as the first nation founded by former slaves. In August 1791, tens of thousands of enslaved people, inspired partly by the French Revolution's declaration that "men are born free and equal in rights," organized a coordinated uprising in the colony's Northern Plain, burning plantations and killing enslavers. Saint-Domingue in 1789 produced roughly 40 percent of the world's sugar and half its coffee, generating more wealth than all thirteen former British American colonies combined — making the revolt an assault on the single most valuable node in the Atlantic economy.

Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man, emerged as the revolution's central strategist. He allied first with Spain, then with France after French commissioners abolished slavery in the colony in 1793 (ratified by the National Convention in Paris in 1794), and used this alliance to defeat Spanish and British invasion forces by 1798 — Britain alone lost an estimated 15,000 to 20,000 soldiers, mostly to yellow fever and combat, in its failed attempt to seize the colony. When Napoleon Bonaparte sent an expedition in 1802 to restore slavery, Toussaint was captured and died in a French prison in 1803, but his former general Jean-Jacques Dessalines continued the fight, defeating the French at the Battle of Vertières in November 1803.

The problem-solving core of the revolution was strategic: the rebels had to convert a labor force with no formal military training into an army capable of outlasting three professional European powers. Their solution combined guerrilla tactics suited to Saint-Domingue's mountainous terrain, exploitation of tropical disease that devastated acclimatized European troops, and shifting alliances that played France, Spain, and Britain against one another. On January 1, 1804, Dessalines declared independence, renaming the colony Haiti. The revolution's consequences radiated outward: it ended slavery in the colony two generations before most of the hemisphere, contributed to Napoleon's decision to sell the Louisiana Territory to the United States in 1803, and stood as living proof — terrifying to slaveholding powers, inspiring to enslaved people elsewhere — that a slave society could be overthrown by the enslaved themselves.
