### function (0.0s)

## Function

A function is a rule that assigns to each input exactly one output. If $f$ is a function from a set $X$ (the domain) to a set $Y$ (the codomain), we write $f: X \to Y$, and for each $x \in X$ there is a unique element $f(x) \in Y$. This uniqueness condition — one output per input — is the entire content of the definition. It is what separates a function from a general relation, and it is why the vertical line test works: a curve represents a function precisely when no vertical line crosses it more than once, since two intersection points would mean one input producing two outputs.

The crucial shift for calculus is to stop thinking of a function as "a formula" and start thinking of it as a mapping — an assignment of outputs to inputs that may or may not have a closed-form expression. Consider $f(x) = x^2$, $g(x) = \sin(x)$, and the piecewise function
$$
h(x) = \begin{cases} x & x < 0 \\ x^2 & x \geq 0 \end{cases}.
$$
Despite looking structurally different, all three are functions in exactly the same sense: each obeys the one-output rule on its domain. A table of values, a graph, or even an algorithm that computes an output from an input all qualify as functions, provided the assignment is well-defined.

This mapping-first view matters for problem-solving because it tells you what to check before applying any calculus tool. Suppose you're asked whether $y^2 = x$ defines $y$ as a function of $x$. Solve for $y$: $y = \pm\sqrt{x}$. For $x = 4$, this gives $y = 2$ and $y = -2$ — two outputs for one input — so $y^2 = x$ does *not* define a function of $x$. But if you restrict to $y = \sqrt{x}$ alone (discarding the negative branch), the rule now assigns exactly one output to each $x \geq 0$, and it is a function with domain $[0, \infty)$.

This habit — asking "does exactly one output correspond to each input, and over what domain?" — is the first check you'll run before differentiating, composing, or inverting any expression, since operations like finding $f^{-1}$ or evaluating $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ presuppose that $f$ is a genuine function to begin with.

### limit (0.0s)

## Limit

The limit of a function $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches a value $a$ describes the value that $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to as $x$ gets arbitrarily close to $a$ — without ever requiring $x = a$. This distinction matters: $f$ may not even be defined at $a$, yet the limit can still exist. We write

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

to mean that $L$ is that approached value. The idea is made rigorous by the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition: for every tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$ on the output, there exists a tolerance $\delta > 0$ on the input such that whenever $0 < |x - a| < \delta$, it follows that $|f(x) - L| < \delta$... more precisely, $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. In words: no matter how tightly you demand $f(x)$ stay near $L$, you can always find an interval around $a$ (excluding $a$ itself) that guarantees it.

**Worked example.** Consider $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$ at $a = 2$. Direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, an undefined expression — $f$ is not even defined at $x=2$. But for $x \neq 2$, we can factor:

$$f(x) = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x + 2$$

As $x$ approaches 2 from either side, $f(x)$ approaches $4$, so $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 4$, even though $f(2)$ itself doesn't exist. This is the essential payoff of the limit concept: it lets us reason about behavior *near* a point independently of what happens *at* that point.

**Problem-solving application.** Limits are the operation underlying the rest of calculus. The derivative is defined as the limit of a difference quotient, $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$, capturing instantaneous rate of change. The definite integral is a limit of Riemann sums as rectangle widths shrink to zero. An infinite series is defined as the limit of its sequence of partial sums. In each case, an intuitive but imprecise idea — instantaneous speed, exact area, infinite sum — is rescued from paradox by phrasing it as a limit and proving that limit exists. When solving problems, first check whether direct substitution works; if it produces an indeterminate form like $\frac{0}{0}$, try algebraic simplification (factoring, rationalizing) or, later, L'Hôpital's rule, to reveal the limit that direct evaluation obscures.

### derivative (0.0s)

## Derivative

The derivative of a function $f$ at a point $x$ is defined as

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

This expression measures the **instantaneous rate of change** of $f$ at $x$. The fraction $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ is the slope of a secant line through the points $(x, f(x))$ and $(x+h, f(x+h))$ — the *average* rate of change over that interval. As $h \to 0$, the second point slides toward the first, and the secant line rotates into the **tangent line** at $x$. The derivative is the slope of that tangent.

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$. Then

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) = 2x$$

So at $x = 3$, the slope of the tangent to $y = x^2$ is $f'(3) = 6$: the curve is rising six times as fast as $x$ is changing, at that instant.

**Problem-solving application.** The derivative converts *position* into *velocity* and *total cost* into *marginal cost* because both are instances of the same underlying idea: an instantaneous rate of change. If $s(t)$ gives position at time $t$, then $v(t) = s'(t)$ gives velocity — not the average speed over an interval, but the speed *right now*. Suppose $s(t) = 4t^2$ (meters, with $t$ in seconds). Then $s'(t) = 8t$, so at $t = 5$ seconds the object's instantaneous velocity is $40\text{ m/s}$, even though its *average* velocity from $t=0$ to $t=5$ was only $20\text{ m/s}$.

The same logic applies economically: if $C(x)$ is the total cost of producing $x$ units, $C'(x)$ is the marginal cost — the approximate cost of producing one additional unit. If $C(x) = 0.01x^2 + 5x + 200$, then $C'(x) = 0.02x + 5$. At $x = 100$, marginal cost is $C'(100) = 7$: producing the 101st unit costs about \$7, distinct from the *average* cost per unit, $C(100)/100 = \$4.90$. This distinction between average and instantaneous rates is precisely what the limit in the derivative's definition is built to resolve, and it is the reason derivatives, not simple ratios, drive optimization problems in physics, economics, and engineering.

### continuity (10.4s)

## Continuity

A function $f$ is **continuous at $x = a$** when three conditions hold simultaneously: $f(a)$ is defined, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists, and the two agree —

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a).$$

Informally, the graph has no jump, hole, or vertical asymptote at $a$: you could trace it through that point without lifting your pencil. Failing any one of the three conditions produces a distinct type of discontinuity — a hole (limit exists but $\neq f(a)$, or $f(a)$ undefined), a jump (one-sided limits exist but disagree), or an infinite/essential discontinuity (the limit fails to exist as a finite number).

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$ for $x \neq 2$, with $f(2) = 5$. Algebraically, $f(x) = x + 2$ wherever $x \neq 2$, so $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 4$. But $f(2) = 5$ by definition. Since $4 \neq 5$, $f$ is discontinuous at $x = 2$ — specifically a removable discontinuity, because redefining $f(2) = 4$ would repair it.

**Problem-solving application.** Continuity is the gatekeeper condition for two of the most useful theorems in analysis. The **Intermediate Value Theorem** guarantees that if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $N$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f(c) = N$ for some $c \in [a,b]$ — this is exactly why continuous functions can't skip over values, and it underlies root-finding methods like bisection search. Continuity is also *necessary* for differentiability: if $f$ isn't continuous at $a$, it cannot be differentiable there, since the difference quotient defining the derivative would fail to have a limit. (The converse is false — $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $x=0$.)

In practice, checking continuity is a diagnostic step before applying stronger tools. Given a piecewise function like

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 & x < 1 \\ 2x - 1 & x \geq 1 \end{cases},$$

you verify continuity at the seam $x=1$ by comparing $\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 1$, $\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 1$, and $f(1) = 1$ — all match, so $f$ is continuous there, and you may safely proceed to test differentiability or apply IVT on any interval containing that point.

### differential_equation (0.0s)

## Differential Equation

A differential equation relates a function to its own derivatives. Instead of solving for a number, you solve for the function itself — the equation constrains how a quantity changes, and the solution is the family of quantities consistent with that constraint. The simplest nontrivial example is $y' = ky$: the rate of change of $y$ is proportional to $y$ itself. This single line encodes population growth, radioactive decay, compound interest, and cooling — anything whose rate of change scales with its current amount.

To solve $y' = ky$, separate variables: $\frac{dy}{y} = k\,dt$. Integrating both sides gives $\ln|y| = kt + C_1$, and exponentiating yields

$$
y(t) = Ce^{kt}
$$

where $C = y(0)$ is fixed by an initial condition. Check it: differentiate $y = Ce^{kt}$ and you get $y' = kCe^{kt} = ky$, confirming the solution satisfies the original equation. Notice that the differential equation alone doesn't pin down one function — it defines an infinite family, indexed by $C$. The initial condition selects the specific member of that family that matches your physical situation.

This is why differential equations are the native language of physics. Newton's second law, $F = ma$, is really $F = m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$ — a statement about how position changes over time, not position itself. Maxwell's equations relate electric and magnetic fields through their spatial and temporal derivatives. The Schrödinger equation governs how a quantum wavefunction evolves. In each case, physical law doesn't hand you the answer directly; it hands you a local rule for change, and solving the differential equation is how you extract global behavior — a trajectory, a field, a probability distribution — from that rule.

Problem-solving practice: suppose a bacterial culture doubles every 3 hours. Model it with $y' = ky$. Since $y(3) = 2y(0)$, substitute into $y(t) = y(0)e^{kt}$ to get $2 = e^{3k}$, so $k = \frac{\ln 2}{3} \approx 0.231$. You now have a complete predictive model, $y(t) = y(0)e^{0.231t}$, built entirely from one derivative relationship and one data point — the general pattern for turning a rate-of-change statement into a usable forecast.

### optimization (9.0s)

## Optimization

Optimization is the process of finding the input to a function that produces the largest or smallest output. Formally, $x = c$ is a **local maximum** of $f$ if $f(c) \geq f(x)$ for all $x$ near $c$, and a **local minimum** if $f(c) \leq f(x)$ near $c$. Calculus locates these points through a single observation: at a smooth local extremum, the tangent line is horizontal, so $f'(c) = 0$. Points where $f'(x) = 0$ or $f'$ fails to exist are called **critical points**, and every local extremum of a differentiable function must occur at one.

Not every critical point is an extremum — $f(x) = x^3$ has $f'(0) = 0$, but $x=0$ is neither a max nor a min. The **second derivative test** resolves this: if $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0$, the graph curves upward and $c$ is a local minimum; if $f''(c) < 0$, it curves downward and $c$ is a local maximum; if $f''(c) = 0$, the test is inconclusive and the first-derivative sign change must be checked instead.

**Worked example.** A manufacturer estimates profit (in thousands of dollars) from producing $x$ hundred units as $P(x) = -x^3 + 6x^2 - 9x + 2$ for $x \in [0,4]$. Setting $P'(x) = -3x^2 + 12x - 9 = 0$ gives $x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0$, so $x = 1$ or $x = 3$. Since $P''(x) = -6x + 12$, we get $P''(1) = 6 > 0$ (local min) and $P''(3) = -6 < 0$ (local max). But the problem asks for the *global* maximum profit over $[0,4]$, so the second derivative test alone is insufficient.

This is where continuity matters: the **Extreme Value Theorem** guarantees that a continuous function on a closed interval $[a,b]$ attains both a global maximum and minimum on that interval. Crucially, the global extrema can occur either at an interior critical point or at an endpoint — so the search must compare all candidates. Evaluating $P(0) = 2$, $P(1) = -2$, $P(3) = 2$, and $P(4) = 6$ shows the global maximum profit is $\$6{,}000$ at $x = 4$ — the boundary, not either critical point. The general optimization procedure is therefore: find all critical points in the domain, evaluate $f$ there and at the endpoints (if the domain is closed), and compare all the resulting values directly.

### population_dynamics (10.1s)

## Population Dynamics

A population's growth rate rarely stays constant — resources run out, and growth slows as numbers rise. The logistic model captures this with a single differential equation:

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{K}\right)$$

Here $P(t)$ is population size, $r$ is the intrinsic growth rate, and $K$ is the carrying capacity — the population level the environment can sustain indefinitely. When $P$ is small relative to $K$, the term $(1 - P/K) \approx 1$ and growth is nearly exponential. As $P$ approaches $K$, the term shrinks toward zero, throttling growth. This single equation encodes a self-limiting feedback loop that exponential growth cannot.

Solving the equation by separation of variables gives the logistic curve:

$$P(t) = \frac{K}{1 + \left(\frac{K - P_0}{P_0}\right)e^{-rt}}$$

which produces the familiar S-shaped curve: slow start, rapid rise, then leveling off near $K$. Equilibria — points where $dP/dt = 0$ — occur at $P = 0$ and $P = K$. To classify stability, examine the sign of $dP/dt$ near each equilibrium: for $0 < P < K$, $dP/dt > 0$, so trajectories move away from $P=0$ (unstable) and toward $P=K$ (stable). This is why $P=0$ is called an unstable equilibrium and $P=K$ a stable one — a small perturbation from $P=0$ grows, while one from $P=K$ decays back.

This equilibrium analysis generalizes to systems of interacting populations. The SIR model for epidemics extends the same logic to three coupled variables — susceptible ($S$), infected ($I$), recovered ($R$):

$$\frac{dS}{dt} = -\beta SI, \quad \frac{dI}{dt} = \beta SI - \gamma I, \quad \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I$$

Here the "carrying capacity" logic reappears as a threshold condition: an epidemic grows only if $\beta S > \gamma$, i.e., the reproduction number $R_0 = \beta S_0/\gamma$ exceeds 1. Finding equilibria (where $dI/dt = 0$) and testing their stability is exactly the same procedure used for logistic growth — set derivatives to zero, then check the sign of nearby derivatives.

The practical payoff: whether modeling a bacterial colony or a disease outbreak, framing the problem as "find equilibria, then classify their stability" turns population dynamics into a solvable optimization-like question, not just a curve-fitting exercise.
