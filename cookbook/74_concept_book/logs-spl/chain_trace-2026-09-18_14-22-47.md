## Pathogen

A pathogen is any biological agent capable of causing disease in a host organism. Pathogens fall into four broad categories: viruses (non-living particles of genetic material wrapped in protein that hijack host cells to replicate), bacteria (single-celled prokaryotic organisms, only some of which cause disease), fungi (organisms like molds and yeasts that can infect tissue, especially in immunocompromised hosts), and parasites (organisms, from single-celled protozoa to multicellular worms, that live in or on a host and draw resources from it). What unites these otherwise very different organisms under one concept is function, not biology: each is an agent from outside the body that disrupts normal physiology, and none of them can be understood, predicted, or countered using anatomy or physiology alone.

**Worked example.** Consider influenza virus infecting the respiratory tract. The virus binds to receptors on epithelial cells lining the airway, injects its RNA, and hijacks the host cell's ribosomes to manufacture new viral particles, eventually rupturing the cell to release thousands of copies. This triggers the immune system's fever and inflammatory response, producing the classic symptoms of illness — the felt experience of infection is largely the *host's response* to the pathogen, not damage from the virus itself. Contrast this with a bacterial infection like strep throat: the bacterium *Streptococcus pyogenes* multiplies extracellularly and releases toxins that damage tissue directly.

**Problem-solving application.** Distinguishing pathogen type is not academic — it dictates treatment. A physician who reflexively prescribes antibiotics for a viral infection wastes the drug (antibiotics target bacterial cell walls or ribosomal machinery, structures viruses don't have) and contributes to antibiotic resistance in unrelated bacterial populations. Given a patient's symptoms, lab culture results, and response to prior treatment, you should be able to reason toward pathogen class: rapid symptom onset with no bacterial culture growth suggests viral origin; a positive bacterial culture with response to antibiotics confirms bacterial origin; response to antifungal but not antibacterial agents points to fungal infection. This diagnostic reasoning — inferring the invisible cause from its visible effects — is the practical heart of infectious disease medicine, and it depends entirely on treating the pathogen as a distinct causal agent layered onto the body's own physiology.
