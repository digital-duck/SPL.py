## 一

You already know 一 (yī) as "one" — the simplest possible stroke, a single horizontal line. But 一 is also doing quiet structural work across the writing system, and once you see it, you'll spot it everywhere.

First, as radical 1 (Kangxi radical), 一 is the ancestor of a whole family of characters. It's not just "the character that means one" — it's a *classifying unit* that dictionaries use to group characters by shared form or meaning. Characters like 二 (two) and 三 (three) build directly on it by stacking strokes, showing you a rare case where a number's *shape* literally encodes its *value*: one line, two lines, three lines.

Second, 一 shows up as an intensifier or totality marker, not just a count. In 一生 (yīshēng, "one's whole life") or 一切 (yīqiè, "everything"), 一 pushes toward *entireness* — "all of it," "the whole thing" — rather than the number 1 you'd use in arithmetic. This is a pattern worth noticing: a character's core meaning (unity, oneness) can stretch into related meanings (wholeness, totality) depending on context.

Third, in spoken math or when reading digits aloud one at a time (like a phone number or ID), 一 is sometimes pronounced *yāo* instead of *yī* — this avoids confusion with 七 (qī, "seven"), which sounds similar in noisy conditions.

**Practice problem:** Below are three phrases using 一. For each, decide whether 一 is functioning as (a) the number "one," or (b) an intensifier meaning "whole/entire":
1. 一个苹果 (yī gè píngguǒ)
2. 一生 (yīshēng)
3. 一年 (yī nián)

### 又 (0.0s)

## 又

You've probably picked up 又 (yòu) as a word that pops up when something happens *again*, or when two things are true *at the same time*. That intuition is a great start — now let's look at how it actually works as a structural piece of the sentence.

Think of 又 as a repetition or addition marker that attaches to a verb or adjective, not to a noun. It signals: "this action/state, one more time" or "this trait, alongside another trait." A useful pattern to notice is the paired construction **又...又...**, which links two qualities or actions happening together, similar to how in English we might say "both... and..." — for example, describing weather that is both hot and humid, you'd frame it as 又热又闷 ("both hot and stuffy").

There's a subtlety worth flagging: 又 describes something that *already happened* or is a *known repeating pattern*, which is different from 再 (zài), which is used for something that *will* repeat in the future. This distinction is a small but important rule: 又 = past/realized repetition, 再 = future/intended repetition. Getting this pairing right is a mark of moving from beginner to intermediate fluency, because English "again" doesn't make that time distinction at all — you have to track it yourself.

**Practice problem:** Using 又...又..., write one short sentence in Chinese (with pinyin) describing a fruit or food that has two qualities at once — for example, something that is both sweet and sour. Then write a second sentence using 又 alone to say "it rained again yesterday."

### 宀 (0.0s)

## 宀

Picture a little peaked roof sitting over something below it — that's 宀. You won't see it standing alone as its own word; instead, it shows up as a radical, quietly doing its job at the top of other characters, covering whatever comes underneath.

Think about what a roof actually does in real life: it shelters, it encloses, it marks the boundary between "inside, protected" and "outside, exposed." That's exactly the job 宀 plays inside a character. Whatever sits beneath this radical is being framed as something contained, settled, or domestic — a house, a place, a state of being sheltered.

Look at 安 (safe, peaceful): a woman 女 sheltered under a roof 宀.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = \underbrace{安}_{\text{peace, safety}}$$

The logic isn't random — someone protected under a roof is imagined as being at peace. Or take 家 (home, family): a pig 豕 under a roof 宀, reflecting an ancient idea of a household as a place where livestock and people lived together under one shelter.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{豕}_{\text{pig}} = \underbrace{家}_{\text{home, family}}$$

**Simple rule:** when you spot 宀 sitting on top of a character, expect the meaning to lean toward shelter, containment, or domestic life — houses, safety, settling in.

### 寸 (11.3s)

## 寸

You've likely met 寸 (cùn) as "inch" — but it's really a **unit definition**, and units are one of the first formal structures you meet in measurement theory. A unit is not a fixed physical distance forever; it's a *reference length* that a whole system of measurement is built on top of.

Historically, 1 寸 was defined as roughly the width of a thumb — hence the character's shape, built from 一 (a mark) placed near 又 (a hand), pointing at a hand's own measure. That's the intuition. The structure is that every larger unit in the traditional Chinese system is a fixed multiple of 寸:

$$
1 \text{ 尺 (chǐ)} = 10 \text{ 寸}, \qquad 1 \text{ 寸} = 10 \text{ 分 (fēn)}
$$

Notice the pattern: this is a **base-10 positional system for length**, exactly like the metric system's meter → decimeter → centimeter chain. If $L$ is a length in 寸, you can write it in mixed units the same way you'd expand a decimal number:

$$
L = 10a + b, \quad a = \lfloor L/10 \rfloor \text{ (chǐ)}, \quad b = L \bmod 10 \text{ (fen equivalents)}
$$

This is the same "unit conversion by powers of a base" idea you'll formalize later with dimensional analysis and unit conversion factors in physics — a ratio like $\frac{1\text{ 尺}}{10\text{ 寸}} = 1$ lets you multiply through any measurement without changing its actual size.

**Practice problem:** A board is measured as 3 尺 and 7 寸 long. Express its total length in 寸 only, then convert that number to 分.

### 守 (10.9s)

## 守

Think of 守 as *standing guard at a post you're responsible for*. The character shows this directly: 宀 (a roof) sits over 寸 (a hand, or a measured unit — the character for "inch" and also for careful, deliberate action). Someone stationed *under a roof, hand ready* — that's a guard watching a doorway, a boundary, a rule.

Structurally, 守 works like a small formal system: it names an agent, a domain, and a constraint the agent maintains *over time*. Compare it to an invariant in math or code — a property $P$ that must hold at every step of a process, not just once. If $S$ is a system evolving through states $s_0, s_1, s_2, \dots$, we say $P$ is *守-ed* (guarded) if

$$P(s_0) \text{ is true, and } P(s_i) \Rightarrow P(s_{i+1}) \text{ for all } i.$$

That's exactly what 守法 (*shǒu fǎ*, "to abide by the law") means in practice: the rule isn't checked once — it's maintained continuously, the way a loop invariant must survive every iteration.

The simple rule: **守 = agent + boundary + persistence**. Drop any one of the three and the meaning shifts — 守 without persistence is just "watch once"; without a boundary, there's nothing to guard.

Notice too how 守's two neighboring senses — "to defend" and "nearby/adjoining" — aren't a coincidence. A guard's post defines the edge between "inside" and "outside"; what's *adjoining* is precisely what sits at that boundary.

**Practice problem:** A number-guessing game keeps the invariant "the secret number is always inside the current guess-range $[a,b]$." If the range starts at $[1, 100]$ and after one guess narrows to $[1, 50]$, write the invariant $P(s_i)$ symbolically, and explain in one sentence why 守 is a good word for what the range-narrowing step must do.
