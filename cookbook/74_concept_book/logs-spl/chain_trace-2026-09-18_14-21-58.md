## Anatomy

Anatomy is the study of the body's physical structure — the sizes, shapes, positions, and connections of its parts, from organs visible to the naked eye down to tissues seen under a microscope. It answers the question "where and what," in contrast to physiology, which asks "how it works." Anatomy is often called the body's map, because every other discipline in medicine depends on it: a surgeon cannot remove an appendix, a radiologist cannot read a scan, and a pharmacologist cannot predict where a drug will act without a shared, precise vocabulary for location. That vocabulary is built on standard reference points. The body is described in "anatomical position" — standing upright, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms forward — and three imaginary planes cut through it: the sagittal plane divides left from right, the coronal (frontal) plane divides front from back, and the transverse plane divides top from bottom. Paired directional terms — superior/inferior, anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, proximal/distal — let clinicians specify a location unambiguously, the way latitude and longitude specify a point on Earth.

Consider a worked example: a patient reports pain "in the lower right abdomen." A physician immediately narrows the possibilities using anatomical organization. The abdomen is conventionally divided into four quadrants or nine regions; the right lower quadrant contains the appendix, the cecum, part of the ascending colon, and in women, the right ovary. Because the appendix sits at a fairly predictable position — roughly two-thirds of the way from the navel to the right hip bone, a landmark called McBurney's point — tenderness localized there raises suspicion of appendicitis rather than, say, gallbladder disease, which would present in the right upper quadrant instead.

This is where anatomy becomes a problem-solving tool rather than a memorization exercise: clinical reasoning is often a process of using known anatomical relationships to narrow a diagnosis before any imaging is done. A sudden numbness in one hand, for instance, prompts a clinician to trace the nerve pathway anatomically — spinal cord, brachial plexus, individual peripheral nerve — to predict which specific nerve root is compressed, based on exactly which fingers are affected. Surgeons rely on the same skill in reverse, planning an incision that reaches a target structure while avoiding nearby vessels and nerves whose positions are fixed and well documented. In every case, the underlying logic is the same: because structure predicts function and vulnerability, an accurate mental map of the body lets a practitioner reason from where symptoms appear to what and where the underlying problem must be.
