## Perceptron 1958

**Definition.** In 1958, psychologist Frank Rosenblatt introduced the perceptron, the first artificial neuron that could learn from data through an explicit, automatic update rule rather than having its parameters set by hand. A perceptron takes an input vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$, computes a weighted sum plus a bias, and passes the result through a step function to produce a binary output:

$$
\hat{y} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b > 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

The learning rule is what made the perceptron remarkable: for each training example, if the prediction $\hat{y}$ differs from the true label $y$, the weights are nudged in the direction that reduces the error,

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta (y - \hat{y})\, \mathbf{x}, \qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta (y - \hat{y})
$$

where $\eta$ is a small learning rate. Rosenblatt proved that if the training data is linearly separable — meaning some hyperplane can perfectly split the two classes — this rule is guaranteed to converge to a solution in a finite number of steps. This convergence guarantee, not just the biological metaphor, is what made the perceptron a genuine theoretical result rather than a heuristic.

**Worked example.** Consider learning the logical AND function: inputs $(0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1)$ with labels $0,0,0,1$. Starting from $\mathbf{w}=(0,0)$, $b=0$, the update rule processes each example, incrementing weights whenever the perceptron misclassifies a positive case and decrementing whenever it misclassifies a negative one. After a few passes through the data, the perceptron converges to weights like $\mathbf{w}=(1,1)$, $b=-1.5$, which correctly separates the single positive point from the three negative ones with the line $x_1 + x_2 = 1.5$.

**Problem-solving application.** The perceptron's convergence theorem is a practical diagnostic tool: if training a perceptron on a dataset never stops making updates, that is a formal proof the data is *not* linearly separable — the most famous case being XOR, which no single perceptron can solve. This limitation, publicized by Minsky and Papert in 1969, triggered the first "AI winter," but it also motivated the multi-layer networks and backpropagation that revived the field decades later — the single neuron's failure defined exactly what deeper architectures needed to fix.

### ai_winter_1974 (0.0s)

## Ai Winter 1974

By the early 1970s, a single technical critique had reshaped the trajectory of artificial intelligence research. In their 1969 book *Perceptrons*, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert rigorously analyzed the [[perceptron_1958]], the single-layer neural network model that had generated enormous optimism a decade earlier. Their central finding was mathematical, not merely rhetorical: a single-layer perceptron can only compute functions that are *linearly separable* — that is, functions whose positive and negative outputs can be divided by a straight line (or hyperplane, in higher dimensions). The canonical counterexample is the XOR function, which outputs 1 when its two binary inputs differ and 0 when they match. No single straight line can separate the "1" cases from the "0" cases in XOR's input space, so no single-layer perceptron, regardless of how its weights are tuned, can learn it.

This was a precise proof of a fundamental limitation, and its consequences were severe. Minsky and Papert further speculated — incorrectly, as later work showed — that extending perceptrons to multiple layers would not meaningfully overcome this limitation, since no efficient training algorithm for multi-layer networks existed at the time. Funding agencies, including DARPA and the UK's Science Research Council (via the 1973 Lighthill Report), interpreted this as evidence that neural approaches to AI had hit a dead end. Government and industry funding for neural network research collapsed, and broader disillusionment with AI's unmet promises — machine translation had failed to deliver, and symbolic reasoning systems struggled to scale — compounded the retreat. This funding collapse, lasting roughly from 1974 into the early 1980s, became known as the first "AI winter."

The deeper lesson for problem-solving is about the danger of generalizing from a proven limitation to an unproven one. Minsky and Papert's XOR proof was rigorous; their pessimism about multi-layer networks was speculative and turned out to be wrong once the backpropagation algorithm was developed in the 1980s, enabling efficient training of multi-layer perceptrons that easily solve XOR. When evaluating a technical critique, separate what is proven from what is merely inferred — and recognize that an "unsolved" problem is not the same as an "unsolvable" one. This distinction determines whether a field pauses to build better tools or abandons a promising direction prematurely.

### expert_systems_1980 (0.0s)

## Expert Systems 1980

An **expert system** is a program that captures the decision-making knowledge of a human specialist as an explicit set of if-then rules, then applies those rules to new cases through a mechanical inference process. Where earlier AI systems tried to build general reasoning or learning ability from scratch, expert systems made a narrower bet: intelligence in a specific domain could be reproduced by extracting the facts and heuristics an expert already knows and encoding them directly. The architecture separates the **knowledge base** (the domain-specific rules and facts) from the **inference engine** (the general-purpose program that matches rules against a working set of facts and derives new conclusions). This separation was the key engineering insight — the same inference engine could, in principle, run many different knowledge bases.

The canonical example is MYCIN, developed at Stanford, which diagnosed bacterial infections and recommended antibiotics. A rule might read: "IF the organism's identity is unknown AND the infection is primary bacteremia AND the site of the culture is one of the sterile sites AND the suspected portal of entry is the gastrointestinal tract, THEN there is suggestive evidence that the organism is bacteroides." MYCIN chained hundreds of such rules through **backward chaining**: starting from a goal (identify the organism, recommend a drug), it worked backward, asking the physician for facts needed to satisfy each rule's conditions, and it attached a numeric certainty factor to each conclusion to represent partial confidence rather than binary truth.

The commercial breakthrough came with XCON (originally R1), built for Digital Equipment Corporation to configure VAX computer orders. Given a customer's list of components, XCON used forward chaining — starting from known facts and firing rules whenever their conditions were met — to determine which additional parts were needed and how they should be arranged, saving DEC millions of dollars annually in configuration errors.

Problem-solving practice with this paradigm means writing a knowledge base as a rule set and tracing execution by hand: given facts {A, B} and rules "A ∧ B → C" and "C → D," a forward-chaining engine fires the first rule to add C, then the second to add D, terminating when no new facts can be derived. This exercise — simulating the inference engine's fixed-point computation — is the practical skill the era demanded: expertise not as intuition, but as a traceable chain of rule firings.

### backprop_1986 (0.0s)

## Backprop 1986

Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes how a small change in each weight of a multi-layer neural network affects the network's total error, so that every weight can be adjusted to reduce that error. Although the underlying mathematics — the chain rule of calculus — had been known for centuries, Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams's 1986 paper demonstrated that it could be applied efficiently and systematically to train networks with hidden layers, something earlier single-layer perceptrons could not do.

A neural network computes an output through a chain of layers: each layer takes a weighted sum of its inputs, applies a nonlinear activation function, and passes the result forward. If $L$ is the loss (error) measured at the output, and $w_{ij}$ is a weight connecting neuron $i$ to neuron $j$, backpropagation computes $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}}$ for every weight in the network. It does this by first running the network forward to get the output, then propagating the error backward, layer by layer, using the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_j} \cdot \frac{\partial a_j}{\partial z_j} \cdot \frac{\partial z_j}{\partial w_{ij}}$$

Here $z_j$ is neuron $j$'s weighted input and $a_j$ is its activated output. Each factor is easy to compute locally, and the "error signal" $\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_j}$ at one layer is expressed in terms of the error signals already computed at the layer above it — this reuse is what makes the algorithm efficient: rather than recomputing derivatives from scratch for each weight, backprop computes them all in a single backward pass, with a cost proportional to just one extra forward pass through the network.

Consider a network trying to classify handwritten digits. After a forward pass, suppose it assigns too much confidence to the wrong digit. Backpropagation traces that error backward: a weight in the output layer connected strongly to the wrong answer gets a large negative adjustment; a weight in an early hidden layer that only weakly influenced the mistake gets a smaller nudge, in proportion to its actual causal contribution to the error. This weight-by-weight, layer-by-layer attribution is precisely what stumped earlier researchers, since hidden-layer weights have no directly observable "correct" target — backprop solves this by deriving their error signal mathematically rather than requiring one to be specified by hand, which is why it made deep, multi-layer learning practical for the first time.

## Deep Blue 1997

In May 1997, IBM's Deep Blue defeated reigning world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match — the first time a computer beat a top human player under tournament conditions. Deep Blue's intelligence had nothing to do with learning: it was a purpose-built brute-force search engine, evaluating roughly 200 million chess positions per second using custom hardware chips designed specifically for chess move generation.

**How brute-force search works.** Chess is a finite, fully observable game: from any position, a fixed set of legal moves leads to new positions, forming a tree. In principle, an exhaustive search of this tree — the minimax algorithm — finds the optimal move by assuming both players play perfectly, alternating between maximizing the searcher's advantage and minimizing the opponent's. The problem is scale: chess has an estimated $10^{120}$ possible game sequences, far beyond exhaustive enumeration. Deep Blue's engineers addressed this with alpha-beta pruning, which discards branches of the search tree that cannot influence the final decision, and with a hand-crafted evaluation function — a weighted scoring formula built from human chess knowledge (material balance, king safety, piece mobility) — applied at the search depth limit rather than at a true endgame.

**Worked example.** Suppose Deep Blue is choosing between two candidate moves. Move A leads to a position evaluated at +2.3 (favoring Deep Blue) but exposes a line where, several moves later, the opponent forces a queen trade dropping the evaluation to +0.5. Move B leads to a steadier +1.8 with no such vulnerability. Minimax with sufficient look-ahead depth would prefer B, because it correctly assumes Kasparov plays the strongest response, not a careless one. Alpha-beta pruning lets the engine skip evaluating deeper continuations of clearly inferior branches, so the same 200 million positions per second could reach a much greater effective search depth — often 6 to 20 ply depending on position complexity.

**Why this matters historically.** Deep Blue's victory demonstrated that raw computational search, paired with domain-specific engineering and no statistical learning at all, could exceed elite human performance in a narrow, well-defined domain — a sharp contrast to the learning-based approach of backpropagation. This distinction — search versus learning — became a central organizing axis for later AI systems, including hybrid approaches like AlphaGo, which combined tree search with learned evaluation functions.
