### heart_governs_spirit (23.6s)

## The Heart Governs the Spirit (心主神明)

You've already seen that in TCM an organ name stands for a whole functional system, not just one piece of anatomy. The heart is the clearest example. TCM holds that the heart pumps blood and also "houses the spirit" (*shen*, 神).

**What "spirit" covers.** In TCM, *shen* is a broad word. It covers consciousness, clear thinking, memory, emotional steadiness and restful sleep. TCM holds that heart blood nourishes the spirit. When the heart system is described as healthy, a person sleeps well, thinks clearly, speaks coherently and has bright, alert eyes. When it is described as disturbed, TCM looks for signs like these:

| Sign TCM reads as "spirit disturbed" | What the person notices |
|---|---|
| Insomnia or dream-heavy sleep | Trouble falling or staying asleep |
| Palpitations | Feeling the heartbeat race or flutter |
| Restlessness, anxiety | Can't settle or focus |
| Poor memory | Forgetting things |

**What modern science says.** Physiology places consciousness, thinking and sleep control in the brain, not the heart. So this idea is a historical framework, not a description of anatomy. Still, the people who first described it were noticing real links. When you're anxious, your heart rate rises. The brain is also completely dependent on blood from the heart: it makes up only about 2% of body weight but receives roughly 15% of cardiac output. If blood flow to the brain stops, a person loses consciousness within about 10 seconds.

**The pattern, in words:** TCM treats the state of the mind (sleep, clarity, mood) as a readout of the heart system. Modern medicine explains the same connection through blood flow to the brain and through the nervous system's control of heart rate.

**Practice problem**

1. A resting adult's cardiac output is about 5 L/min. If the brain receives about 15% of it, how many liters of blood reach the brain each minute? (Hint: $5 \times 0.15 = ?$)
2. Which of these would TCM read as signs of the heart's spirit: waking up repeatedly at night, a sprained ankle, trouble concentrating, a runny nose?

### spirit_shen (15.1s)

## Spirit (Shen, 神)

You may already know that, in TCM, a person's "spirit" is something a practitioner can see. A bright look in someone's eyes or a quick, clear answer counts as a sign of good spirit. Let's look more closely at what the idea covers.

**What shen includes.** TCM holds that shen covers three related things:

1. **Consciousness**: being awake, alert and aware of your surroundings.
2. **Mental activity**: thinking, memory, emotions and sleep.
3. **Outward vitality**: how much life shows in the eyes, face, voice and movement.

**Where it is said to live.** TCM holds that shen is "housed" in the Heart and fed by the blood. That is why a TCM practitioner may link restless sleep, vivid dreams or anxiety to the Heart. Modern physiology places consciousness and mood in the brain, not the heart. The heart link is a traditional way of grouping symptoms, not an anatomical fact.

**How it is assessed.** Practitioners look for signs and sort them roughly like this:

| Sign | "Spirit present" | "Spirit lacking" |
|---|---|---|
| Eyes | bright, focused | dull, unfocused |
| Face | clear color, expressive | pale or blank |
| Speech | clear and logical | slow or confused |
| Behavior | responsive, steady | listless or agitated |

**The pattern in words.** In TCM, shen is judged by observation. No single number measures it. Several signs that point the same way carry more weight than any one sign alone. Many of these signs overlap with things modern medicine also checks, such as alertness, orientation and mood, which can reflect fatigue, illness or depression.

(b) Name one ordinary physical reason, outside TCM, that could explain all three signs.

### form_spirit_unity (17.0s)

## Form and Spirit as One (形神合一)

You've probably noticed that a bad night's sleep makes you grumpy, and that nerves before a test can upset your stomach. TCM puts this everyday experience at the center of its view of health. In TCM, form (形), meaning the physical body, and spirit (神), meaning a person's mind, emotions and alertness, are treated as one system. Neither is cared for on its own.

**How the idea is built.** TCM describes the link as running in both directions:

| Direction | TCM description | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| Body → spirit | The body is the "house" of the spirit. A well-nourished body keeps the spirit clear and steady. | Skipping meals leaves you foggy and irritable. |
| Spirit → body | Strong or long-lasting emotions affect the body. | Long-term worry goes along with poor appetite and tense shoulders. |
| Spirit shows on the body | A practitioner reads the spirit from the eyes, voice, posture and complexion. | A tired person's eyes look dull. |

Modern physiology supports the general point that mind and body affect each other. Stress hormones raise heart rate and blood pressure, and regular exercise improves mood. It does not support TCM's specific explanations, such as the spirit being "housed" in the heart organ.

**The pattern, in words.** When you look at a health problem, check both sides. Ask what is happening in the body and what is happening in the mind, and how each might be feeding the other. You can often measure the body side directly. A typical resting heart rate for a teenager is about 60–100 beats per minute, and anxiety can push it noticeably higher.

### common_mental_disorders (13.7s)

## Depression and Anxiety Basics

Everyone feels sad, worried, or stressed sometimes. Those feelings are normal reactions to hard situations, like a failed test or a fight with a friend. Depression and anxiety are different. They are **medical conditions**. The feelings last longer, run deeper, and get in the way of daily life.

**How doctors tell the difference.** Mental health professionals look at three things:

| Feature | Ordinary sadness or worry | Possible disorder |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | Hours to a few days | Most days, for **2 weeks or more** (depression) or **several months** (generalized anxiety) |
| **Intensity** | Uncomfortable but manageable | Overwhelming, hard to shake |
| **Impact** | Life goes on mostly as usual | Grades, friendships, sleep, or eating are affected |

**Warning signs of depression** include persistent low mood, loss of interest in things you used to enjoy, changes in sleep or appetite, low energy, trouble concentrating, and feelings of hopelessness or worthlessness.

**Warning signs of anxiety** include constant worry that's hard to control, restlessness, a racing heart, muscle tension, trouble sleeping, and avoiding situations out of fear.

**The key pattern:** one sign alone usually isn't a disorder. A doctor looks for a *cluster* of signs that lasts over time and interferes with life. That's why diagnosis is done by trained professionals, not by checklists online.

**The good news:** both conditions are common and treatable. Talk therapy, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), regular exercise, good sleep, and sometimes medication all help. Most people improve with treatment. If you or someone you know has thoughts of self-harm, tell a trusted adult right away or contact a crisis line (in the U.S., call or text **988**).

**Practice problem:** Maya has felt down and uninterested in soccer for three weeks. She's sleeping much more than usual and her grades are dropping. Jordan felt sad for two days after losing a game, then bounced back. Using the three features in the table, explain which situation is more concerning, and name one step Maya could take to get help.

### five_spirits_shen_hun_po_yi_zhi (17.7s)

## The Five Spirits (五神)

You already know that TCM links each of the five zang organs to an element, a season, and an emotion. TCM goes one step further and gives each organ a "spirit," meaning a type of mental activity it is said to house. Together these are called the five spirits.

Here is the standard set:

| Spirit | Organ | Mental function in TCM | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shen (神) | Heart | Overall awareness and clarity of mind; it leads the other four | Feeling alert and "present" |
| Hun (魂) | Liver | Imagination, planning, dreams, sense of direction | Picturing your future career |
| Po (魄) | Lung | Bodily feeling, instinct, reflexes | Flinching from a hot stove |
| Yi (意) | Spleen | Focused thinking, studying, intention | Working through a math problem |
| Zhi (志) | Kidney | Willpower, determination, long-term memory | Training for months before a race |

There is a clear pattern here. Shen sits at the top as the "ruler," and the other four each cover one part of mental life. Hun and po form a pair. Hun is the light, outward-looking side (dreams and plans), and po is the grounded, physical side (sensations and reflexes). TCM holds that when an organ is out of balance, its spirit suffers too. For example, too much worrying is said to strain the spleen and make it hard to concentrate, which is a problem for yi.

One honest note: modern neuroscience shows that awareness, planning, reflexes, focus and memory are all produced by the brain and nervous system. There is no evidence that the liver or kidney store mental functions. Treat the five spirits as a historical way of sorting the mind into categories, not as a description of anatomy.

**Rule in words:** each zang organ is paired with one spirit, and shen in the heart coordinates the rest.

**Practice problem:** Maya stays up late cramming for a test. She can't focus, she keeps forgetting what she read, and she has vivid, restless dreams. According to TCM, which two or three spirits would be described as affected? For each one, name its organ. Then write one sentence on what a neuroscientist would say is actually going on.

### interoception (14.2s)

## Interoception

You already know that your body sends you signals: a pounding heart before a test, a growling stomach before lunch. **Interoception** is the name for how your brain senses and makes sense of those signals from *inside* the body.

**How the system works**

Interoception has three parts:

1. **Sensors.** Nerve endings in the heart, lungs, gut, blood vessels and muscles detect stretch, pressure, chemistry and temperature.
2. **Pathways.** Signals travel to the brain, mostly through the vagus nerve and the spinal cord.
3. **Interpretation.** A brain region called the **insula** combines these signals into feelings such as "I'm hungry," "I'm tense" or "I'm calm."

The interpretation step is where emotion comes in. A fast heartbeat is just a body signal. The context decides what it means. Before a race, your brain might read it as excitement. Before a speech, it might read it as fear. Many psychologists think emotions are partly built from this kind of interpretation.

**How it is measured**

One common lab test is the **heartbeat counting task**. You silently count your heartbeats for a set time without touching your pulse, while a monitor records the real count. Researchers then score your accuracy:

$$
\text{Accuracy} = 1 - \frac{\left|\,n_{\text{actual}} - n_{\text{reported}}\,\right|}{n_{\text{actual}}}
$$

A score of 1 means a perfect count, and lower scores mean bigger errors. People differ a lot on this test. Researchers also debate how well it measures interoception, because some people simply estimate their count instead of feeling each beat.

**The pattern to remember:** your body sends a signal, your brain interprets it, and you feel something. Learning to notice these signals, for example by slowing your breathing, can change how strongly you feel stress.

### mindfulness_meditation (13.8s)

## Mindfulness and Meditation

You may already know mindfulness as "paying attention to the present moment." That's the core idea. At this level, it's worth looking at what that training actually involves and what the research shows it can and can't do.

**The structure of a practice.** Most mindfulness meditation follows a repeating loop with four steps:

1. **Anchor:** pick a focus, such as the feeling of breath at the nostrils or belly.
2. **Wander:** sooner or later, the mind drifts to a worry, a plan or a memory. This is normal. It is not failure.
3. **Notice:** you catch that your attention has moved.
4. **Return:** you bring your attention back gently, without scolding yourself.

The "non-judging" part matters. Each time you notice and return, you are practicing the real skill. A session where your mind wandered 30 times and you came back 30 times is a productive session. Some styles widen the focus to body sensations or sounds. Others, like loving-kindness meditation, focus on kind intentions toward yourself and other people.

**What the evidence says.** Many randomized controlled trials, including programs like Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), show *modest* improvements in stress, anxiety, low mood and sleep. When mindfulness is compared with other active treatments, such as exercise or certain kinds of therapy, the benefits are often similar rather than better. It is a useful tool, not a cure. It does not replace medical care for serious depression or anxiety.

**A simple pattern.** Short sessions done regularly tend to work better than rare long ones. Many beginner programs start with 5–10 minutes a day and build up slowly. Standard MBSR courses run 8 weeks.

(b) During one 8-minute session, she notices her mind wandered 12 times and brings it back each time. Using the four-step loop, explain why this counts as a successful session and not a failed one.

### body_to_mind_pathways (16.7s)

## Body-to-Mind Pathways

You've probably felt this already. Slumping makes you feel heavier, and a few slow breaths before a test take some of the edge off. That isn't only in your head. Your body is always sending reports to your brain.

**Reading the body from the inside**

Scientists call the brain's sensing of the body's internal state **interoception**. Nerves carry steady signals upward about how fast your heart is beating, how deep your breathing is, how tight your muscles are and how your gut feels. A major route is the **vagus nerve**, which runs from the brainstem down to the heart, lungs and digestive organs. Most of its fibres carry information *to* the brain, not away from it.

Your brain uses these reports to decide how alarmed or calm to be. A racing heart and fast, shallow breathing read as "danger." Slow breathing and loose shoulders read as "safe." So the signals run in both directions: the mind affects the body, and the body also shapes the mind.

**Breath is the easiest lever**

You can't directly order your heart to slow down, but you can control your breath. Heart rate naturally rises a little when you breathe in and falls when you breathe out. This is called *respiratory sinus arrhythmia*. Long, slow exhales take advantage of that dip. Many relaxation methods aim for about 6 breaths per minute. The breathing rate is simply

$$
\text{breaths per minute} = \frac{60\ \text{s}}{\text{inhale time} + \text{exhale time}}
$$

TCM describes the same link as 形神合一, the unity of body and spirit. Interoception research gives a modern, testable account of the idea that caring for the body helps settle the mind.

(c) Which pattern gives her longer exhales, and why might that help her feel calmer?

### brain_extraordinary_organs (16.4s)

## The Brain and the Extraordinary Organs (奇恒之腑)

You may already know that TCM sorts the body's main organs into two families: the *zang* organs, which store precious substances, and the *fu* organs, which are hollow and keep food and fluids moving. Some body parts did not fit either family, so TCM gave them a third category of their own.

**Exploring the structure**

The six **extraordinary organs** (奇恒之腑) are the brain, marrow, bones, blood vessels, gallbladder and uterus. TCM describes them as a hybrid. Most are shaped like *fu* organs, hollow or tube-like, but they work like *zang* organs because they store essence instead of passing material through. The gallbladder belongs to both lists: it is one of the six *fu*, and it counts as extraordinary because TCM says it stores bile, a "clear" fluid.

| Feature | *Zang* | *Fu* | Extraordinary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical shape | Solid | Hollow | Mostly hollow |
| Main job | Store | Transport | Store |

In TCM, the brain is called the **sea of marrow** (髓海). The marrow fills the spine and gathers in the skull, which is why the brain is linked to the kidneys, the organ TCM says produces marrow. Later writers, most famously Li Shizhen in the 1500s, called the brain the **residence of the primal spirit** (元神之府). Even so, the core theory kept thinking and feeling with the heart.

**The key pattern**

TCM groups organs by what they *do* (store or transport) more than by what they look like. On the mind, modern neuroscience is clear that thought, memory and emotion come from the brain, not the heart. The traditional placement of the mind in the heart is simply wrong.

### five_spirits_and_organs (18.6s)

## Five Spirits and Five Organs (五神脏)

You've already seen how TCM links each of the five organs to a phase, a season, and an emotion. The idea of the five spirits takes this one step further. TCM holds that each organ also "houses" one side of the mind. The mind is not treated as one single thing. It is split into five faces that work together.

Here is the structure:

| Spirit | Organ | Face of mind | Common signs of imbalance (in TCM) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shen** (神) | Heart | Overall awareness, the "leader" of the others | Restlessness, poor sleep, scattered attention |
| **Hun** (魂) | Liver | Vision, planning, imagination, sense of direction | Indecision, aimlessness, irritability |
| **Po** (魄) | Lung | Bodily instinct, physical sensation | Lingering grief, shallow breathing |
| **Yi** (意) | Spleen | Thought, focus, studying | Overthinking, worry, mental fog |
| **Zhi** (志) | Kidney | Will, determination, drive | Fear, low motivation, giving up easily |

The shen comes first in this list because TCM treats the heart as the ruler of the organs. When the shen is settled, the other four can do their jobs.

A note on evidence: modern neuroscience places planning, thinking, and willpower in the brain, not in the liver, spleen, or kidneys. Read the five spirits as a traditional way of *describing* mental life, not as a map of where the mind physically lives.

**The pattern, in words:** in TCM, each spirit matches the emotion of its organ. The yi (spleen) goes with worry, the zhi (kidney) with fear, and the hun (liver) with frustration. So a TCM practitioner who hears about one problem, such as constant worry, will look at the organ and spirit paired with it.

### sleep_architecture (18.2s)

## Sleep Architecture

You already know sleep isn't one long, flat blackout. Over the night your brain moves through different stages in a repeating pattern, and that pattern is called **sleep architecture**.

**The stages.** Sleep scientists divide sleep into non-REM and REM sleep:

| Stage | What's happening |
|---|---|
| N1 (light) | Drifting off; easy to wake |
| N2 (light) | Heart rate and body temperature drop; about half of the night is spent here |
| N3 (deep) | Slow brain waves; the body does physical repair and growth hormone is released |
| REM | Brain is very active, vivid dreams, muscles are temporarily relaxed; important for memory and emotion |

You pass through N1 → N2 → N3 → N2 → REM in one **cycle** that lasts about 90 minutes, and you repeat that cycle 4–6 times a night. The cycles change as the night goes on. Most deep sleep comes in the first few cycles, and REM periods get longer toward morning. That's why cutting sleep short mostly costs you REM.

**The clock behind it.** Your **circadian rhythm** is an internal clock that runs on roughly 24 hours. It's set mainly by light, and in the evening it signals melatonin to rise so you feel sleepy. In teenagers this clock naturally shifts later, which is why falling asleep early can feel hard.

**Sleep and mood.** Poor sleep makes you more irritable and makes stress harder to handle. Stress and low mood also make it harder to fall asleep, so each one can make the other worse.

**Two useful measures:**

$$
\text{Number of cycles} \approx \frac{\text{total sleep time}}{90\ \text{min}}
\qquad
\text{Sleep efficiency} = \frac{\text{time asleep}}{\text{time in bed}} \times 100\%
$$

A sleep efficiency of 85% or higher is generally considered healthy.

**Practice problem:** Maya gets into bed at 10:30 pm, takes 15 minutes to fall asleep, and wakes at 6:45 am. (a) How many minutes was she asleep? (b) What is her sleep efficiency? (c) About how many full sleep cycles did she complete?

### depression_in_tcm_and_biomedicine (16.9s)

## Low Mood in TCM and Biomedicine (郁证)

Everyone has gloomy days, like after a bad test or a fight with a friend. Usually that mood lifts within a few days. Sometimes it doesn't. Doctors and traditional practitioners describe that longer-lasting low mood in two different vocabularies.

**Biomedicine** sorts low mood into named disorders using checklists. Doctors diagnose **major depressive disorder** when a person has at least 5 of 9 listed symptoms for at least 2 weeks. One of those symptoms must be either a depressed mood or a loss of interest in things the person used to enjoy. Other symptoms on the list include changes in sleep or appetite, tiredness, trouble concentrating, and feelings of worthlessness. Anxiety disorders are diagnosed in a similar way, by counting persistent worry and its effects on daily life. A standard questionnaire called the PHQ-9 gives each of the 9 symptoms a score from 0 to 3, so the total runs from 0 to 27. Higher totals suggest more severe depression.

**TCM** groups low mood under 郁证 ("stagnation syndrome") and describes patterns rather than disorders:

| TCM pattern | Typical description |
|---|---|
| Liver-qi stagnation | irritability, sighing, a tight chest, moods that swing with stress |
| Heart–spleen deficiency | worry, poor sleep, tiredness, poor appetite |

These patterns are not backed by biological evidence. Their practical use is organizing lifestyle care such as sleep routines, exercise, regular meals, and talking things through.

**The key rule:** either vocabulary can help describe how someone feels. But low mood that lasts two weeks or more, gets in the way of school or friendships, or comes with thoughts of self-harm needs assessment by a doctor or mental-health professional. A TCM pattern label does not take the place of that assessment.

### heart_and_brain_two_models (16.7s)

## Heart and Brain — Two Models of Mind

You've probably heard people say "I know it in my heart" or "my heart is racing with worry." Both modern science and TCM link feelings to the body, but they put the thinking mind in different places.

**Two maps, two questions.** Modern neuroscience places the mind in the brain. Thoughts, memories and emotions come from networks of about 86 billion neurons, and the brain uses roughly 20% of the body's resting energy even though it is only about 2% of body weight. The evidence is strong. Damage to one brain region changes speech, memory or personality in predictable ways. People who receive a heart transplant still think and remember as themselves. As a claim about anatomy, the TCM idea that the heart "houses the mind" is not correct.

TCM holds that the Heart (written with a capital H) "governs the blood vessels and houses the shen," or spirit. In this system, the Heart is less a pump and more a label for a group of functions that tend to show up together:

| TCM "Heart" function | What modern physiology connects it to |
|---|---|
| Houses the mind (shen) | Brain: attention, mood, consciousness |
| Governs sleep | Brain sleep centres, stress hormones |
| Shows emotion (joy, anxiety) | Brain emotion circuits plus the nervous system's effect on heart rate |
| Governs blood vessels | Heart and circulation |

**The pattern to remember.** Read the TCM Heart as a function map: a list of experiences that seem connected (restless sleep, a pounding pulse, anxiety). Don't read it as a rival anatomy. The connection people feel is real. Stress signals from the brain speed up the heart, which is why worry can make your heart pound. The cause, though, starts in the brain.

### heart_kidney_and_sleep (15.9s)

## Heart–Kidney Disharmony and Sleep

You've already seen that TCM pairs the heart with fire and the kidneys with water. TCM holds that at night these two should meet: the heart's warmth sinks downward and the kidneys' coolness rises, so the spirit (*shen*) can settle and you fall asleep. When the two fail to connect, a state called *heart–kidney disharmony* (心肾不交), the spirit stays "up," and the result is insomnia.

**How the idea is structured.** TCM describes this pattern through a cluster of signs that tend to show up together:

| TCM sign | What a person might notice |
|---|---|
| Heart fire rising | Restlessness, racing thoughts, palpitations |
| Kidney water depleted | Night sweats, warm palms and soles, dry mouth |
| Spirit unsettled | Trouble falling asleep, waking often, vivid dreams |

These are descriptions, not measurements. There's no clinical evidence that the heart and kidneys literally regulate sleep this way. Still, the description lines up with something modern science calls *hyperarousal*: the stress system (high cortisol, fast heart rate) stays switched on at bedtime, and the brain can't hand control over to sleep.

**A useful rule from sleep science.** Doctors judge sleep quality partly by *sleep efficiency*, which is the share of time in bed that you actually spend asleep:

$$
\text{Sleep efficiency} = \frac{\text{total time asleep}}{\text{total time in bed}} \times 100\%
$$

Healthy sleepers usually score about 85% or higher. The first-line treatment for long-term insomnia is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which includes calming the mind before bed and keeping wake times regular. In practice, that does a lot of what "settling the heart fire" tries to describe.

**Practice problem.** Maya goes to bed at 10:00 p.m. and gets up at 6:30 a.m. It takes her 45 minutes to fall asleep, and she lies awake for another 25 minutes during the night.

1. How long was she in bed, and how long was she asleep?
2. What is her sleep efficiency, to the nearest percent?
3. Is it above or below the healthy 85% mark?

### moving_meditation_baduanjin (16.5s)

## Baduanjin as Moving Meditation

You may already know Baduanjin, the "Eight Pieces of Brocade," as a set of eight gentle exercises. Its traditional description is *dong zhong qiu jing* (动中求静), "seeking stillness within movement." The body moves, but the mind settles down.

**How it's built.** Sitting meditation trains attention by keeping you still and focused on one thing, often the breath. Baduanjin does the same attention training while you move. Each of the eight movements, such as "Holding Up the Sky with Both Hands," is linked to three things:

| Layer | What you do |
|---|---|
| Movement | Slow, smooth, continuous motions with no jerks |
| Breath | Inhale as the arms rise or open, exhale as they lower or close |
| Attention | Notice your posture, muscle stretch and breath, and gently come back when your mind wanders |

In TCM, these movements are said to guide the flow of qi. That is a traditional explanation, not a measured quantity. What researchers can measure is breathing rate, heart rate and balance. Small clinical trials, mostly in older adults, suggest modest benefits for balance, flexibility and mood, but many of the studies are small or loosely controlled.

**The pattern.** Breathing slows down because each breath is matched to one slow movement. Breathing rate has a simple definition:

$$
\text{breathing rate} = \frac{\text{number of breaths}}{\text{time in minutes}}
$$

A resting teenager or adult usually breathes about 12–20 times per minute. During Baduanjin, breathing often slows to around 6–10 breaths per minute. A slow, steady rhythm like that tends to come with a calmer, more focused state.

2. Is it above, within, or below the typical resting range?
3. In one sentence, explain why matching each breath to a slow movement might lower her breathing rate.

### when_to_seek_professional_help (15.2s)

## When to Seek Professional Help

Gentle practices like Baduanjin, regular sleep, and time outdoors can help you feel steadier day to day. But they work like a good pair of running shoes: helpful, and still not a cast for a broken leg. Knowing when to ask a professional for help is a skill in its own right.

**Exploring the idea**

Everyone has bad days. Feeling sad after a lost game or anxious before a test is normal. Doctors and counselors look at three things to tell an ordinary low patch from something that needs care:

| Signal | What to watch for |
|---|---|
| **Duration** | Low mood, worry, poor sleep, or loss of interest that lasts **more than two weeks** |
| **Impact** | Trouble keeping up with school, work, friendships, or family life |
| **Safety** | Thoughts of self-harm, feeling hopeless, or feeling that nothing will get better |

A single signal can be enough. The safety signal never waits for the two-week mark. It needs attention right away.

**The rule, in words**

If symptoms last beyond two weeks *or* get in the way of daily life, talk to a trusted adult, a school counselor, or a doctor. If someone is in danger *right now*, contact local emergency or crisis services immediately. In the United States you can call or text **988**, and most countries have a similar line. Baduanjin and other self-care practices can *support* treatment, but they are not a replacement for it.

Asking for help is not weakness. It's the same decision you'd make if a sprained ankle kept hurting after two weeks.

## The Daily Mind–Body Practice (养神)

You've already met the pieces: sleep habits, Baduanjin, seated meditation (静坐), and breath awareness. Now let's put them together into one daily plan that belongs to you. In TCM, caring for the mind and spirit is called 养神, "nourishing the spirit." Here we treat it as a practical routine you can test and adjust.

**How the plan is built.** A good practice has layers. Some parts happen every day, and some only when they help:

| Layer | What it looks like | How often |
|---|---|---|
| Wind-down and sleep | Screens off 30–60 min before bed, steady bedtime | Nightly |
| Moving meditation | One Baduanjin session (about 10–15 min) | Most days |
| Seated meditation (静坐) | 5–10 min sitting quietly | Whatever suits you; a few times a week still helps |
| Breath or body check | 2–3 min of slow breathing or a body scan | Daily, anytime |
| Mood check-in | Rate your mood 1–5 and write one word | Daily |

Teenagers need about 8–10 hours of sleep a night, and students aged 6–12 need about 9–12, so sleep is the base layer the rest is built on. Research on mindfulness and gentle movement such as Baduanjin shows modest benefits for stress and sleep. These practices support your health but don't replace medical care.

**A simple rule.** Start small, write down what you do, and adjust every week or two. Your mood log is data. If you see a pattern, such as better sleep on days you practiced, keep doing that part.

**When to get help.** Talk to a parent, school counselor, or doctor if low mood, worry, or poor sleep lasts most days for **two weeks or more**, or gets in the way of school or friendships. If you ever have thoughts of hurting yourself, tell a trusted adult or call a crisis line **right away**.
