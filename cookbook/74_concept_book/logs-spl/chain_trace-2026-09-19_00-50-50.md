## Natural Selection

Heritable variation exists in every population: individuals differ in traits, and those differences trace back to differences in DNA sequence, ultimately produced by mutation and reshuffled each generation through cell division and reproduction. Natural selection is what happens when that variation meets an environment that doesn't treat all variants equally. If some heritable trait affects how well an organism survives and reproduces, individuals carrying favorable variants leave more offspring, and the alleles underlying those variants become more common in the next generation. Selection is not a force pushing populations toward a goal — it's a filter, removing and promoting existing variation based on reproductive success. It cannot create new alleles; mutation does that. Selection only changes how common existing alleles are.

**Worked example.** Consider a beetle population where shell color is controlled by a single gene: a dark allele ($D$) and a light allele ($d$). Suppose dark beetles are more visible to birds on light-colored bark, so dark beetles survive to reproduce at a lower rate than light beetles. If the dark allele starts at frequency $p = 0.6$ and, after one generation of selective predation, survivors carrying $D$ produce fewer offspring on average, $p$ decreases in the next generation. Note what's required for this to work: (1) variation in color must exist, (2) that variation must be heritable (encoded in the $D/d$ alleles, not just diet or exposure), and (3) it must cause a real difference in survival or reproduction. Remove any one of these three conditions and allele frequency won't shift, no matter how strong the environmental pressure looks.

**Problem-solving application.** Biologists use this three-condition checklist to diagnose real cases. If a population of insecticide-resistant beetles emerges after spraying, ask: was resistance present before spraying (standing variation, possibly from a prior mutation), is resistance heritable, and did resistant beetles survive and reproduce more? If all three hold, allele frequencies for resistance genes should rise measurably across generations — a testable prediction, not just a plausible story. This same checklist distinguishes true selection from other causes of frequency change, such as genetic drift (random sampling in small populations) or migration, which shift allele frequencies without any consistent fitness advantage.
