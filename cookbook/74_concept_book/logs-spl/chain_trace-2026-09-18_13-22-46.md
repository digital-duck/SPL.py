## Suffix

A suffix is a bound morpheme attached to the right edge of a stem that determines the lexical category — noun, verb, adjective, adverb — of the resulting word, and often adds a systematic layer of meaning tied to that category. In English morphology, suffixation is the primary mechanism of derivation: it is the engine that converts a verb like *act* into a noun (*action*), an agent noun (*actor*), or an adjective (*actionable*), each transformation governed by a suffix that carries its own selectional restrictions on what kind of stem it will attach to and what category it produces.

**Mechanism.** A derivational suffix operates as a category-changing (or category-preserving, in some cases) function applied to a base:

$$
\underbrace{act}_{\text{verb stem}} + \underbrace{\text{-ion}}_{\text{nominalizer}} = \text{action} \quad(\text{noun: "the act of"})
$$

$$
\underbrace{act}_{\text{verb stem}} + \underbrace{\text{-or}}_{\text{agentive}} = \text{actor} \quad(\text{noun: "the doer"})
$$

$$
\underbrace{act}_{\text{verb stem}} + \underbrace{\text{-able}}_{\text{potential/capability}} = \text{actionable} \quad(\text{adjective, via the derived noun})
$$

Each suffix imposes phonological and morphological constraints on the base it accepts — *-ion* typically attaches to Latinate verb stems (*create → creation*, not *\*singion*), *-able* prefers transitive verbs (*readable*, *breakable*), and *-or* competes with the near-synonymous *-er* along lines partly historical (Latinate vs. Germanic stems) and partly lexicalized on a word-by-word basis (*sailor* vs. *worker*).

**Literature context.** Generative morphology (Aronoff 1976; Marchand 1969) treats suffixation as rule-governed word formation, but the rules are notoriously leaky: productivity is gradient rather than categorical. Baayen and Lieber's corpus-based work on morphological productivity quantifies this by measuring how often a suffix generates novel (hapax legomenon) forms in large corpora, showing that *-ness* is far more productive than *-ity* despite both nominalizing adjectives. What remains open is why certain suffixes stall in productivity — blocked by an existing irregular form (*\*gloriosity* blocked by *glory*), by phonological mismatch, or by semantic drift that makes the derived form unpredictable from its parts (*dresser* as furniture, not "one who dresses").

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could measure the productivity of two or three suffixes (e.g., *-ness* vs. *-ity*, or *-able* vs. *-ible*) across a diachronic corpus (COHA or Google Ngrams), tracking hapax-based productivity scores by decade, and cross-referencing against etymological source (native vs. Latinate stems) to test whether stem origin predicts suffix choice and productivity trend over time.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the productivity measure used and why (type/token ratios are insufficient; hapax-conditioned measures are standard since Baayen), report results per suffix per period with confidence intervals appropriate to corpus sampling, and explicitly address any blocking effects observed (cases where a productive suffix failed to apply because a lexicalized competitor already occupied that semantic slot) — since blocking, not just phonological fit, is often the deciding factor in whether a suffix attaches.

## -Able

**Definition.** *-able* (with its orthographic variant *-ible*, distributed by etymology rather than rule) is a derivational suffix that attaches to a verb stem to form an adjective meaning "capable of undergoing X" or, less commonly, "capable of performing X." *Washable* means capable of being washed; *readable* means capable of being read; *knowledgeable* is an outlier, built on a noun rather than a verb, showing that the suffix's productivity has outrun its historical base. The core semantic contribution is modal: it converts an event predicate into a stative property predicate, asserting a potential rather than an actuality.

**Mechanism.** The morphological operation is straightforward affixation to a verb stem, typically with a passive reading of the base verb's object:

$$
\underbrace{wash}_{\text{verb}} + \underbrace{\text{-able}}_{\text{"capable of being V-ed"}} = washable
$$

The interesting mechanism is not the attachment but the argument-structure shift it forces. *Break* the vase (transitive, vase = object) yields *the vase is breakable* (the object becomes the subject of a stative adjective). This is the same subject-promotion pattern seen in passive voice and in middle constructions ("the vase breaks easily"), which is why *-able* formations are sometimes analyzed as adjectival passives rather than simple derivational suffixation. Productivity is high but not unconstrained: the suffix generally requires a transitive base with a well-defined patient (*eatable*, *movable*), resists intransitives (*\*arrivable*), and interacts with negation productively via *un-* (*undoable*, *unbreakable*), giving speakers a compositional way to coin novel forms on the fly.

**Literature context.** Aronoff's (1976) foundational work on word-formation used *-able* as a central case study for the notion of "possible but nonexistent words," distinguishing lexicalized, semantically drifted forms (*readable* meaning "well-written," not just "able to be read") from fully compositional, rule-generated ones (*liftable*). Subsequent work in lexical semantics (Levin & Rappaport 1986) has used *-able* selectivity as a diagnostic for verb classes and argument structure, since only verbs with an affected theme argument admit the suffix felicitously. What remains open is the precise boundary of productivity: corpus and acceptability-judgment studies disagree on how freely native speakers extend *-able* to novel or borrowed verbs, and on whether apparent gaps (*\*sleepable*) are grammatical or merely pragmatic.

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could build a small corpus-based acceptability study: extract a sample of attested and nonce *-able* forms from a large corpus (e.g., COCA or a web-crawled corpus), classify their base verbs by transitivity and thematic-role structure, and run an acceptability-judgment task (Likert-scale or forced-choice) on the nonce forms with native-speaker participants. The goal is to test whether transitivity and patient-affectedness predict acceptability better than frequency of the base verb alone.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the classification scheme for verb bases explicitly (transitive/intransitive, affected/unaffected theme), report inter-rater agreement on that classification, and present the judgment data with effect sizes rather than only significance, since the theoretical claim at stake is a graded productivity constraint, not a categorical rule.
