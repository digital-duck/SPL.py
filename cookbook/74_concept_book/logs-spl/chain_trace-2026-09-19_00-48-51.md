### cell_membrane (0.0s)

## Cell Membrane

Every cell needs a boundary that separates its internal chemistry from the outside world while still allowing controlled exchange with it. The cell membrane performs this job using a **phospholipid bilayer**: two stacked layers of phospholipid molecules, each about 2.5 nm thick, for a total membrane thickness of roughly 5 nm. Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic (water-attracting) phosphate head and two hydrophobic (water-repelling) fatty acid tails. In water, the molecules spontaneously arrange into two sheets with tails pointing inward, away from water, and heads pointing outward, toward the watery cytoplasm on one side and the extracellular fluid on the other. This self-assembly happens without any energy input from the cell — it is driven by the same principle that keeps oil droplets clustered in water.

The bilayer is **selectively permeable**: small nonpolar molecules like O₂ and CO₂ slip through the hydrophobic core easily, but ions and large polar molecules like glucose cannot cross unassisted. This is where membrane proteins come in. Rather than sitting in fixed positions, proteins are embedded throughout the bilayer and can drift laterally — a behavior captured by the **fluid mosaic model**, which pictures proteins floating in the lipid layer like icebergs in the sea. Some proteins form channels or pumps that move specific substances across the membrane; others act as receptors that bind signaling molecules from outside the cell.

**Worked example**: Suppose a drug must reach a target inside a cell. If the drug is polar and charged, it cannot diffuse through the hydrophobic tail region of the bilayer — the membrane blocks it, just as it blocks free ions. Pharmaceutical chemists exploit this by designing drugs with a balance of polar and nonpolar character, or by relying on transport proteins that already recognize a similar natural molecule, to shuttle the drug across.

**Problem-solving application**: If you observe a substance passively crossing a membrane without any protein channel, you can predict it is small and nonpolar (like O₂, CO₂, or ethanol). If crossing requires a specific membrane protein and stops when that protein is genetically knocked out, you can conclude the substance is polar or charged (like glucose or Na⁺), and its transport depends on the protein embedded in the bilayer — a direct application of structure predicting function.

### diffusion (0.0s)

## Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of dissolved solute particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, driven by random molecular motion rather than any directed force. Because individual molecules move randomly, they are simply more likely to wander from a crowded region into an empty one than the reverse — so at the population level, this randomness produces the predictable, directional flow we call diffusion. The process continues until concentration is uniform throughout the system, a state called dynamic equilibrium: molecules still move, but with no *net* directional flow.

**Worked example.** Picture a sealed tank of water in which a spoonful of dye is dropped in one corner. Initially, the dye concentration is high near the drop point and zero elsewhere. Over time, dye molecules spread outward, and the color gradually becomes uniform across the tank. If you tracked concentration $C$ at a fixed point 10 cm from the drop site over several hours, it would rise steadily, then level off once equilibrium is reached — it never reverses and re-concentrates, because there are vastly more ways for molecules to be spread out than clustered together.

**Quantitative treatment.** Because diffusion is a physical transport process governed by a genuine mathematical law, it merits formal treatment: Fick's First Law states that the diffusive flux $J$ (amount of solute crossing a unit area per unit time) is proportional to the concentration gradient:

$$J = -D\frac{dC}{dx}$$

Here $C$ is concentration, $x$ is position, and $D$ is the diffusion coefficient (units of $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$), which depends on the solute, solvent, and temperature. The negative sign captures the essential physics: flux flows *down* the gradient, from high to low concentration.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose glucose diffuses across a thin membrane with $D = 6.7 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}$, membrane thickness $\Delta x = 0.01\ \text{cm}$, and concentrations of $0.5\ \text{mol/L}$ and $0.1\ \text{mol/L}$ on either side. Approximating the gradient as $\Delta C/\Delta x$:

$$J = -D\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = -(6.7\times10^{-6})\left(\frac{0.1-0.5}{0.01}\right) = 2.68\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}\,\text{cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$$

This flux tells a physiologist how quickly glucose crosses a cell membrane passively — a calculation underlying models of nutrient absorption, drug delivery, and cellular respiration.

### electrolyte (0.0s)

## Electrolyte

An electrolyte is a mineral that dissolves in water and separates into charged particles called ions. In the human body, the four electrolytes most responsible for nervous-system function are sodium ($\text{Na}^+$), potassium ($\text{K}^+$), chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$), and calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$). Dissolved in the fluid inside and outside a neuron, these ions are free to move, and because each carries an electric charge, their movement constitutes an electric current — the physical basis of every signal the nervous system produces.

The one new idea to add here is the concentration gradient: a neuron's cell membrane keeps the concentration of each ion different inside versus outside the cell. Sodium and calcium are far more concentrated outside; potassium is far more concentrated inside. This imbalance is maintained by the cell and represents stored potential energy, much like water held behind a dam — energy that is released once a pathway opens for the ions to flow back down their gradient, from where they are abundant to where they are scarce.

That pathway is the membrane's ion channel, a protein that opens or closes in response to a trigger. Consider a concrete case: a neuron at rest has an internal voltage of about $-70\ \text{mV}$ relative to the outside. When a stimulus opens sodium channels, $\text{Na}^+$ ions flood inward down their gradient, and the interior voltage rises rapidly toward $+30\ \text{mV}$ — the depolarization phase of the action potential. Almost immediately after, potassium channels open, $\text{K}^+$ flows outward down its own gradient, and the voltage falls back toward its resting level. This is not a chemical reaction consuming the ions; it's ion traffic redistributing charge across the membrane.

Problem-solving applications of this concept extend well beyond neuroscience: interpreting an ECG or EEG requires recognizing that the recorded voltages are the aggregate electrical signature of ion movement across many cell membranes at once. Clinically, an electrolyte imbalance — too little potassium, for example — directly impairs the ability of neurons and cardiac cells to generate normal electrical signals, which is why blood electrolyte panels are a standard diagnostic tool.

### electrostatic_force (0.0s)

## Electrostatic Force

Electrostatic force is the interaction between charged particles: opposite charges attract, like charges repel. This force is what pulls ions across cell membranes, drives current through wires, and holds atoms together in ionic bonds. Because it is a fundamental force with a precise mathematical description, understanding it requires the actual law that governs it — not just the qualitative rule of "opposites attract."

Coulomb's law quantifies the force between two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by a distance $r$:

$$
F = k_e \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}
$$

where $k_e \approx 8.99 \times 10^9 \ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$ is Coulomb's constant. The force acts along the line connecting the two charges. If $q_1$ and $q_2$ have the same sign, $F$ is positive and the force is repulsive; if they have opposite signs, $F$ is negative and the force is attractive. Notice the inverse-square dependence on $r$: doubling the distance between two charges reduces the force to one-quarter of its original strength.

**Worked example.** Suppose a sodium ion ($\text{Na}^+$, charge $q_1 = +1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) sits $2.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$ from a chloride ion ($\text{Cl}^-$, charge $q_2 = -1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) inside a cell membrane channel. The magnitude of the force between them is:

$$
F = (8.99 \times 10^9) \cdot \frac{(1.6 \times 10^{-19})(1.6 \times 10^{-19})}{(2.0 \times 10^{-9})^2} \approx 5.75 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}
$$

Since the charges are opposite, this force is attractive, pulling the ions toward each other.

**Problem-solving application.** In biology and chemistry, electrostatic force explains why ions move down electrochemical gradients: a positively charged ion is pushed away from regions of net positive charge and pulled toward regions of net negative charge, contributing (alongside diffusion) to the net flow of ions across a membrane. When a problem involves several surrounding charges rather than just one pair, apply Coulomb's law to each pair separately, then combine those individual pulls and pushes into a single overall force — accounting for both their strength and the direction each one acts in — to find the net effect on the ion in question.

### electrical_potential (0.0s)

## Electrical Potential

Every membrane in a living cell is a tiny battery. On one side sits a solution rich in some ion — say, potassium — and on the other, a solution poor in it. Left alone, those ions would diffuse down their concentration gradient, spreading out until evenly mixed. But ions carry charge, and a membrane restricts their movement to specific channels. As positive potassium ions leak out through open channels, they leave behind unbalanced negative charge inside the cell, and the resulting electrostatic pull drags the escaping ions back. The system settles into a standoff: chemical diffusion pushing ions one way, electrostatic attraction pulling them the other. The "pressure" stored in that standoff — the work required to move a unit of charge from one side of the membrane to the other — is the electrical potential, measured in volts (joules per coulomb).

This potential is not abstract; it is directly measurable and directly consequential. A resting neuron holds its interior at about $-70\ \text{mV}$ relative to the outside, meaning roughly 70 millivolts of pent-up driving force sit ready to move charge the instant a channel opens. That voltage is set by the balance of ion gradients, described quantitatively by the Nernst equation:

$$E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{outside}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{inside}}}\right)$$

Here $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is temperature in kelvin, $z$ is the ion's charge, $F$ is Faraday's constant, and the bracketed terms are concentrations on each side. This equation is unavoidable, not decorative: it is the only way to predict the equilibrium voltage for a given ion gradient, since it captures the exact point where the electrical force balances the diffusional one.

Problem-solving application: suppose extracellular potassium concentration is 5 mM and intracellular is 140 mM. At body temperature ($T \approx 310\ \text{K}$), with $z = +1$, plugging into the Nernst equation yields $E_K \approx -91\ \text{mV}$ — close to a real neuron's resting potential, confirming potassium is the dominant driver at rest. The same equation, applied to any other permeant ion, gives that ion's own equilibrium voltage from its own inside/outside concentration ratio — a single calculation you repeat, plugging in different concentrations and charges, to characterize any ion gradient across any membrane. Computing $E_{\text{ion}}$ this way is the standard first step in modeling any excitable-cell circuit.
