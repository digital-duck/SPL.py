## Overt Attention

Overt attention is the process of physically redirecting the eyes and head so that a stimulus falls on the small, densely packed region of the retina responsible for sharp, high-resolution vision. This region spans only about 1–2 degrees of visual angle, so resolving fine detail anywhere else in the visual field requires an eye movement to bring that location into central view. Overt attention is the default mode of visual processing in everyday life: whenever you consciously look *at* something rather than merely sensing it out of the corner of your eye, you are engaging overt attention.

**Worked example.** Consider reading a road sign at a distance. Peripheral vision detects that something rectangular with text exists ahead, but the letters are a blur — the periphery is far too coarse for legibility. The eyes execute a rapid rotation, lasting roughly 20–50 milliseconds, that swings the eyeball so the sign's image lands on the high-acuity center of the retina. During the brief pause that follows, typically 200–300 milliseconds, the visual system extracts detailed shape and letter information before the eyes jump again. Reading a full sentence therefore isn't one continuous scan but a rapid sequence of these jump-and-pause cycles, each overtly relocating attention to a new word or cluster of letters.

**Problem-solving application.** Overt attention is measurable and diagnostic, which makes it a practical tool in fields from human-computer interaction to clinical assessment. Eye-tracking devices record where and how long a person's gaze lands, producing a moment-by-moment map of visual behavior that reveals what actually captured attention — not just what a person *reports* noticing. This has direct design applications: a UX researcher reviewing this gaze data on a webpage can identify whether users' eyes overtly land on a call-to-action button or skip past it entirely, then redesign layout accordingly. In clinical and safety settings, overt attention patterns serve as behavioral markers: reduced or atypical face-scanning patterns in eye-tracking studies are used as one line of evidence in autism research, and monitoring a driver's gaze (e.g., detecting insufficient overt attention to mirrors or the road) underlies drowsiness- and distraction-detection systems in modern vehicles. In each case, the researcher's task is the same: infer the allocation of attention not from introspection, but from the physically observable, overt behavior of the eyes and head.
