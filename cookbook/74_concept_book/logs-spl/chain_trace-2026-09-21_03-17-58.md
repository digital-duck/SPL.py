## Japan 1858 Unequal Treaties

The treaties signed in 1858, notably the Harris Treaty with the United States, represented a critical systemic shock to the Tokugawa Shogunate's governance structure. These agreements did not merely open Japanese ports; they imposed severe economic and legal constraints that fundamentally undermined Japan’s national sovereignty. Key elements included granting foreign powers extraterritoriality—meaning foreigners were exempt from Japanese law and subject only to their own consulates—and, most critically for analysis, fixing low tariffs. By agreeing to fixed, minimal duties on imports and exports (often at rates far below what independent trade negotiations would dictate), Japan effectively surrendered control over its primary fiscal mechanism: the ability to set protective tariffs. This created a constrained economic model where national policy decisions were externally dictated by treaty obligations.

Consider the tariff structure as a function of Japanese policy, $T(P)$, where $P$ is the level of desired protectionism (e.g., raising tariffs on foreign goods). Under normal circumstances, Japan could set its tariff rate $\tau$. The treaties enforced an external ceiling and floor on this variable: $\tau \in [\tau_{\min}, \tau_{\max}]$, where $\tau_{\min}$ was fixed at a low percentage (often 5% or less) regardless of domestic economic needs. This constraint limits the optimal control variables available to the central government.

Imagine Japan attempts to raise tariffs to protect nascent domestic industries, which would require setting $\tau > \tau_{treaty}$. The treaty provisions act as an insurmountable constraint on this variable:
$$\text{Constraint:} \quad \tau(t) = c$$
where $c$ is a constant value dictated by foreign powers. This fixed parameter prevents the government from achieving economic equilibrium based on optimal tariff setting, creating a structural deficiency in the national economy's regulatory capacity.

This systemic constraint forces an analysis of deviation and necessary reform. If Japan’s domestic production requires tariffs $\tau_{optimal}$ to maintain a positive trade balance or protect key sectors, but the treaties enforce $\tau_{treaty}$, the resulting deficit in revenue generation and protected market share can be quantified as the loss function $L$:
$$L = \int_{t_0}^{t_f} (\tau_{optimal}(t) - \tau_{treaty}) \cdot R(t)\, dt$$
where $R(t)$ is the volume of protected goods. The ongoing, unmitigated existence of this loss function—driven by perpetually fixed tariffs and foreign legal jurisdiction—provided a powerful intellectual and political model for anti-Shogunate sentiment, demonstrating that national policy required fundamental structural change to restore sovereignty over its own economic variables.
