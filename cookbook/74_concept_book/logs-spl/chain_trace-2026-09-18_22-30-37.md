### submatrix (11.1s)

## Submatrix

Given an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the submatrix $A(i|j)$ is the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ matrix formed by deleting row $i$ and column $j$ from $A$. This operation is the structural building block of cofactor expansion, the method used to compute determinants and inverses of matrices larger than $2 \times 2$.

**Worked example.** Let

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix}
$$

To find $A(2|1)$, delete row 2 and column 1:

$$
A(2|1) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Notice that $A(2|1)$ retains the relative order of the remaining entries — rows 1 and 3, columns 2 and 3 — but is reindexed as a fresh $2\times 2$ matrix. This reindexing matters: when submatrices are nested (a submatrix of a submatrix), you must track positions relative to the *current* matrix, not the original.

**Problem-solving application.** Submatrices are essential for computing the determinant via cofactor (Laplace) expansion along any row $i$:

$$
\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{i+j} \, a_{ij} \det\big(A(i|j)\big)
$$

For the matrix above, expanding along row 1:

$$
\det(A) = 1\cdot\det(A(1|1)) - 2\cdot\det(A(1|2)) + 3\cdot\det(A(1|3))
$$

with $A(1|1) = \begin{pmatrix}5&6\\8&9\end{pmatrix}$, $A(1|2) = \begin{pmatrix}4&6\\7&9\end{pmatrix}$, $A(1|3) = \begin{pmatrix}4&5\\7&8\end{pmatrix}$. Computing each $2\times 2$ determinant gives $1(-3) - 2(-6) + 3(-3) = -3+12-9 = 0$, confirming $A$ is singular.

This recursive reduction — an $n \times n$ determinant defined in terms of $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ submatrix determinants — is why submatrices are indispensable beyond definition-level understanding: they are the mechanism that makes determinants, cofactor matrices, and adjugate-based matrix inversion computable for any size, and they generalize directly to minors used in rank and eigenvalue calculations.

### determinant (10.6s)

## Determinant

The determinant is a single number computed from a square matrix that captures how much the linear transformation it represents stretches or compresses space, and whether it flips orientation. For a matrix $A$, we write $\det(A)$ or $|A|$. It is defined recursively through **cofactor expansion**: for an $n \times n$ matrix, expand along the first row,

$$
\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{1+j} \, a_{1j} \, \det(M_{1j})
$$

where $M_{1j}$ is the **submatrix** obtained by deleting row 1 and column $j$ from $A$. The base case is a $1 \times 1$ matrix, whose determinant is just its single entry. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix this reduces to the familiar formula $\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc$.

**Worked example.** Compute the determinant of
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Expanding along the first row:
$$
\det(A) = 2\det\begin{pmatrix}1 & 4 \\ 2 & 0\end{pmatrix} - 0\det\begin{pmatrix}3 & 4 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} + 1\det\begin{pmatrix}3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
= 2(0 - 8) - 0 + 1(6 - 1) = -16 + 5 = -11.
$$

**Why it matters — problem-solving applications.** A nonzero determinant tells you a matrix is invertible: $\det(A) \neq 0$ if and only if $A^{-1}$ exists, which is exactly the condition for a linear system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ to have a unique solution for every $\mathbf{b}$. If $\det(A) = 0$, the transformation collapses space into a lower dimension (some information is lost), and the system either has no solution or infinitely many. Geometrically, $|\det(A)|$ equals the factor by which $A$ scales area (in 2D) or volume (in 3D); a negative sign indicates the transformation reverses orientation. This makes determinants essential for testing invertibility before attempting to solve a system numerically, for computing volumes via the Jacobian in multivariable calculus, and for finding eigenvalues, which are the roots of $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. In practice, direct cofactor expansion is only efficient for small matrices; for larger systems, row reduction (which tracks how each operation scales the determinant) is the standard computational approach.

### minor_and_cofactor (13.1s)

## Minor And Cofactor

Given a square matrix $A$, the **minor** $M_{ij}$ of the entry $a_{ij}$ is the determinant of the submatrix $A(i \mid j)$ obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$ from $A$. The **cofactor** $C_{ij}$ attaches a sign to that minor:

$$
C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}
$$

The sign pattern alternates like a checkerboard, starting with $+$ in the top-left corner. Minors and cofactors are the building blocks of **cofactor expansion**, a method for computing determinants of any size by reducing them to smaller determinants.

**Worked example.** Let

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}
$$

To find the minor $M_{12}$ (delete row 1, column 2):

$$
A(1\mid 2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad M_{12} = \det A(1\mid 2) = (0)(6) - (1)(5) = -5
$$

The cofactor is then $C_{12} = (-1)^{1+2} M_{12} = (-1)(-5) = 5$.

**Problem-solving application.** Cofactors let you compute a determinant along any row or column: $\det A = \sum_{j} a_{1j} C_{1j}$ (expanding along row 1). Expanding $A$ above along row 1:

$$
\det A = 2 C_{11} + 1 \cdot C_{12} + 3 \cdot C_{13}
$$

with $C_{11} = \det\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&6\end{pmatrix} = 22$, $C_{12} = 5$ (found above), and $C_{13} = \det\begin{pmatrix}0&4\\5&2\end{pmatrix} = -20$. So $\det A = 2(22) + 1(5) + 3(-20) = 44 + 5 - 60 = -11$.

Beyond computing determinants, the full grid of cofactors builds the **adjugate matrix**, $\operatorname{adj}(A) = C^T$ (the transpose of the cofactor matrix), which gives the inverse via $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\operatorname{adj}(A)$ whenever $\det A \neq 0$. When solving a system by hand, checking whether a single cofactor vanishes can reveal that an entire row or column contributes nothing to the determinant, letting you choose the cheapest row or column to expand along rather than grinding through all of them.

### expansion_about_rows (194.6s)

## Expansion About Rows

For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the determinant is defined by expansion along the first row:

$$
\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{1+j} a_{1j} M_{1j}
$$

where $M_{1j}$ is the minor obtained by deleting row 1 and column $j$. The theorem of expansion about rows states that this same value results from expanding along *any* row $i$:

$$
\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{i+j} a_{ij} M_{ij} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} C_{ij}
$$

where $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$ is the cofactor of entry $a_{ij}$. The sign pattern $(-1)^{i+j}$ alternates in a checkerboard fashion across the matrix. This equality is not obvious from the definition alone — it requires proof, typically by showing the expansion is multilinear and alternating in the rows, properties that uniquely characterize the determinant. Once established, it gives enormous computational freedom: you may choose whichever row simplifies the arithmetic.

**Worked example.** Consider

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Row 1 has only one nonzero entry, so expanding there requires computing just one $2\times 2$ minor:

$$
\det(A) = (-1)^{1+2}(5)\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = -5(0 - 0) = 0
$$

Expanding along row 1 avoided three cofactor computations entirely. Had you defaulted to expanding along row 2 (with no zero entries), you would have needed three $2\times 2$ determinants for the same answer.

**Problem-solving application.** The strategic value of this theorem is choosing the row with the most zeros — each zero entry eliminates a cofactor computation, since $a_{ij} \cdot C_{ij} = 0$ whenever $a_{ij} = 0$. For a sparse matrix, this can reduce an $n \times n$ expansion from $n!$ arithmetic operations (brute-force permutation expansion) to a small fraction of that. In practice — verifying invertibility, solving small linear systems by Cramer's rule, or computing Jacobians in symbolic form — scanning rows for zeros before expanding is the first move, since it turns an otherwise tedious calculation into a short one without changing the result.

### determinant_row_column_multiple (10.9s)

## Determinant Row Column Multiple

If a matrix $B$ is obtained from $A$ by multiplying every entry of a single row (or column) by a scalar $\alpha$, then

$$\det(B) = \alpha \det(A).$$

This is one of the defining multilinearity properties of the determinant: the determinant is a linear function of each row (or column) separately, when the other rows are held fixed.

**Worked example.** Let

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(A) = (1)(4) - (2)(3) = -2.$$

Multiply the first row by $\alpha = 5$:

$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(B) = (5)(4) - (10)(3) = 20 - 30 = -10 = 5(-2).$$

Indeed $\det(B) = 5\det(A)$, confirming the rule. The same holds if you scale a column instead: scaling column 2 of $A$ by $\alpha$ multiplies $\det(A)$ by $\alpha$ as well.

**Why it's true.** In the cofactor expansion along the scaled row, every term in the sum contains exactly one factor from that row. Multiplying the whole row by $\alpha$ multiplies every term of the expansion by $\alpha$, so the entire sum — the determinant — is multiplied by $\alpha$. This is different from scaling the *entire* matrix: scaling all $n$ rows of an $n \times n$ matrix by $\alpha$ multiplies the determinant by $\alpha^n$, since each row contributes a separate factor of $\alpha$.

**Problem-solving application.** This rule lets you simplify determinant computations by factoring out common values before expanding. For instance,

$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 9 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 3 \det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 3\big[(2)(2)-(3)(4)\big] = 3(-8) = -24.$$

Pulling the common factor 3 out of row 1 first avoids working with larger numbers. This property also underlies why a matrix with a zero row (equivalent to scaling that row by $\alpha = 0$) always has determinant zero, and it is a key step in proving how determinants change under general row-reduction operations used to solve linear systems.

### determinant_row_column_multiple_and_addition (11.6s)

## Determinant Row Column Multiple And Addition

For a square matrix $A$, if you form a new matrix $A'$ by adding a scalar multiple of one row to another row (leaving all other rows unchanged), the determinant does not change:

$$\det(A') = \det(A).$$

The identical statement holds for columns. This is one of the three elementary row operations used in Gaussian elimination, and it is the one that lets you simplify a matrix into triangular form *without disturbing its determinant* — the key fact that makes determinant computation by elimination possible.

**Why it's true.** Determinants are multilinear in the rows: fixing all rows but one, $\det$ is a linear function of that remaining row. Suppose we replace row $i$ with $R_i + cR_j$ (where $j \ne i$). By linearity in row $i$,
$$\det(\dots, R_i + cR_j, \dots, R_j, \dots) = \det(\dots, R_i, \dots, R_j, \dots) + c\det(\dots, R_j, \dots, R_j, \dots).$$
The second term has row $j$ appearing twice, and a determinant with two identical rows is always zero (swapping them negates the determinant, but swapping identical rows changes nothing — so it must equal its own negative). That term vanishes, leaving $\det(A') = \det(A)$.

**Worked example.** Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det(A) = 1\cdot4 - 2\cdot3 = -2.$$
Replace row 2 with $R_2 - 3R_1$:
$$A' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det(A') = 1\cdot(-2) - 2\cdot0 = -2.$$
The determinant is unchanged, as predicted, and $A'$ is now upper triangular, so its determinant reads off directly as the product of diagonal entries.

**Problem-solving application.** This theorem is what makes computing determinants of large matrices tractable: instead of expanding by cofactors (which costs $O(n!)$ operations), you eliminate entries below the diagonal using only this row operation, track any row swaps (each flips the sign), and multiply the resulting diagonal — an $O(n^3)$ computation. It also underlies proving linear dependence: if elimination produces a zero row, the determinant is zero, revealing that the original rows were linearly dependent, since no combination of the allowed operations could have introduced a genuine zero from independent rows.

### determinant_row_column_swap (9.7s)

## Determinant Row Column Swap

**Definition.** For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, let $A'$ be the matrix obtained by exchanging any two rows (or any two columns) of $A$. Then

$$\det(A') = -\det(A).$$

This is a row-operation property of the determinant, alongside scaling and row addition, and it follows directly from the definition of the determinant as a signed sum over permutations, where each term carries a sign equal to $\pm 1$ depending on the parity of the permutation. Swapping two rows corresponds to composing that permutation with a single transposition, which always flips its parity — hence the sign of every term flips, and so does the determinant.

**Worked example.** Consider

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(A) = (1)(4) - (2)(3) = -2.$$

Swap the two rows:

$$A' = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(A') = (3)(2) - (4)(1) = 2 = -\det(A).$$

The same holds for a $3\times 3$ matrix or for column swaps — try swapping columns 1 and 2 of $A$ above and you'll again get $\det = 2$.

**Problem-solving application.** This property is a practical shortcut, not just a curiosity. First, it explains why a matrix with two identical rows must have determinant zero: swapping those identical rows leaves the matrix unchanged, yet the theorem forces $\det(A) = -\det(A)$, which is only possible if $\det(A) = 0$. This fact is itself the seed of linear dependence tests used throughout linear algebra.

Second, it is a computational tool. When performing determinant calculation via row reduction (turning $A$ into upper-triangular form via Gaussian elimination), row swaps are sometimes necessary to avoid a zero pivot. Each swap flips the sign of the running determinant, so you must track the number of swaps and multiply the final triangular determinant by $(-1)^{(\text{number of swaps})}$. For example, if two row swaps are used during elimination, the sign correction is $(-1)^2 = 1$ (no net change); with three swaps, it's $(-1)^3 = -1$. Omitting this tracking is a common source of sign errors in hand computation and in naive elimination code.

### row_operations (11.4s)

## Row Operations

Row operations are the three legal moves for transforming a matrix while preserving the solution set of its associated linear system. They are the mechanical engine behind Gaussian elimination, and their validity is guaranteed by the fact that each one corresponds to a reversible manipulation of the underlying equations.

**Definition.** Given a matrix, the three elementary row operations are:

1. **Swap**: interchange rows $i$ and $j$, written $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$.
2. **Scale**: multiply row $i$ by a nonzero scalar $c$, written $R_i \to c R_i$.
3. **Replace**: add a multiple of row $j$ to row $i$, written $R_i \to R_i + c R_j$.

Each operation is invertible: swapping twice restores the original order, scaling by $c$ can be undone by scaling by $1/c$, and replacing can be undone by subtracting the same multiple back out. This invertibility is precisely why row operations never change the solution set — any solution to the new system is also a solution to the old one, and vice versa.

**Worked example.** Consider the system
$$
\begin{aligned}
x + 2y &= 5 \\
2x + 5y &= 12
\end{aligned}
\qquad \longleftrightarrow \qquad
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 2 & 5 & 12 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Apply $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$: the second row becomes $(2-2, 5-4, 12-10) = (0, 1, 2)$, giving
$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$
which reads back as $x + 2y = 5$ and $y = 2$. Substituting gives $x = 1$. The replace operation eliminated $x$ from the second equation without altering the solution $(x,y) = (1,2)$.

**Problem-solving application.** The strategic use of row operations is what makes elimination systematic rather than ad hoc: at each step, choose a replace operation that zeroes out an entry below the pivot, using the scale operation only when a leading entry needs to become $1$, and using swap when the current pivot position holds a zero and a nonzero entry lower in the column must be brought up. Tracking which operations were performed (and in what order) also lets you compute the determinant's sign changes (from swaps) and scaling factors, which becomes essential later when connecting row reduction to LU decomposition and determinant calculation.

### triangular_matrix_determinant (11.9s)

## Triangular Matrix Determinant

A square matrix is **upper triangular** if every entry below the main diagonal is zero, and **lower triangular** if every entry above the diagonal is zero. For such matrices, computing the determinant by full cofactor expansion collapses into something remarkably simple.

Recall that expansion about a row (or column) writes the determinant as a sum of entries times their cofactors: $\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}C_{ij}$, where $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$ and $M_{ij}$ is the minor obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$. If we expand an upper triangular matrix along its first column, every entry below $a_{11}$ is zero, so all terms except $a_{11}C_{11}$ vanish. The remaining minor $M_{11}$ is itself upper triangular, one size smaller. Repeating this argument down to a $1\times 1$ matrix gives:

$$\det(A) = a_{11}a_{22}a_{33}\cdots a_{nn}$$

The determinant of a triangular matrix is simply the product of its diagonal entries. The same holds for lower triangular matrices by expanding along the first row instead.

**Worked example.** Let

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 & 7 \\ 0 & 5 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Expanding along column 1: only $a_{11}=3$ survives, leaving $\det(A) = 3 \cdot \det\begin{pmatrix}5 & 4\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$. That $2\times 2$ minor is itself upper triangular, so its determinant is $5 \cdot(-1) = -5$. Thus $\det(A) = 3(-5) = -15$ — matching the diagonal product $3 \cdot 5 \cdot (-1) = -15$ directly.

**Problem-solving application.** This shortcut is the real payoff of Gaussian elimination: row-reducing any matrix $A$ to upper triangular form (tracking any row swaps, each of which flips the sign of the determinant, and any row scaling, which scales it) lets you compute $\det(A)$ in $O(n^3)$ operations instead of the $O(n!)$ cost of full cofactor expansion. For example, if elimination reduces a $4\times 4$ matrix to triangular form with diagonal entries $2, -3, 1, 5$ using one row swap, the determinant is $(-1)(2)(-3)(1)(5) = 30$. This is exactly how software (and you, by hand for small systems) efficiently tests whether a matrix is invertible: $\det(A) = 0$ if and only if some diagonal pivot vanishes after elimination.

### determinant_via_row_operations (10.2s)

## Determinant Via Row Operations

The determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix can be computed directly from cofactor expansion, but that method costs $O(n!)$ operations — impractical beyond $4 \times 4$. Row reduction computes the same number in $O(n^3)$ operations by exploiting how each elementary row operation transforms the determinant in a predictable, trackable way.

Three operations govern the process. Swapping two rows multiplies the determinant by $-1$. Scaling a row by a nonzero constant $c$ multiplies the determinant by $c$. Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged. Once the matrix is reduced to upper triangular form, its determinant is simply the product of the diagonal entries:

$$
\det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} u_{ii}
$$

where $u_{ii}$ are the diagonal entries after elimination — provided every scaling and swap factor has been tracked and multiplied back in.

**Worked example.** Consider

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Eliminate below the pivot in column 1: $R_2 \to R_2 - \tfrac{1}{2}R_1$ gives $(0, 3, 2)$; this operation does not change the determinant. Then $R_3 \to R_3 - \tfrac{3}{2}R_1$ gives $(0, 0, 5)$, again with no effect on the determinant. The matrix is now upper triangular with diagonal $(2, 3, 5)$, so $\det(A) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 30$. No swaps or scalings were used, so no correction factor is needed.

**Problem-solving application.** This method matters beyond hand computation because it is exactly how software computes determinants for large matrices, via LU decomposition. If a row swap is required during elimination (to avoid a zero pivot), the running product must be multiplied by $-1$ for each swap. If you deliberately scale a row by $c$ to simplify arithmetic, divide the final product by $c$ to compensate. A useful diagnostic: if elimination ever produces an all-zero row, the determinant is immediately $0$, and the matrix is singular — no further computation is needed. This shortcut is often faster than computing the full determinant when the only question is whether $A$ is invertible.
