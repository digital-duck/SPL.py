## Matrix Vector Product

Given an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ with columns $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n \in \mathbb{R}^m$, and a vector $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with entries $u_1, \dots, u_n$, the matrix-vector product is defined as the linear combination of the columns of $A$ weighted by the entries of $\mathbf{u}$:

$$A\mathbf{u} = u_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + u_2 \mathbf{a}_2 + \cdots + u_n \mathbf{a}_n$$

This definition is the conceptual anchor for everything else linear algebra does with matrices: $A\mathbf{u}$ is not "row times column arithmetic" first and foremost — it is a weighted sum of vectors, and the result always lands in $\mathbb{R}^m$, the space spanned by $A$'s columns.

**Worked example.** Let

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Then

$$A\mathbf{u} = 2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} + 1\begin{pmatrix}0\\3\end{pmatrix} + (-1)\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\-2\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}0\\3\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}-2\\-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-2\\0\end{pmatrix}$$

The familiar row-by-row computation (dot each row of $A$ with $\mathbf{u}$) gives the identical answer, and is useful for hand computation, but the column-combination view is what reveals *why* the answer has the shape it does: it is a point reachable by scaling and adding $A$'s columns.

**Why this matters for problem-solving.** The column view converts two of the most common linear algebra questions into span/independence questions you already know how to reason about. First: "does $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ have a solution?" is exactly the question "is $\mathbf{b}$ in the span of $A$'s columns?" Second: "is the solution unique?" is exactly the question "are $A$'s columns linearly independent?" — if they are dependent, some nonzero $\mathbf{u}$ combines them to $\mathbf{0}$, and you can add that $\mathbf{u}$ to any solution to get another one.

For instance, if $A$'s three columns above happen to be linearly independent (verify: no nontrivial combination gives $\mathbf{0}$), then every vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ that lies in their span has a *unique* representation as $A\mathbf{u}$ — a fact you will rely on constantly when solving linear systems, computing coordinates in a new basis, or checking whether a transformation is invertible.

### diagonal_matrix (0.0s)

## Diagonal Matrix

A **diagonal matrix** is a square matrix in which every off-diagonal entry equals zero. Only the entries $a_{ii}$ along the main diagonal may be nonzero. Formally, an $n \times n$ matrix $D = (a_{ij})$ is diagonal if $a_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i \neq j$. Such a matrix is written compactly as $D = \text{diag}(d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n)$, meaning the diagonal entries are $d_1, \ldots, d_n$ in order.

**Worked example.** Consider

$$
D = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

This is $\text{diag}(3, -2, 5)$. Diagonal matrices have a striking algebraic property: matrix operations that are normally expensive collapse into simple entrywise arithmetic. Multiplying two diagonal matrices of the same size gives another diagonal matrix, formed by multiplying corresponding diagonal entries:

$$
\text{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)\,\text{diag}(e_1,\ldots,e_n) = \text{diag}(d_1e_1,\ldots,d_ne_n).
$$

Raising $D$ to a power $k$ is equally simple: $D^k = \text{diag}(d_1^k, \ldots, d_n^k)$, since off-diagonal zeros never mix with the diagonal entries under repeated multiplication — a fact easily confirmed by induction on $k$. Likewise, $D$ is invertible exactly when every $d_i \neq 0$, and in that case $D^{-1} = \text{diag}(1/d_1, \ldots, 1/d_n)$.

**Problem-solving application.** This computational shortcut is why diagonal matrices sit at the center of *diagonalization*: given a general square matrix $A$, if one can find an invertible matrix $P$ such that $A = PDP^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal, then computing $A^k$ — normally requiring $k-1$ full matrix multiplications — reduces to

$$
A^k = PD^kP^{-1},
$$

where $D^k$ is obtained instantly by raising each diagonal entry to the $k$-th power. This technique underlies solving systems of linear differential equations, analyzing long-run behavior of Markov chains, and computing matrix exponentials in physics and engineering. For example, to find the state of a population model after 10 time steps governed by a transition matrix $A$, diagonalizing $A$ once and then computing $D^{10}$ entrywise is far faster and more numerically stable than multiplying $A$ by itself ten times.

### similar_matrices (0.0s)

## Similar Matrices

Two square matrices $A$ and $B$ of the same size are **similar** if there exists a nonsingular matrix $S$ such that

$$A = S^{-1}BS.$$

Similarity is not just an abstract relation — it captures the idea that $A$ and $B$ represent the *same linear transformation*, just described using different coordinate systems (different bases). The matrix $S$ is the "change of basis" matrix that translates between them, and $S^{-1}$ translates back. Because matrix-vector multiplication $Bv$ describes how $B$ acts on a vector $v$, the equation $A = S^{-1}BS$ says: to apply $A$ to a vector, first change coordinates with $S$, apply $B$, then change back with $S^{-1}$.

**Worked example.** Let

$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad S^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then

$$A = S^{-1}BS = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Notice $A$ and $B$ look very different, yet they must share key algebraic properties, since similarity preserves them.

**Why this matters — invariants under similarity.** Similar matrices always have the same trace, determinant, rank, eigenvalues, and characteristic polynomial. This follows directly from properties of matrix multiplication: for determinant,

$$\det(A) = \det(S^{-1}BS) = \det(S^{-1})\det(B)\det(S) = \det(B),$$

since $\det(S^{-1})\det(S) = 1$. For eigenvalues, if $Bv = \lambda v$, then setting $w = S^{-1}v$ gives $Aw = S^{-1}BSw = S^{-1}Bv = \lambda S^{-1}v = \lambda w$, so $A$ has the same eigenvalue $\lambda$.

**Problem-solving application.** This invariance is the practical payoff: to compute eigenvalues, determinant, or trace of a complicated matrix $A$, find a similar matrix $B$ that is diagonal or triangular — where these quantities are read off instantly — then transfer the answer back to $A$. This is precisely the strategy behind diagonalization, a core technique for solving systems of linear differential equations and analyzing repeated transformations like $A^n$.

### diagonalizable_matrix (0.0s)

## Diagonalizable Matrix

An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is **diagonalizable** if it is similar to a diagonal matrix — that is, if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that

$$A = PDP^{-1}.$$

The columns of $P$ are eigenvectors of $A$, and the entries of $D$ are the corresponding eigenvalues, in matching order. This ties together two ideas: similarity (matrices representing the same linear transformation under different bases) and diagonal matrices (whose simplicity makes computation trivial). Diagonalizability means $A$'s transformation, viewed in the right basis — the eigenbasis — is just independent scaling along each axis.

**Worked example.** Let

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Its characteristic polynomial is $\det(A - \lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)$, giving eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 5$, $\lambda_2 = 2$. Solving $(A - 5I)v = 0$ gives eigenvector $v_1 = (1,1)^T$; solving $(A - 2I)v = 0$ gives $v_2 = (1,-2)^T$. Since these two eigenvectors are linearly independent, $A$ is diagonalizable:

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A = PDP^{-1}.$$

**The theorem, and why it matters for problem-solving.** An $n \times n$ matrix is diagonalizable if and only if it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors — equivalently, for each eigenvalue, the dimension of its eigenspace (geometric multiplicity) equals its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial (algebraic multiplicity). This test is the practical payoff: rather than guessing, compute eigenvalues, find each eigenspace's dimension, and compare. A matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is automatically diagonalizable, since eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are always independent. Diagonalizability is what makes computing $A^k$ tractable: $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$, where $D^k$ just raises each diagonal entry to the $k$-th power — turning an otherwise expensive matrix-multiplication problem into simple scalar exponentiation, essential in solving linear recurrences, Markov chain long-run behavior, and systems of differential equations.

### eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors (12.4s)

## Eigenvalues And Eigenvectors

For a square matrix $A$ (size $n \times n$), an eigenvector is a nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}$ that, under the transformation $A$, does not change direction — it only stretches or shrinks by a scalar factor $\lambda$, the eigenvalue:

$$A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$$

While $A$ generally rotates and scales vectors in complicated ways, eigenvectors are the special directions along which $A$ acts like simple scalar multiplication.

**Finding eigenvalues.** Rewrite $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ as $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, where $I$ is the identity matrix. Since $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$, the matrix $A - \lambda I$ must be singular (non-invertible), which forces its determinant to vanish:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$

This is the **characteristic equation**. Expanding the determinant produces a degree-$n$ polynomial in $\lambda$, called the characteristic polynomial, whose roots are the eigenvalues of $A$.

**Worked example.** Let

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$

Then $A - \lambda I = \begin{pmatrix} 4-\lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3-\lambda \end{pmatrix}$, and

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)$$

So $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$. For $\lambda_1 = 5$: solve $(A - 5I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, i.e. $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, giving $x_1 = x_2$, so $\mathbf{x}_1 = (1,1)^T$ (any nonzero scalar multiple works). For $\lambda_2 = 2$: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $2x_1 = -x_2$, so $\mathbf{x}_2 = (1,-2)^T$.

**Problem-solving application.** Eigenvalues reveal long-run behavior without repeated matrix multiplication. If $A$ models a system evolving as $\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = A\mathbf{v}_t$ (population dynamics, Markov chains, coupled oscillators), and $\mathbf{v}_0$ is expressed as a combination of eigenvectors, then after $t$ steps each eigenvector component simply scales by $\lambda_i^t$. The eigenvalue with largest magnitude dominates as $t \to \infty$, determining whether the system grows, decays, or stabilizes — the basis for stability analysis, PageRank, and principal component analysis.

### matrix_powers_via_diagonalization (0.0s)

## Matrix Powers Via Diagonalization

Computing $A^k$ directly by repeated matrix multiplication costs $O(n^3)$ per multiplication, so raising an $n \times n$ matrix to the $k$-th power naively takes $O(kn^3)$ operations. When $A$ is diagonalizable, this cost collapses dramatically — a direct payoff of the diagonalization $A = SDS^{-1}$, where $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ holds the eigenvalues of $A$ and the columns of $S$ are the corresponding eigenvectors.

The key algebraic fact is that the inverse factors cancel telescopically:

$$
A^k = (SDS^{-1})(SDS^{-1}) \cdots (SDS^{-1}) = SD^kS^{-1}
$$

since each $S^{-1}S$ pair in the middle equals the identity. Raising the diagonal matrix $D$ to the $k$-th power is trivial — each diagonal entry is simply raised to that power independently:

$$
D^k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \lambda_2^k, \dots, \lambda_n^k)
$$

So $A^k$ requires only one diagonalization (computed once, up front) plus two matrix multiplications and $n$ scalar exponentiations, regardless of how large $k$ is.

**Worked example.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = 3$, with eigenvectors $v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ and $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}$. Then

$$
S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad S^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
$$

To find $A^{10}$, compute $D^{10} = \operatorname{diag}(2^{10}, 3^{10}) = \operatorname{diag}(1024, 59049)$, then

$$
A^{10} = SD^{10}S^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1024 & 58025 \\ 0 & 59049 \end{pmatrix}
$$

— obtained without ever multiplying $A$ by itself ten times.

**Problem-solving application.** This technique is the standard tool for solving linear recurrence relations and discrete dynamical systems, such as population models $x_{k+1} = Ax_k$ or the Fibonacci recursion written in matrix form. Rather than iterating the recursion $k$ times, one diagonalizes $A$ once and evaluates $A^k x_0 = SD^kS^{-1}x_0$ for any $k$, immediately revealing long-run behavior: since $\lambda_1^k$ dominates whichever eigenvalue has the largest magnitude, the system's growth rate as $k \to \infty$ is governed entirely by that dominant eigenvalue.

## Fibonacci Closed Form

**Definition.** The Fibonacci sequence is defined by the recurrence $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$, with $F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = 1$. Computing $F_{100}$ directly from this recurrence requires 100 additions — not expensive, but it hides a deeper structure. Diagonalization lets us write $F_n$ as a single closed-form expression in $n$, no iteration required.

The key move is to encode the recurrence as a linear map. Define the vector $v_n = \begin{pmatrix} F_{n+1} \\ F_n \end{pmatrix}$. Then

$$
v_{n+1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} v_n = A v_n,
$$

since $F_{n+2} = F_{n+1} + F_n$ and $F_{n+1} = F_{n+1}$. Iterating gives $v_n = A^n v_0$, so the whole problem reduces to computing a matrix power efficiently — and diagonalization is exactly the tool for that.

**Worked example.** The eigenvalues of $A$ solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0$, giving $\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$ — the golden ratio $\varphi$ and its conjugate $\psi = 1 - \varphi$. Corresponding eigenvectors are $\begin{pmatrix} \varphi \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} \psi \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Writing $A = PDP^{-1}$ with $D = \text{diag}(\varphi, \psi)$, we get $A^n = P D^n P^{-1}$, and since $D^n$ just raises each eigenvalue to the $n$-th power, the exponential blow-up of computing $A^n$ by repeated multiplication collapses into two scalar powers.

**Problem-solving application.** Carrying out the matrix algebra (or solving the recurrence with ansatz $F_n = C_1\varphi^n + C_2\psi^n$ and matching $F_0, F_1$) yields Binet's formula:

$$
F_n = \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}}, \qquad \varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},\ \ \psi = \frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}.
$$

Because $|\psi| < 1$, the term $\psi^n$ shrinks to zero, so $F_n$ is essentially $\varphi^n/\sqrt{5}$ rounded to the nearest integer — this is how you compute $F_{50}$ in one calculator step instead of 50 additions, and it also explains why ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers converge to $\varphi$.
