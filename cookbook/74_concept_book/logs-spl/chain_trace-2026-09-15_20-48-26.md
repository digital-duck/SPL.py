### 木 (27.5s)

## 木

**Picture this:** Imagine drawing a tree very fast—just a trunk with branches sticking out. Now squeeze it into a small box. That sketch is 木 (*mù*), the character for "tree." A vertical line is the trunk; little arms reaching out are branches.

Chinese characters work like LEGO bricks: one brick builds different things depending on what connects to it. 木 is exactly like that.

Stack two 木 bricks side by side, and the meaning multiplies:

$$\underbrace{\text{木}}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{\text{木}}_{\text{tree}} = \text{林}$$

That's 林 (*lín*, "forest")—many trees together. Place a person figure next to 木, and rest appears:

$$\underbrace{\text{人}}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{\text{木}}_{\text{tree}} = \text{休}$$

That's 休 (*xiū*, "rest")—someone resting by a tree.

But 木 has another superpower. In characters like 沐 (*mù*, "bathing"), 木 doesn't mean trees. It's there purely for *sound*—the character sounds like *mù*, and that sound brick does the work instead.

**Now you try:** Look for more combinations with 木. What do you think happens when you stack three 木 together? Can you guess what it means?

### 女 (53.8s)

## 女

**Picture this:** Imagine trying to draw "woman" without using any words—just a picture. In ancient China, people didn't write the way we do today. They drew tiny pictures instead, and those pictures had to be simple enough to write quickly with a brush.

**The shape:** Look at 女—it shows a figure sitting or kneeling. The top part curves like a head and arms, and the bottom part is like legs tucked beneath. It's a real person, frozen in that quiet moment. When Chinese people needed a word for "woman," they didn't invent an abstract symbol. They just drew what they saw.

**A building block:** 女 is the start of a story, not the end. This character works like a LEGO brick—Chinese speakers use it over and over to build words about family. You'll find 女 hiding in words for mothers, sisters, daughters, and aunts. Every time you see it, it's a hint: "This word connects to women or family." The picture does the teaching.

**Now you try:** Hunt for 女 in other Chinese characters. Every time you find it, ask yourself: "Why put the woman-shape here? What does this word mean?" The picture itself gives you part of the answer.

### 人 (14.6s)

## 人

**Picture this:** You're walking down a busy street and spot someone in the distance—just their silhouette against the sky. Their legs take quick steps, one foot forward, one back. In that split second, you *know* it's a human. You didn't see their face or their hands; you saw the *shape of movement*. The ancient Chinese writers saw the same thing and asked: what does a person *look like* at its simplest?

**Why it feels right:** The character 人 captures that silhouette perfectly. It's two legs planted on the ground, a torso rising up—the *shape of someone walking or standing*. So simple, so instantly recognizable, that it became the building block for dozens of other characters.

**How it works:** 人 is the most-used semantic piece in Chinese writing. On its own, it means "person" or "human." But it also acts as a root. When you see 人 tucked into another character, it usually tells you that character is related to people, families, or human qualities.

**Examples in action:**
- $\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{大}_{big} = 大人$ (adult—literally "big person")
- $\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 休$ (rest—person leaning against a tree)

**Now you try:** Look at the character 从 (follow). It's 人 + 人. What do you think it means? Why might two people side-by-side suggest "following"?

### 子 (13.1s)

## 子

**Picture this:** Imagine looking down at a newborn baby all wrapped up in blankets—head, tiny body, little arms and legs folded inside. That's what this character started as: an actual drawing of a swaddled infant, seen from above.

**Why it works:** The ancient Chinese didn't have cameras, so they drew what they saw. A baby bundled up looks exactly like this shape—compact and curled. Over thousands of years, the picture got simpler, but the *feeling* stayed the same. When you see 子, you're looking at the memory of a newborn.

**How it's used:** 子 means "child" or "baby" all by itself. But here's the clever part: when you put 子 next to other characters, it often becomes a suffix that means "thing" or "type of person"—like adding "-er" to "teach" makes "teacher."

The most beautiful example:
$$\underbrace{\text{女}}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{\text{子}}_{\text{child}} = \text{好} \quad \text{(good)}$$

A woman with her child—the ancient writers thought: what could be more good than that?

**Now you try:** Spot 子 hiding inside 学 (to study) or 孙 (grandchild). What does each character make you think?

### 宀 (9.6s)

## 宀

**Picture this:** You're building with blocks, and your little sister is putting a roof on top of a house. That triangle or curved top that covers everything underneath—that's the idea of home. In Chinese writing, there's a radical that looks like a roof over a room, and we call it 宀.

**The key idea:** Whenever you see this roof shape sitting over letters in a character, it's like the character is saying, "This thing belongs *inside* somewhere safe." It could be a home, a room, a shelter—anywhere that protects you. The roof is the hint.

**How it works:** Think of 宀 as the character's way of drawing a protective canopy above something. If a character has this radical, you know the story involves houses, rooms, or things that need shelter.

$$\underbrace{\text{roof}}_{\text{宀}} + \underbrace{\text{inside}}_ {\text{...}} = \text{home or shelter concept}$$

**Now you try:** Look for 宀 in these characters—家 (home), 安 (safe), 宝 (treasure). What do all of them have in common? They're all about things people care for and protect under a roof.

### 马 (38.3s)

## 马

Picture a horse galloping across ancient grasslands—that's the image the earliest Chinese writers drew to create this character. 马 once meant only one thing: horse. Fast, powerful, real.

But here's where the puzzle begins. You ask your mom, "妈，你在吗?" (Mom, are you here?). The character 妈 has nothing to do with horses. So why does it use 马?

This is called **sound-borrowing**. Once 马 existed and everyone knew it meant "horse," writers realized they could reuse it for a different reason: not for horseness, but for the *sound* it makes—"ma." It's like using the letter *C* in English: sometimes it sounds like "k" (cat), sometimes like "s" (city). The horse character became a sound-lending piece.

The composition works like this:

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{ma sound}} = 妈 \text{ (mother)}$$

The 马 isn't doing horse-work anymore—it's just loaning its sound to help create the word for "mother."

**Now you try**: Can you think of other words that sound like "ma"? How might ancient Chinese writers have borrowed 马 to write them?

### 从 (11.1s)

## 从

**Picture this:** Imagine you're walking down a path. Your friend is behind you, following you step by step. You're leading, and they're coming along. That's the feeling of 从 — following, going with, or moving after someone.

**Why does this feel right?** Look at the character: it's two people. One person makes $\underbrace{人}_{person}$, but 从 shows $\underbrace{人}_{leader} + \underbrace{人}_{follower}$. When people move together, one naturally goes ahead, and the other follows. The character captures that exact moment — the picture of movement and connection between two people.

The English word "from" actually captures this too: we follow a path "from" one place; we follow an idea "from" a teacher. The direction flows from leader to follower.

**Now you try:** Think about a time you followed someone — maybe a sibling down the stairs, or a friend walking to school. Can you see how 从 is really showing that picture of "you following them"?

### 口 (18.8s)

## 口

What's an opening? Think of your mouth. It's a hole in your face where sounds come out and food goes in. A very, very long time ago, people in China looked at a mouth and said, "That looks like a small square!" So they drew it as a square: 口. And that became the character for *mouth*.

Why does this matter? Because a mouth isn't just for chewing. It's where your words escape. It's where you ask questions. It's where your laugh comes out. When the Chinese wanted to show that something was a *question*, they tucked 口 inside other characters like a tiny sign that says, "A question lives here!"

But here's the magic: 口 loves to team up. Add it to 言 (word) and boom—you get 話, which means *speak*. Add it to 食 (food) and boom—you get 飯, which means *meal*. The characters snap together like puzzle pieces.

$$\underbrace{言}_{\text{word}} + \underbrace{口}_{\text{mouth}} = \text{話 (speak)}$$

**Now you try**: Look at your face in a mirror. Can you see your mouth as a square? What other openings do you notice?

### 日 (25.0s)

## 日

Picture this: thousands of years ago, someone needed to draw the sun on bamboo. They had only a brush and ink. What's the fastest way to show "sun"? A circle, yes—but to make it really shine, they added a mark in the middle, like light radiating outward.

This is exactly what happened. The Chinese character for sun is a square (or circle, depending on your brush stroke) with a dot or line in the center. That center mark is the key—it shows the sun's brightness, the source of light. The square edges come from how brushes naturally work: your strokes make straight lines and corners. So this one simple shape captures two big ideas: what the sun *does* (it shines bright from its center) and what it *looks like* when you draw it quickly.

Here's how it builds:
$$\underbrace{\text{boundary}}_{\text{sun's container}} + \underbrace{\text{center mark}}_{\text{brightness}} = \text{日}$$

Now you try: Look at the sun or a photo of it. Pretend you have only a brush and one second. How would you draw it? Most people draw a circle with a dot in the middle—and that's almost exactly what this character chose centuries ago.

### 月 (35.5s)

## 月

**Picture this:** Go outside on a clear night and look up. The moon is there—a glowing crescent or circle in the sky. Now look at your arm. It's curved and smooth, round like a little moon. People long ago noticed this too. They thought: the moon has a certain shape, and my body parts are shaped the same way. That shape means *something*.

**The intuition:** The character 月 began as a drawing of the moon—that beautiful thing we see in the night sky. But here's the clever part: ancient people noticed that body parts are curved and round like the moon too. Your arms, legs, and belly all have that moon-like flowing shape. So 月 became the character for "flesh" and "body." One drawing, but it grew two different jobs.

**How it works:** When 月 stands by itself, it means "moon." But when 月 hides inside another character, it whispers: this is about *your body*.

$$\underbrace{月}_{\text{flesh/body}} + \underbrace{\text{other part}}_{\text{specific meaning}} = \text{body-character}$$

**Now you try:** Hunt for 月 inside characters about legs, arms, or knees. Once you spot this shape hiding in other words, you've learned one of Chinese writing's secrets.

### 林 (18.1s)

## 林

**Picture this:** Imagine you're walking through one tree. You're surrounded by leaves and branches—interesting, but it's just one tree. Now imagine stepping into a place where there are TWO trees standing right next to each other, then ten more, then a hundred. Suddenly you're not just near a tree anymore; you're *in a forest*. The feeling changes completely. One tree is cool. Many trees *together*? That's a whole new thing.

**The pattern:** Chinese writers noticed something clever about how things work in the real world: when you put the same thing twice, it doesn't just mean "more"—it means something *bigger* or *more intense*. One tree (木, *mù*) is one tree. But two trees standing together? That's not just "two trees." It's a *forest*—a completely different experience (林, *lín*).

**The rule:** This is one of the simplest building blocks in Chinese characters. It's pure repetition:

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \underbrace{林}_{woods/forest}$$

**Now you try:** Can you think of other things where "two is different from one"? Two friends playing together feels different than playing alone. Two colors next to each other look different than just one. What other things change when you put two of them side by side?

### 水 (58.0s)

## 水

Picture a flowing stream. When ancient Chinese people wanted to write "water," they didn't pick a random shape—they drew what water *looks like*. The character 水 shows a center line (the flow) with curves (the ripples). Writing it, you almost feel the water moving.

Here's what's clever: once people had 水, they made it do double duty as a building block. When 水 goes inside other words, it becomes 氵—three little dots, like water droplets, squished onto the left side. It's a badge that says, "This word is about water."

$$\underbrace{\text{water}}_{\text{氵}} + \underbrace{\text{other parts}}_{\text{meaning}} = \text{water-word}$$

See 池 (pond)? Water badge ✓. See 泳 (swim)? Water badge ✓. See 泪 (tear)? Water badge ✓. The ancient writers were smart: they put a hint right in the character to tell you water is involved.

**Now you try**: Look at 河 (river), 溪 (stream), and 海 (ocean). Can you spot the water badge 氵 in each one? What does this tell you?

### 休 (12.7s)

## 休

**Story: Finding shelter**
Imagine you've been running around all day, and you're tired. Your legs are sore, your energy is gone. You look around and see a big tree. You lean your back against its trunk, and suddenly everything feels better—the shade cools you down, the solid wood supports you. You're not moving anymore. You're resting.

**The picture inside the word**
When Chinese writers wanted a picture-word for "rest," they thought of exactly this moment: a person against a tree. They drew the person (人) standing next to the tree (木), and together those two ideas became one word—休—that captures what rest really means.

**How the character builds:**
$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = 休 \text{ (rest)}$$

**Now you try**
Next time you rest against a wall, a pillow, or a friend's shoulder, think about what you're doing: you're putting your body somewhere solid so you can stop working. That's exactly what 休 means. Can you think of other times a person leans on something? Those moments are all 休.

### 众 (14.7s)

## 众

**Picture this:** Imagine you're alone on a playground—just you. One friend arrives, then another. Now there are three of you playing. But when ten more kids show up and you're surrounded by running, laughing, shouting people? That feels completely different. You've gone from "someone is here" to "wow, there's a whole crowd!"

**Why does it work?** The Chinese character 众 captures exactly this idea. It shows what happens when you gather people together. One person is just one person, but when you keep adding more and more people, something shifts—you have a crowd, a group, many people all together.

**How it's built:**

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = \text{众}$$

The character stacks three of the person radical (人). That's the secret—just like real crowds form when you bring many humans together, this character builds its meaning by putting person on top of person on top of person. More humans = 众.

**Now you try:** You learned that 森 (forest) also uses stacking. If 众 is three people making a crowd, what do you think three trees stacked together create?

### 吗 (9.8s)

## 吗

**Picture this.** Your friend tells you, "You like pizza." You nod. But then they ask, "You like pizza?" Same words, but now it's a question. What changed? Nothing but how you said it—and sometimes, a tiny marker at the end. In Chinese, that marker is 吗.

**Here's the key idea.** When you want to turn a statement into a yes-or-no question, you can add 吗 at the very end. It's like a signal that says, "Hey, I'm asking, not telling!" For example:
- 你喜欢书 (You like books) becomes 你喜欢书吗? (Do you like books?)

**Why this character?** Look at how 吗 is built:

$$\underbrace{口}_{\text{mouth/spoken}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{ma sound}} = \text{吗}$$

The mouth reminds us that questions are something we *speak*. The 马 (ma) gives us the sound. Together, they make a tiny sound-marker that transforms your voice from stating to asking.

**The clever part.** Notice that 吗 sounds exactly like 妈 (mom), but they're different characters doing different jobs. Same sound, totally different meanings—just like how "to" and "too" sound alike in English but mean different things.

**Now you try.** Take a sentence like 他在家 (He is at home) and add 吗 to turn it into a question. Say it aloud. Can you hear how your voice rises?

### 妈 (19.0s)

## 妈

**Picture this:** Imagine you're learning a language where people invented pictures to write with. Some pictures look like the thing they mean—a mouth means "mouth," a tree means "tree." But then, how do you write a word that's hard to draw, like the sound "ma"?

**The clever trick:** In Chinese, people found a smart way. They took a picture that meant something close to what they wanted to explain—in this case, a picture of a woman—and added a piece that told them how to say it. That piece sounds like "ma."

### 字 (10.8s)

## 字

**Picture this:** Imagine you're an ancient scribe, and you've just invented a new picture by combining two simpler pictures. How would you name the act of creating something new? What would you call that?

The Chinese scribes chose 字 (*zì*), which shows a child (子) sitting under a roof (宀). Why a child? Because children are born and raised under roofs. And because new characters are "born and raised" — created and developed — from simpler pieces. The whole system named *itself* with this beautiful image.

**See the pattern:** Every complex character in Chinese started as a simple idea (like a roof, a child, the sun, or water). Then scribes combined these simple pictures to build new meanings. The character 字 captures this whole creative process: taking what you know, putting it together under shelter, and watching something new grow.

The composition is:
$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{字}$$

**Now you try:** Think about your own name or a word you know well. Can you imagine it as a "child" made from smaller pieces? What simple ideas would you combine to build it?

### 明 (28.3s)

## 明

Picture yourself outside on a night when the moon is full and glowing. It's like nature handed you a light. But then the sun rises in the morning, and suddenly everything floods with brightness. What if someone asked you to draw a picture of brightness itself—not just one light, but *all* the light in the world?

Long ago, the Chinese had an insight: the sun by itself shines bright, and the moon by itself shines bright too. But imagine them together—that's the idea of true brightness. It's not about one light or the other. It's about completeness, about all the light there is.

$$\underbrace{日}_{sun} + \underbrace{月}_{moon} = 明$$

### 森 (16.1s)

## 森

**Picture this:** You're walking through the woods. At first, you spot one tree standing alone in a clearing. Keep walking, and you see two trees growing close together—a small huddle of green. Walk deeper still, and suddenly there are so many trees pressed together that you can barely see the sky. That thick, crowded mass of green? That's a forest. More trees stacked up means something denser and bigger.

The Chinese character for forest, 森 (sēn), tells this exact story by drawing it. It doesn't use words—it *builds* the idea by piling trees on top of each other.

**How it's built:** Start with 木 (mù), the character that looks like a little tree with a trunk and branches. Now copy it twice: put one tree next to another, and you get 林 (lín), meaning "small grove." Copy it three times and stack them up: 

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \text{ (forest)}$$

See the pattern? One tree is just a tree. Two trees side by side make a group. Three trees stacked together? Now you've got a forest—something thick and crowded. The shape of the character literally shows how much "treeness" you've piled up.

**Now you try:** Think of other things where having more of something changes what it *is*. What about one person versus a crowd, or one drop of water versus the ocean?

### 沐 (19.4s)

## 沐

Imagine you already know how to say the sound *mu*—maybe you've heard it in other words. You also know that water 水 washes things. What if you could combine those two ideas? Put the sound *mu* together with the washing meaning, and you'd have a new character: 沐, "to wash yourself."

Here's the clever trick Chinese writers discovered: sometimes you don't need a picture to explain what a character means. Sometimes you can take a sound from one character and pair it with a *meaning* from another. In 沐, the water radical 水 shows you what's happening—the washing. The wood 木 is there just to make the right sound: *mu*. It's like saying, "You know how to pronounce this? Great—now imagine water doing that action."

This pattern is:

$$\underbrace{水}_{meaning: to wash} + \underbrace{木}_{sound: mu} = \text{沐}$$

Think about your own hands. When you wash them, what do you see? The water 水. What do you say? The sound *mu*. That's exactly what this character shows you.

### phonetic_borrowing (50.2s)

## Phonetic Borrowing

**A borrowed trumpet**: Imagine your best friend plays trumpet really well. You ask them to play at your birthday party because you need that sound. Your friend still plays trumpet in their band—they just do both! That's how phonetic borrowing works in Chinese characters.

A piece of a character can be really good at making one sound. Other characters can borrow that piece for their sound. It's the same brick, doing the same sound job, but helping to make different words.

Watch the horse piece 马 lend its sound "mā" to two different words:

$$\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{马}_{horse} = \underbrace{妈}_{mother}$$

$$\underbrace{口}_{mouth} + \underbrace{马}_{horse} = \underbrace{吗}_{question marker}$$

Same piece. Same sound. Different meanings! The horse piece is the "trumpet player."

**Now you try**: When you see a character made of two parts, ask: "Does one part sound like a word I know? Is it borrowing that sound?" You're a character detective!

### semantic_composition (27.9s)

## Semantic Composition

**Imagine a puzzle:** You see a picture of a person resting against a tree. Without anyone explaining, you *feel* what rest means. Two pictures—one idea. This is how many Chinese characters are built.

In Chinese, simple pictures stack together to create meaning. A person + tree makes "rest." The pictures combine their meanings into something new.

**Why does this work?** Your brain doesn't see two separate drawings—it sees one connected thought. It's like building blocks clicking together. Each piece has its own meaning, and when you join them, something bigger emerges.

Here are examples using the same building block:

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 休 \text{ (rest)}$$

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \text{ (forest)}$$

**Now you try:** If one tree (木) means tree, and two trees (木木) stacked together means "forest," what do you think three trees close together might feel like? Can you picture it?

### phono_semantic_principle (47.9s)

## Phono Semantic Principle

**Picture this:** You walk into a toy store and see thousands of different LEGO creations. But here's the secret—they're all made from the same small pile of bricks. That's how Chinese characters work.

Imagine your friend shows you a character you've never seen before. You panic—it looks completely new! But wait. If you look closely, you notice it has two pieces stuck together inside it. One piece (on the left or bottom usually) gives you a *hint about the meaning*. The other piece (often on the right or top) gives you a *hint about how to say it*.

Think of it this way:

$$\underbrace{\text{meaning piece}}_{\text{semantic}} + \underbrace{\text{sound piece}}_{\text{phonetic}} = \text{whole character}$$

For example, characters about water often have a water-shaped piece (三点水). Characters about words or mouth often have a mouth-shaped piece. But the right side tells you the *pronunciation*—the sound brick.

**Now you try:** When you see a new character, ask yourself: "What does the left part remind me of? What could this character mean?" Then listen to someone say it. You've just decoded a new character using the LEGO method!
