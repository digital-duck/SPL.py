### column_vector (0.0s)

## Column Vector

A column vector is an ordered list of $m$ numbers arranged vertically:

$$
\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_m \end{bmatrix}
$$

Each $v_i$ occupies a fixed position, so $\mathbf{v}$ is formally an $m \times 1$ matrix — one column, $m$ rows. Order matters: $\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}$ are different vectors even though they contain the same numbers. This structure is what lets a column vector represent a *point* in $m$-dimensional space, a *direction*, or a *solution* to a system of equations — the position of each entry encodes which variable or coordinate it belongs to.

**Worked example.** Consider the linear system

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x + y &= 5 \\
x - y &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

The constants on the right-hand side form the column vector $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and the unknowns form $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$. The coefficients form a matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$, so the whole system compresses into a single equation, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. Solving gives $x = 2$, $y = 1$, so the solution itself is the column vector $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ — a single object representing the entire answer.

**Problem-solving application.** Writing quantities as column vectors is what makes matrix algebra usable for real systems: instead of tracking each equation separately, you track one vector equation, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, and every operation on the system (elimination, scaling, substitution) becomes an operation on vectors and matrices. This generalizes cleanly — a system with $n$ unknowns and $m$ equations packs into an $m \times n$ matrix acting on an $n \times 1$ column vector to produce an $m \times 1$ column vector. When you later solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ by Gaussian elimination or by computing $A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$, you are manipulating column vectors directly, so recognizing which quantity plays the role of unknowns versus constants — and keeping their order consistent with the matrix's columns — is the first step in setting up any linear system correctly.

### complex_conjugate_of_vector (12.1s)

## Complex Conjugate Of Vector

Given a vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{C}^n$ with entries $v_k = a_k + i b_k$, its complex conjugate $\overline{\mathbf{v}}$ is the vector obtained by conjugating each entry individually:

$$\overline{\mathbf{v}} = \begin{pmatrix} \overline{v_1} \\ \overline{v_2} \\ \vdots \\ \overline{v_n} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 - i b_1 \\ a_2 - i b_2 \\ \vdots \\ a_n - i b_n \end{pmatrix}$$

This is a purely entrywise operation — no reordering, transposing, or scaling is involved. It matters wherever complex numbers arise naturally from oscillations, waves, or quantum states, because conjugation is the algebraic tool that recovers real, measurable quantities from complex representations.

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 + 2i \\ -1 + 4i \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$. Conjugating each entry gives

$$\overline{\mathbf{v}} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 - 2i \\ -1 - 4i \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$$

Notice the real entry $5$ is unchanged, since $\overline{5} = 5 - 0i = 5$.

**Problem-solving application.** The conjugate vector is the essential ingredient in defining a well-behaved notion of "length" for complex vectors. If you tried to compute $\sum v_k^2$ directly, the result could be a negative or complex number — useless as a measure of magnitude. Instead, the squared norm is defined using the conjugate:

$$\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^n v_k \overline{v_k} = \sum_{k=1}^n |v_k|^2$$

which is guaranteed real and non-negative. For the vector above:

$$\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = (3+2i)(3-2i) + (-1+4i)(-1-4i) + 5 \cdot 5 = (9+4) + (1+16) + 25 = 55$$

This same idea extends to the inner product on $\mathbb{C}^n$: $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \sum_k u_k \overline{v_k}$, which conjugates the *second* argument so that $\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$ is always real. This convention underlies Hermitian matrices, unitary transformations, and quantum mechanics, where conjugating a state vector's amplitudes is how probabilities (always real and non-negative) are extracted from complex-valued wavefunctions.

### inner_product (0.0s)

## Inner Product

A vector space $V$ tells you how vectors add and scale, but by itself it has no notion of length, angle, or perpendicularity. An **inner product** is an additional structure — a function $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ — that supplies geometry. It must satisfy three properties: bilinearity (linear in each argument separately), symmetry ($\langle u, v \rangle = \langle v, u \rangle$), and positive-definiteness ($\langle v, v \rangle \geq 0$, with equality only when $v = \mathbf{0}$). This last property is what lets you define length: the **norm** of a vector is $\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v, v \rangle}$, and it is never zero unless the vector itself is.

The familiar example is the dot product on $\mathbb{R}^n$: $\langle u, v \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i$. From it you recover the law of cosines in disguise — the **Cauchy-Schwarz inequality** $|\langle u, v \rangle| \leq \|u\|\|v\|$ — which guarantees that

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\langle u, v \rangle}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}
$$

is always a valid number between $-1$ and $1$, so "angle between vectors" makes sense in any space with an inner product, not just $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Worked example.** Let $u = (2, -1, 3)$ and $v = (1, 4, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Then $\langle u, v \rangle = 2(1) + (-1)(4) + 3(1) = 1$, $\|u\| = \sqrt{4+1+9} = \sqrt{14}$, $\|v\| = \sqrt{1+16+1} = \sqrt{18}$. So $\cos\theta = 1/\sqrt{252} \approx 0.063$, meaning $u$ and $v$ are nearly perpendicular even though neither component-wise comparison would suggest it. This is the essential payoff: the inner product converts algebraic data into geometric fact.

**Problem-solving application.** Two vectors are **orthogonal** when $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$, and orthogonality is the tool for decomposing a vector into independent, non-interfering parts. That decomposition is built from the inner product itself: to split $u$ into a part parallel to $v$ and a part orthogonal to $v$, you compute $\frac{\langle u, v \rangle}{\langle v, v \rangle}\, v$ — the component of $u$ lying along $v$ — and subtract it off. This single construction, expressed purely in terms of the inner product, underlies least-squares regression (splitting a data vector into its part along a model's predictions and the leftover error), Fourier analysis (splitting a function into its sine and cosine components using $\langle f, g \rangle = \int f g\, dx$ as the inner product), and the Gram-Schmidt process for building orthogonal bases. None of this is recoverable from vector addition or scalar multiplication alone — it requires the inner product's positive-definite geometry, which is why it stands as an independent structure on $V$ rather than a byproduct of the vector space axioms.

### scalar_multiplication (0.0s)

## Scalar Multiplication

**Definition.** Scalar multiplication takes a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and a vector $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n)$ and produces a new vector by multiplying every component by $c$:

$$c\mathbf{v} = (cv_1, cv_2, \ldots, cv_n)$$

Geometrically, this stretches or shrinks $\mathbf{v}$ by a factor of $|c|$, and flips its direction if $c < 0$. If $|c| > 1$, the vector lengthens; if $0 < |c| < 1$, it shortens; if $c = 0$, it collapses to the zero vector. Two nonzero vectors $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ are called **parallel** (or scalar multiples of each other) precisely when $\mathbf{w} = c\mathbf{v}$ for some scalar $c$ — this single operation is what lets us formalize the idea of "same direction, different length."

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{v} = (3, -2, 1)$. Compute $2\mathbf{v}$, $-1\mathbf{v}$, and $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}$:

$$2\mathbf{v} = (6, -4, 2), \qquad -\mathbf{v} = (-3, 2, -1), \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v} = \left(\tfrac{3}{2}, -1, \tfrac{1}{2}\right)$$

Each result points along the same line through the origin as $\mathbf{v}$; only the length and, for $-\mathbf{v}$, the direction change. Scalar multiplication satisfies key algebraic properties that make it compatible with vector addition — distributivity, $c(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = c\mathbf{v} + c\mathbf{w}$, and associativity, $(cd)\mathbf{v} = c(d\mathbf{v})$ — which together with addition make $\mathbb{R}^n$ a vector space.

**Problem-solving application.** A common use is normalizing a vector: converting $\mathbf{v}$ into a unit vector (length 1) pointing in the same direction, by choosing $c = \tfrac{1}{\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert}$. For $\mathbf{v} = (3, 4)$, we have $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, so the unit vector is

$$\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{1}{5}(3, 4) = (0.6, 0.8)$$

This technique appears constantly in physics (finding a direction of force without its magnitude) and in computer graphics (normalizing surface normals for lighting calculations). Scalar multiplication also lets you test whether a given vector lies along a specific line: solve $c\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}$ component-by-component and check that a single consistent $c$ satisfies all equations — if it does, the vectors are parallel; if not, they diverge in direction.

### vector_addition (0.0s)

## Vector Addition

The first radical of a vector space is closure under a binary operation: given two vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$, their sum $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ must also belong to $V$. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, addition is defined componentwise:

$$
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n) + (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n) = (u_1+v_1, u_2+v_2, \dots, u_n+v_n)
$$

Geometrically, this is the tip-to-tail rule: place the tail of $\mathbf{v}$ at the tip of $\mathbf{u}$; the sum is the arrow from the origin to the new tip. The operation is commutative ($\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}$) and associative, and it has an identity element, the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$, since $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{u}$ for every $\mathbf{u}$. These properties are not incidental — they are exactly the axioms that make $V$ a group under addition, and closure is what guarantees the operation never sends you outside the space.

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{u} = (3, -1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (-2, 4, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Then

$$
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (3+(-2),\ -1+4,\ 2+1) = (1, 3, 3)
$$

The result is again a triple of real numbers, confirming closure: $\mathbf{R}^3$ never produces a vector with, say, four components or a non-real entry.

**Problem-solving application.** Closure becomes a diagnostic tool, not just a formality, when you check whether a subset $W \subseteq V$ is itself a subspace. Suppose $W = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$, the unit circle. Take $\mathbf{u} = (1, 0)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (0, 1)$, both in $W$. Their sum is $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$, and $1^2 + 1^2 = 2 \ne 1$, so the sum leaves $W$. Closure fails, and $W$ is not a subspace — no further axioms need checking. This test generalizes: whenever you must decide if a candidate set (a solution set of a linear system, the set of polynomials of bounded degree, the set of continuous functions satisfying $f(0)=0$) forms a vector space, verifying closure under addition first is the fastest way to either rule it out or clear the path to confirming the remaining axioms.

### linear_combination (0.0s)

## Linear Combination

A **linear combination** of vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ is any vector built by scaling each one and adding the results together:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i = \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \cdots + \alpha_n v_n
$$

where the $\alpha_i$ are scalars. This single expression is the atomic operation of linear algebra — every subspace, every matrix-vector product, every solution to a linear system is, at bottom, a statement about which linear combinations are achievable and which are not.

**Worked example.** Let $v_1 = (1, 0)$ and $v_2 = (0, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The vector $(3, -2)$ is the linear combination

$$
3v_1 + (-2)v_2 = 3(1,0) + (-2)(0,1) = (3, -2).
$$

Because $v_1$ and $v_2$ span all of $\mathbb{R}^2$, *every* vector in the plane can be written this way, with the coordinates themselves serving as the scalars $\alpha_i$. This is why $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots)$ and "linear combination of the standard basis vectors" mean the same thing.

**Problem-solving application: is $b$ reachable?** A recurring question in linear algebra is whether a target vector $b$ can be expressed as a linear combination of a given set $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$. This is exactly the question $Ax = b$ asks, where the columns of $A$ are the $v_i$ and $x$ holds the unknown scalars $\alpha_i$. Solving the system is nothing more than searching for coefficients that make the combination land on $b$.

For instance, suppose $v_1 = (1, 2)$, $v_2 = (2, 4)$, and $b = (3, 5)$. Setting up $\alpha_1(1,2) + \alpha_2(2,4) = (3,5)$ gives the system

$$
\alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2 = 3, \qquad 2\alpha_1 + 4\alpha_2 = 5.
$$

The second equation is exactly twice the first equation's left side but not its right side ($2 \times 3 = 6 \neq 5$), so no scalars satisfy both — $b$ is *not* a linear combination of $v_1$ and $v_2$. Geometrically, $v_1$ and $v_2$ are parallel (both point along the direction $(1,2)$), so every combination of them lies on that one line, and $b$ sits off it.

This reachability test — can a target be written as $\sum \alpha_i v_i$ — is the seed from which span, linear independence, and the rank of a matrix all grow.

### orthogonal_vectors (12.7s)

## Orthogonal Vectors

Two vectors are **orthogonal** if their inner product equals zero. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, this means for vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$,

$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i = 0.
$$

Geometrically, orthogonality generalizes perpendicularity: since $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$, an inner product of zero forces $\cos\theta = 0$, meaning $\theta = 90°$ (whenever both vectors are nonzero). This connection is why the concept extends cleanly beyond ordinary 2D or 3D space — to function spaces, signal spaces, and any setting equipped with an inner product — since "angle" itself is *defined* through the inner product rather than measured with a protractor.

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{u} = (3, -2, 1)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (1, 2, 1)$. Their inner product is
$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (3)(1) + (-2)(2) + (1)(1) = 3 - 4 + 1 = 0.
$$
Since the result is zero, $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal, even though neither vector is unusually "simple" — orthogonality is a computed property, not something visually obvious from the entries.

**Problem-solving application.** A key use of orthogonality is testing whether a proposed basis is well-behaved. Suppose you are given three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and asked to confirm they form an orthogonal set (pairwise orthogonal, none zero): $\mathbf{a} = (1,1,0)$, $\mathbf{b} = (1,-1,0)$, $\mathbf{c} = (0,0,2)$. Check each pair:
$$
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 1-1+0 = 0, \qquad \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c} = 0, \qquad \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{c} = 0.
$$
All three pairwise products vanish, so the set is orthogonal. This matters computationally: an orthogonal basis lets you find the coordinates of any vector $\mathbf{x}$ by projecting onto each basis vector independently,
$$
c_i = \frac{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{v}_i}{\mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_i},
$$
rather than solving a full system of linear equations — a substantial shortcut used throughout linear algebra, Fourier analysis, and numerical methods, wherever an orthogonal (or orthonormal) basis is available.

### linear_independence (0.0s)

## Linear Independence

A set of vectors $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ is **linearly independent** if the only linear combination of them that equals the zero vector is the trivial one — every coefficient equal to zero. Formally:

$$c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \cdots + c_n v_n = \mathbf{0} \quad \implies \quad c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_n = 0.$$

If some nontrivial combination (not all $c_i = 0$) also produces $\mathbf{0}$, the set is **linearly dependent** — meaning at least one vector in the set is redundant, expressible as a combination of the others.

**Worked example.** Consider $v_1 = (1, 2)$ and $v_2 = (2, 4)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Set up $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 = \mathbf{0}$:

$$c_1(1,2) + c_2(2,4) = (0,0) \implies c_1 + 2c_2 = 0, \quad 2c_1 + 4c_2 = 0.$$

The second equation is just twice the first, so any pair with $c_1 = -2c_2$ works — for instance $c_1 = 2, c_2 = -1$. Since a nontrivial solution exists, $\{v_1, v_2\}$ is linearly dependent; indeed $v_2 = 2v_1$, so $v_2$ adds no new direction.

Now try $v_1 = (1, 0)$ and $v_2 = (0, 1)$. The system $c_1 = 0, c_2 = 0$ forces the trivial solution only, so this set is linearly independent.

**Problem-solving application.** The practical test for independence of $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is to form the matrix $A$ with the vectors as columns and check whether $\det(A) \neq 0$, or equivalently, whether $A$ row-reduces to a matrix with $n$ pivot positions (full rank). A zero determinant signals dependence — some column is a combination of the others — while a nonzero determinant guarantees independence.

This test matters beyond bookkeeping: linear independence determines whether a set of vectors can serve as a *basis* — a minimal spanning set for a space. In applications like solving systems of equations, checking independence tells you whether a system has a unique solution (independent columns) or infinitely many/no solutions (dependent columns). In data science, checking whether feature vectors are linearly independent flags redundant variables before regression, since dependent predictors make coefficient estimates unstable — a phenomenon called multicollinearity.

### orthogonal_set (10.4s)

## Orthogonal Set

A set of vectors $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ is called an **orthogonal set** if every pair of distinct vectors in the set is orthogonal — that is, $v_i \cdot v_j = 0$ whenever $i \neq j$. The definition places no restriction on the vectors' lengths; it only constrains their relative directions. When each vector in an orthogonal set additionally has unit length, the set is called *orthonormal* — a stronger and often more convenient condition, but not required here.

**Worked example.** Consider
$$
v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \quad
v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \quad
v_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Checking all three pairs: $v_1 \cdot v_2 = (1)(1) + (1)(-1) + (0)(0) = 0$, $v_1 \cdot v_3 = 0$, and $v_2 \cdot v_3 = 0$. Since every pair vanishes, $\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$ is an orthogonal set.

**Theorem (linear independence of orthogonal sets).** If $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ is an orthogonal set of *nonzero* vectors, then the set is linearly independent.

*Proof.* Suppose $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \cdots + c_n v_n = 0$. Take the dot product of both sides with $v_i$ for any fixed $i$:
$$
c_1(v_1\cdot v_i) + \cdots + c_i(v_i \cdot v_i) + \cdots + c_n(v_n \cdot v_i) = 0.
$$
Every term with $j \neq i$ vanishes by orthogonality, leaving $c_i(v_i \cdot v_i) = 0$. Since $v_i \neq 0$, $v_i \cdot v_i = \|v_i\|^2 > 0$, forcing $c_i = 0$. This holds for every $i$, so all coefficients are zero and the set is linearly independent. $\blacksquare$

**Problem-solving application.** This theorem is the reason orthogonal sets are so useful computationally: an orthogonal set of $n$ nonzero vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is automatically a basis, and expressing any vector $b$ in that basis avoids solving a linear system entirely. Instead of Gaussian elimination, each coefficient is computed directly as $c_i = \dfrac{b \cdot v_i}{v_i \cdot v_i}$ — a single dot product per coefficient, independent of the others. This efficiency underlies the Gram–Schmidt process, least-squares regression, and Fourier-style decompositions, where converting a basis into an orthogonal one turns an otherwise coupled system into $n$ independent one-line computations.

### gram_schmidt_procedure (11.1s)

## Gram Schmidt Procedure

Given a linearly independent set of vectors $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ in an inner product space, the Gram-Schmidt procedure constructs an orthogonal set $\{u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n\}$ that spans the same subspace. Each new vector is built by subtracting from $v_k$ its projections onto all previously constructed orthogonal vectors, removing every component that overlaps with the space already spanned:

$$
u_1 = v_1, \qquad u_k = v_k - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \text{proj}_{u_i}(v_k), \quad \text{proj}_{u_i}(v_k) = \frac{\langle v_k, u_i \rangle}{\langle u_i, u_i \rangle}\, u_i
$$

Normalizing each $u_i$ by dividing by its norm yields an orthonormal basis, the form most useful in applications.

**Worked example.** Let $v_1 = (1, 1, 0)$ and $v_2 = (1, 0, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Set $u_1 = v_1 = (1,1,0)$. Then

$$
\text{proj}_{u_1}(v_2) = \frac{\langle v_2, u_1\rangle}{\langle u_1, u_1\rangle} u_1 = \frac{1}{2}(1,1,0)
$$

so $u_2 = v_2 - \tfrac{1}{2}(1,1,0) = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}, 1\right)$. Checking $\langle u_1, u_2\rangle = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} + 0 = 0$ confirms orthogonality. Normalizing gives $e_1 = u_1/\|u_1\| = \left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, 0\right)$ and $e_2 = u_2/\|u_2\|$.

**Why it matters for problem-solving.** Orthogonal bases turn hard problems into easy ones: coordinates of a vector along an orthogonal basis are computed independently, one inner product at a time, instead of solving a linear system. This is the mechanism behind the $QR$ decomposition, where the orthonormal vectors $e_i$ form the columns of $Q$ and the projection coefficients form the upper-triangular matrix $R$, satisfying $A = QR$ for a matrix $A$ whose columns are the original $v_i$. $QR$ decomposition, in turn, underlies stable algorithms for solving least-squares regression, since the normal equations $A^TAx = A^Tb$ become $Rx = Q^Tb$, a triangular system solved directly by back-substitution without the numerical instability of computing $A^TA$. When implementing Gram-Schmidt numerically, the "modified" version — subtracting each projection immediately rather than accumulating $v_k$ against all $u_i$ at once — is preferred, since it substantially reduces rounding-error buildup in floating-point computation.
