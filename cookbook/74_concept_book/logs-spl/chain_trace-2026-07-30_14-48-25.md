### sequence (0.0s)

## Sequence

**Definition.** A *sequence* is a function $a : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ (or more generally, into any set $S$). We write its values as an ordered list $(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots)$, or in compact notation $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$. The integer $n$ is called the *index* and $a_n$ the $n$-th *term*. A sequence is *finite* if the domain is $\{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ for some $N \in \mathbb{N}$, and *infinite* otherwise. Two sequences are equal if and only if $a_n = b_n$ for every index $n$ in their shared domain.

**Worked Example.** Consider the sequence defined by $a_n = \dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$. Listing the first five terms:

$$a_1 = 1,\quad a_2 = -\tfrac{1}{2},\quad a_3 = \tfrac{1}{3},\quad a_4 = -\tfrac{1}{4},\quad a_5 = \tfrac{1}{5}.$$

The terms alternate in sign and decrease in absolute value toward zero. This is the sequence of partial harmonic terms with alternating sign, and it plays a central role in the theory of conditionally convergent series.

**Key Theorem (Squeeze Theorem for Sequences).** Let $\{a_n\}$, $\{b_n\}$, $\{c_n\}$ be sequences satisfying $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. If

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L = \lim_{n \to \infty} c_n,$$

then $\lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = L$ as well.

*Proof sketch.* Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N$ large enough that $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ and $|c_n - L| < \varepsilon$ for all $n > N$. The sandwich inequality $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ then forces $L - \varepsilon < b_n < L + \varepsilon$, i.e., $|b_n - L| < \varepsilon$. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import *

n = symbols('n', positive=True, integer=True)

a = ((-1)**(n + 1)) / n

# Compute and display first ten terms
terms = [a.subs(n, k) for k in range(1, 11)]
print("Terms:", terms)

# Verify the limit
print("Limit:", limit(a, n, oo))
```

Running this cell confirms that each term matches the pattern derived above and that $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$, consistent with the Squeeze Theorem applied with $c_n = 1/n$ and $a_n = -1/n$.

### seasonal_component (0.0s)

## Seasonal Component

**Definition.** Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ be a time series observed at equally spaced intervals. The *seasonal component* $S_t$ is a periodic function satisfying

$$S_t = S_{t+m} \quad \forall\, t,$$

where $m \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$ is the *period* (e.g., $m = 12$ for monthly data, $m = 4$ for quarterly data). In the classical additive decomposition

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

$T_t$ denotes the trend, and $\varepsilon_t$ is the irregular residual. In the multiplicative form, $y_t = T_t \cdot S_t \cdot \varepsilon_t$, the seasonal factor is expressed as a ratio rather than an additive offset. The additive model is preferred when seasonal fluctuations are roughly constant in magnitude; the multiplicative model is preferred when they scale with the level of the series.

**Worked Example.** Consider average monthly electricity demand (GWh) over three years. Visual inspection reveals demand peaks each July–August (summer cooling) and dips each April–May. To isolate $S_t$, compute a *centered moving average* (CMA) of order $m = 12$:

$$\hat{T}_t = \frac{1}{12}\sum_{k=-5}^{6} y_{t+k}.$$

Subtract the CMA from the raw series to obtain detrended values $d_t = y_t - \hat{T}_t$. Average $d_t$ across all occurrences of each calendar month to produce twelve seasonal indices $\hat{S}_1, \ldots, \hat{S}_{12}$. For identifiability, impose the constraint $\sum_{j=1}^{12} \hat{S}_j = 0$ (additive case) or $\prod_{j=1}^{12} \hat{S}_j = 1$ (multiplicative case).

**Key Theorem (Uniqueness of Additive Decomposition).** If $y_t = T_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t$ with $S_t$ strictly $m$-periodic and $\sum_{j=1}^{m} S_j = 0$, then $T_t$ and $S_t$ are separately identifiable from $y_t$ up to a constant, provided $T_t$ varies on a timescale much larger than $m$.

*Proof sketch.* Applying the centered moving average operator annihilates $S_t$ (since its $m$-period sum is zero) and smooths $\varepsilon_t$, leaving a consistent estimate of $T_t$. The seasonal component is then recovered by subtraction. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
m      = 12
time   = np.arange(1, 37)                        # 3 years of monthly data
trend  = 0.4 * time + 100
season = 5 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * time / m)        # known ground truth
series = trend + season + rng.normal(0, 1, len(time))

# Centered moving average (the one new primitive introduced here)
cma = np.convolve(series, np.ones(m) / m, mode='same')
detrended = series - cma

# Average each calendar month across years
indices = np.array([detrended[month::m].mean() for month in range(m)])
print(np.round(indices, 2))
```

Running this cell prints twelve estimated seasonal indices. Because `np.convolve` with a uniform kernel of length $m$ implements the CMA operator from the worked example, the output should closely recover the cosine pattern embedded in the synthetic series.

### time_series (0.0s)

## Time Series

**Definition.** A *time series* is a sequence of observations $\{x_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ indexed by discrete time $t \in \mathbb{Z}$, where each $x_t$ is a realization of a random variable $X_t$. The collection $\{X_t\}$ is called a *stochastic process*. A time series is said to be *weakly stationary* (or *covariance-stationary*) if its mean and autocovariance structure are invariant under time shifts:

$$\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu \quad \text{for all } t,$$
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \gamma(k) \quad \text{for all } t, k,$$

where $\gamma(k)$ is called the *autocovariance function* at lag $k$. The normalized version, $\rho(k) = \gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$, is the *autocorrelation function* (ACF). These two quantities — $\gamma$ and $\rho$ — are the primary tools for characterizing the internal dependence structure of a stationary series.

**Worked Example.** Consider daily closing prices of a stock over $T = 5$ days: $x_1 = 100,\ x_2 = 102,\ x_3 = 101,\ x_4 = 105,\ x_5 = 103$. Compute the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{t=1}^{5} x_t = 102.2$. The lag-1 sample autocovariance is

$$\hat{\gamma}(1) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=2}^{T}(x_t - \bar{x})(x_{t-1} - \bar{x}) = \frac{1}{5}\bigl[(-1.2)(-2.2) + (-1.2)(-0.2) + (2.8)(-1.2) + (0.8)(2.8)\bigr] = 0.56.$$

A positive $\hat{\gamma}(1)$ indicates that consecutive observations tend to move together — a feature common in financial data.

**Key Theorem (Properties of the ACF).** For any weakly stationary process $\{X_t\}$, the autocorrelation function satisfies:

$$\rho(0) = 1, \qquad \rho(-k) = \rho(k) \quad \text{for all } k \in \mathbb{Z}, \qquad |\rho(k)| \leq 1.$$

Symmetry follows directly from the definition of covariance; the bound $|\rho(k)| \leq 1$ is a consequence of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $X_t$ and $X_{t+k}$. Together, these constraints rule out spurious correlation estimates outside $[-1, 1]$ and allow the ACF to be plotted on a standardized scale — the foundation of all graphical stationarity diagnostics.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
import sympy as sp

k = sp.Symbol('k', integer=True)
phi, sigma2 = sp.symbols('phi sigma2', positive=True)

# AR(1) autocovariance: gamma(k) = sigma^2 * phi^|k| / (1 - phi^2)
gamma = sigma2 * phi**sp.Abs(k) / (1 - phi**2)
rho   = sp.simplify(gamma / gamma.subs(k, 0))
sp.pprint(rho)  # yields phi^|k|; verify rho(0)=1 and symmetry
```

### trend (0.0s)

## Trend

**Definition.** Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{n}$ be a time series observed at equally spaced time points $t = 1, 2, \ldots, n$. The *trend* of the series is the smooth, long-run component $T_t$ such that

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $S_t$ denotes any periodic (seasonal) component and $\varepsilon_t$ is zero-mean random noise. Informally, the trend captures the direction and rate of sustained change in the mean level of the series, abstracting away short-term fluctuations. A trend is called *linear* if $T_t = \alpha + \beta t$ for constants $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, and *nonlinear* otherwise.

**Worked Example.** Suppose annual global mean temperature anomalies (in °C relative to a 1951–1980 baseline) are modelled as $y_t = 0.02\,t - 0.10 + \varepsilon_t$ for $t = 1, \ldots, 50$. The trend component is $T_t = 0.02\,t - 0.10$, indicating a sustained warming of $0.02$ °C per year. At $t = 25$ the predicted mean anomaly is $T_{25} = 0.02(25) - 0.10 = 0.40$ °C. The slope $\beta = 0.02$ is the *trend rate*; its sign determines whether the series is *upward* ($\beta > 0$) or *downward* ($\beta < 0$) trending.

**Key Theorem (Ordinary Least-Squares Trend Estimation).** Given observations $\{(t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{n}$, the OLS estimators of the linear trend parameters are

$$\hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(t - \bar{t})(y_t - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(t - \bar{t})^2}, \qquad \hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}\,\bar{t},$$

where $\bar{t} = (n+1)/2$ and $\bar{y} = n^{-1}\sum y_t$. Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions ($\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t]=0$, constant variance, no autocorrelation), $\hat{\alpha}$ and $\hat{\beta}$ are the Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUE) of the true trend parameters.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy import symbols, summation, simplify, Rational

t, n = symbols('t n', positive=True, integer=True)

# Symbolic numerator of beta-hat for uniform time index
y_t = 2*t - 1          # example: y_t = 2t - 1 (exact linear series)
t_bar = (n + 1) / 2
numerator   = summation((t - t_bar) * (y_t - summation(y_t, (t, 1, n)) / n), (t, 1, n))
denominator = summation((t - t_bar)**2, (t, 1, n))
beta_hat = simplify(numerator / denominator)
print("Estimated slope:", beta_hat)   # Expected: 2
```

Running this cell confirms that for a perfectly linear series the OLS formula recovers the true slope exactly, providing an algebraic sanity check before applying the estimator to noisy empirical data.

### volatility (0.0s)

## Volatility

**Definition.** Let $\{S_t\}_{t \geq 0}$ be the price process of a financial asset. The *log-return* over the interval $[t, t+\Delta t]$ is defined as

$$r_t = \ln\!\left(\frac{S_{t+\Delta t}}{S_t}\right).$$

The *volatility* $\sigma$ of the asset is the annualized standard deviation of its log-returns:

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(r_t)} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1}{\Delta t}},$$

where $\Delta t$ is expressed as a fraction of one year. When returns are sampled daily, $\Delta t = 1/252$ (the conventional number of trading days per year), so the annualization factor is $\sqrt{252}$. Volatility quantifies the dispersion of price movements and serves as the primary measure of risk in continuous-time finance.

**Worked Example.** Suppose five consecutive daily closing prices are $S = (100, 102, 99, 103, 101)$. Compute the four daily log-returns:

$$r_1 = \ln(102/100) \approx 0.0198,\quad r_2 = \ln(99/102) \approx -0.0299,$$
$$r_3 = \ln(103/99) \approx 0.0392,\quad r_4 = \ln(101/103) \approx -0.0194.$$

The sample mean is $\bar{r} \approx 0.0024$ and the sample variance is

$$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{4}(r_i - \bar{r})^2 \approx 0.000894.$$

The annualized volatility estimate is therefore $\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{0.000894 \times 252} \approx 0.474$, or roughly $47\%$ per year.

**Key Theorem (Itô's Lemma applied to GBM).** Under the Black–Scholes model, the asset price satisfies the stochastic differential equation $dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t$, where $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion. By Itô's Lemma, the log-price $X_t = \ln S_t$ follows

$$dX_t = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)dt + \sigma\,dW_t,$$

confirming that $\sigma$ is precisely the standard deviation of log-returns per unit time. The $\sigma^2/2$ correction (the *Itô drift adjustment*) distinguishes arithmetic from geometric growth and is essential in option pricing.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp
import math

prices = [100, 102, 99, 103, 101]
log_returns = [math.log(prices[i+1]/prices[i]) for i in range(len(prices)-1)]

n = len(log_returns)
mean_r = sum(log_returns) / n
variance = sum((r - mean_r)**2 for r in log_returns) / (n - 1)
sigma_annual = math.sqrt(variance * 252)

print(f"Log-returns : {[round(r,4) for r in log_returns]}")
print(f"Sample variance : {variance:.6f}")
print(f"Annualized volatility: {sigma_annual:.4f}")
```

### stationary (0.0s)

## Stationary

**Definition.** Let $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ be a differentiable function on an open interval $I$. A point $x_0 \in I$ is called a *stationary point* of $f$ if the derivative vanishes there:

$$f'(x_0) = 0.$$

Geometrically, the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $(x_0, f(x_0))$ is horizontal. The value $f(x_0)$ is called a *stationary value*. Stationary points are the candidates for local extrema, but not every stationary point is an extremum — a point may be stationary and yet be neither a local maximum nor a local minimum.

**Worked Example.** Consider $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$ on $\mathbb{R}$. Differentiating gives

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1).$$

Setting $f'(x) = 0$ yields stationary points at $x = -1$ and $x = 1$. The stationary values are $f(-1) = 3$ and $f(1) = -1$. To classify these points, compute the second derivative $f''(x) = 6x$. Since $f''(-1) = -6 < 0$, the point $x = -1$ is a local maximum; since $f''(1) = 6 > 0$, the point $x = 1$ is a local minimum.

**Key Theorem (Second Derivative Test).** Let $f$ be twice differentiable on $I$ and suppose $f'(x_0) = 0$. Then:

1. If $f''(x_0) > 0$, then $x_0$ is a strict local minimum.
2. If $f''(x_0) < 0$, then $x_0$ is a strict local maximum.
3. If $f''(x_0) = 0$, the test is inconclusive.

*Proof sketch.* When $f'(x_0) = 0$ and $f''(x_0) > 0$, the Taylor expansion $f(x_0 + h) \approx f(x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(x_0)h^2$ shows $f(x_0 + h) > f(x_0)$ for all sufficiently small $h \neq 0$, confirming a local minimum. The maximum case is symmetric. $\square$

Note that case (3) requires further analysis; the function $g(x) = x^3$ satisfies $g'(0) = g''(0) = 0$ yet $x = 0$ is a point of inflection, not an extremum.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, latex

x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x + 1

f_prime  = diff(f, x)
f_double = diff(f_prime, x)

stationary_pts = solve(f_prime, x)
for pt in stationary_pts:
    val        = f.subs(x, pt)
    curvature  = f_double.subs(x, pt)
    kind       = "min" if curvature > 0 else "max" if curvature < 0 else "inconclusive"
    print(f"x = {pt}: f = {val}, f'' = {curvature}  →  {kind}")
```

Running this cell confirms $x = -1$ is a local maximum and $x = 1$ is a local minimum, matching the hand calculation above.

### augmented_dickey_fuller_test (0.0s)

## Augmented Dickey–Fuller Test

**Definition.** The **Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test** is a formal hypothesis test for the presence of a unit root in a time series $\{y_t\}$. Building on the simple autoregressive model $y_t = \rho y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, the test reformulates the unit-root question through the augmented regression

$$\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \delta_j \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$ is the first difference, $\alpha$ is a constant (drift), $\beta t$ is an optional deterministic trend, and the lagged difference terms $\Delta y_{t-j}$ absorb serial correlation in the residuals. The null hypothesis is $H_0\colon \gamma = 0$ (unit root present), tested against $H_1\colon \gamma < 0$ (the series is stationary). The augmentation by $p$ lagged differences distinguishes the ADF test from the original Dickey–Fuller test, which assumed uncorrelated errors.

**Worked Example.** Suppose quarterly GDP data over 40 periods appears to trend upward without reverting to a mean. We fit the augmented regression with $p = 2$ lags, a constant, and a trend. The OLS estimate yields $\hat{\gamma} = -0.12$ with test statistic $\tau = \hat{\gamma}/\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\gamma}) = -2.31$. Consulting the Dickey–Fuller critical values (not standard $t$-tables, because the distribution is non-normal under $H_0$), the 5% critical value with trend is approximately $-3.45$. Since $-2.31 > -3.45$, we fail to reject $H_0$ and conclude the series likely contains a unit root, suggesting first-differencing is needed before modelling.

**Key Theorem.** Under $H_0\colon \gamma = 0$, the ADF $\tau$-statistic converges in distribution to a functional of standard Brownian motion $W(\cdot)$:

$$\tau \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\int_0^1 W(r)\,dW(r)}{\left(\int_0^1 W(r)^2\,dr\right)^{1/2}}.$$

Because this limit distribution is non-normal, critical values must be obtained from simulation-based tables (MacKinnon, 1994) rather than from the $t$-distribution.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

# Simulate a random walk (unit root process)
np.random.seed(42)
y = np.cumsum(np.random.randn(200))

result = adfuller(y, maxlag=4, regression='ct', autolag='AIC')
print(f"ADF statistic : {result[0]:.4f}")
print(f"p-value       : {result[1]:.4f}")
print(f"Critical values: {result[4]}")
# Expected: fail to reject H0 — p-value > 0.05
```

The lag order $p$ is selected automatically by minimising the AIC, balancing model fit against overfitting.
