## Suffix

A suffix is a bound morpheme attached to the right edge of a stem that determines the lexical category — noun, verb, adjective, adverb — of the resulting word, and often adds a systematic layer of meaning tied to that category. In English morphology, suffixation is the primary mechanism of derivation: it is the engine that converts a verb like *act* into a noun (*action*), an agent noun (*actor*), or an adjective (*actionable*), each transformation governed by a suffix that carries its own selectional restrictions on what kind of stem it will attach to and what category it produces.

**Mechanism.** A derivational suffix operates as a category-changing (or category-preserving, in some cases) function applied to a base:

$$
\underbrace{act}_{\text{verb stem}} + \underbrace{\text{-ion}}_{\text{nominalizer}} = \text{action} \quad(\text{noun: "the act of"})
$$

$$
\underbrace{act}_{\text{verb stem}} + \underbrace{\text{-or}}_{\text{agentive}} = \text{actor} \quad(\text{noun: "the doer"})
$$

$$
\underbrace{act}_{\text{verb stem}} + \underbrace{\text{-able}}_{\text{potential/capability}} = \text{actionable} \quad(\text{adjective, via the derived noun})
$$

Each suffix imposes phonological and morphological constraints on the base it accepts — *-ion* typically attaches to Latinate verb stems (*create → creation*, not *\*singion*), *-able* prefers transitive verbs (*readable*, *breakable*), and *-or* competes with the near-synonymous *-er* along lines partly historical (Latinate vs. Germanic stems) and partly lexicalized on a word-by-word basis (*sailor* vs. *worker*).

**Literature context.** Generative morphology (Aronoff 1976; Marchand 1969) treats suffixation as rule-governed word formation, but the rules are notoriously leaky: productivity is gradient rather than categorical. Baayen and Lieber's corpus-based work on morphological productivity quantifies this by measuring how often a suffix generates novel (hapax legomenon) forms in large corpora, showing that *-ness* is far more productive than *-ity* despite both nominalizing adjectives. What remains open is why certain suffixes stall in productivity — blocked by an existing irregular form (*\*gloriosity* blocked by *glory*), by phonological mismatch, or by semantic drift that makes the derived form unpredictable from its parts (*dresser* as furniture, not "one who dresses").

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could measure the productivity of two or three suffixes (e.g., *-ness* vs. *-ity*, or *-able* vs. *-ible*) across a diachronic corpus (COHA or Google Ngrams), tracking hapax-based productivity scores by decade, and cross-referencing against etymological source (native vs. Latinate stems) to test whether stem origin predicts suffix choice and productivity trend over time.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the productivity measure used and why (type/token ratios are insufficient; hapax-conditioned measures are standard since Baayen), report results per suffix per period with confidence intervals appropriate to corpus sampling, and explicitly address any blocking effects observed (cases where a productive suffix failed to apply because a lexicalized competitor already occupied that semantic slot) — since blocking, not just phonological fit, is often the deciding factor in whether a suffix attaches.

## -Or

**Definition.** *-Or* is a derivational suffix in English that attaches to a verb stem to produce a noun denoting the agent — the entity that performs the action named by the verb. It descends from Latin agentive *-or/-ōris* (as in *auctor*, "originator"), reaching English through Anglo-Norman and Latinate borrowing, and later through direct suffixation to native and Latinate stems alike. Its productivity places it alongside *-er* in the small class of English agent-noun suffixes, with the two competing for the same semantic slot rather than dividing it cleanly.

**Composition.**

$$\underbrace{act}_{\text{verb: to perform, do}} + \underbrace{\text{-or}}_{\text{agent suffix}} = \text{actor}$$

**Mechanism.** The suffix attaches to a verb (frequently one of Latinate origin, often ending in *-ate*, *-ct*, or *-ess*) and shifts both category and meaning: the resulting noun denotes "one who [verb]s" or, for inanimate referents, "that which [verb]s" (*elevator*, *reactor*). Distribution is only partly rule-governed. *-Or* tends to surface after stems ending in *-t* or *-s* (*direct → director*, *compress → compressor*), while *-er* is the default elsewhere (*teach → teacher*), but the split is riddled with exceptions (*advisor/adviser*, *editor* not *\*editer*) that resist a clean phonological or morphological rule and instead reflect the borrowing history of each word — whether it entered English already bearing a Latin *-or* form or was suffixed natively after the fact.

**Literature context.** Historical linguists (Marchand 1969; Bauer 1983) treat *-or* as a case study in suffix rivalry: two formatives with near-identical semantics coexisting because one was inherited through borrowing rather than assigned by a living synchronic rule. What remains open is how much of the *-or/-er* split synchronic speakers actually track as a rule versus what they simply memorize word-by-word, and whether novel coinages (nonce agent nouns for new verbs, e.g. tech-industry neologisms like *disruptor*) default to *-or* because of prestige/Latinate association or to *-er* because it is the more productive native pattern. Corpus work on neologism formation has not settled this.

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could compile a corpus of agent nouns coined in the last three decades — drawn from technology journalism, patent filings, and social media — and code each for the suffix chosen (*-or* vs. *-er* vs. *-ist*), the phonological shape of the stem, and the register of the source verb (Latinate/formal vs. Germanic/informal). A companion elicitation experiment could present speakers with novel verbs (some clearly Latinate-sounding, some not) and ask them to produce an agent noun, testing whether stem etymology alone predicts suffix choice or whether analogy to a specific known exemplar (*-actor*, *-visor*) is doing the work.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should report suffix distribution as proportions conditioned on stem-final phoneme and etymological class, present the elicitation results as a contingency table with observed patterns rather than a fitted statistical model (this is a descriptive linguistics task, not a hypothesis test), and explicitly flag any items where informants disagreed or hedged — since speaker uncertainty on marginal cases (*advisor/adviser*) is itself the finding, not noise to be averaged away.
