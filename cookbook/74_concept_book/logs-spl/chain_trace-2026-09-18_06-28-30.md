### 一 (6.7s)

## 一

Picture a single grain of rice sitting alone on a table. Just one. Before you can count two grains, or ten, or a hundred, you need this: the idea of "one whole thing." That's what the character 一 captures — a single flat stroke, drawn left to right, like a tiny table itself.

Why does one line feel right for "one"? Because one thing needs nothing extra to show it — no curves, no crossing strokes, just a single mark standing for a single, complete, whole unit. Two lines would be two things. Three lines, three things. But one thing is the simplest idea there is, so it gets the simplest possible symbol.

In math, we call this the *unit* — the building block everything else is counted from. Every number starts by asking: "how many ones do I have?" $1$ is the beginning of counting itself.

Now you try: draw one straight line on paper. That's 一. Now draw a second line right below it — can you guess what number that might become?

### 丿 (7.4s)

## 丿

Try drawing a straight line from the top down, but let it lean — starting high on the right and sliding down to the left, like a stick sliding off a table. That's it: that's the entire stroke. It doesn't turn, it doesn't hook, it doesn't stop and start again. One smooth downward slide, tilted left.

Why does a whole character-building block get to be just one lean? Because Chinese writing is built the same way LEGO towers are built: from a small set of basic pieces, repeated and combined. $丿$ is one of those pieces — the "falling-left" piece. It shows up inside bigger characters like 人 (person) or 八 (eight), always doing the same job: a single confident diagonal stroke from upper-right to lower-left.

Formally, dictionaries group characters by their "radical" — a recurring shape used to file and look them up. $丿$ is radical #4 out of 214, nicknamed 撇 (piě).

Now you try: pick a pencil, start at head height on the right, and slide down-left in one breath. No lifting, no wobble — just the lean.

### 木 (0.0s)

## 木

Stand in a park and look at a tree. A straight trunk rising up, with branches spreading out to the sides — that simple shape is exactly what the character 木 draws. One line down for the trunk, a line across for the branches, and two little strokes at the bottom like roots digging into the ground.

Here's the fun part: 木 isn't just a picture. It's also a building block. Put two trees side by side and you get 林 — a grove, a small forest! Put a person resting against a tree and you get 休 — to rest, because who doesn't want to lean on a tree and relax?

But sometimes 木 isn't about trees at all — it just lends its *sound*. In 沐 (to bathe), the water radical 氵 gives the meaning, while 木 simply says "this sounds like *mù*." Same brick, different job each time!

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = 林 \; (\text{grove})$$

**Now you try:** Can you find another character with 木 hiding inside it? What job is it doing there — meaning, or sound?

### 朱 (12.8s)

## 朱

Imagine you find a bright red stone lying beside a tree stump — the kind of red that jumps out at you from the forest floor, brighter than blood, brighter than a sunset. Ancient people saw this red so often near cut wood that they built a character to remember it: 朱, vermilion.

Look at its shape: 木 (a tree), with a mark drawn right across the trunk — a cut, a scar, a slice through the wood. Why does this feel right? Because the deepest, most brilliant red dye the earliest people found came from the heartwood inside certain trees — the color hidden beneath the bark, revealed only when the trunk was cut open.

So 朱 is not just "the color red." It is redness discovered by cutting into something — color as a secret found inside wood.

Now you try: draw 木, then slice a mark straight through its trunk. That cut is 朱 — the red the tree kept hidden.

### 株 (7.8s)

## 株

Picture a great oak tree, storm-felled, cut down to size — but the stump stays rooted in the ground, solid and unmoving. That's the heart of 株 (zhū): the trunk, the stump, the base of a plant that doesn't walk away.

Why does this feel right? Because a stump is something you can *count* — one stump, two stumps, a hundred saplings planted in neat rows. That's why 株 became the measure word for trees and plants themselves, the way "head" counts cattle. It also names a lineage — a "strain" in biology is really a family tree growing from one stump, generation after generation branching out but sharing the same root. And since a stump is fixed, unable to run, 株 stretches metaphorically to people "tied down" together in a shady scheme — implicated, rooted to the same spot, unable to walk away from what they're involved in.

Notice how 株 is built from 朱 (vermilion, cinnabar-red) sitting under the tree radical 木 — a tree whose base is marked, like a trunk you'd paint red to count it.

Now you try: name three things in your life that are like a "stump" — fixed, countable, and the root that everything else grows from.
