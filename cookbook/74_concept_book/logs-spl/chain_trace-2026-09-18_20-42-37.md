### perceptron_1958 (0.0s)

## Perceptron 1958

Picture a baby learning to recognize cats. At first it guesses randomly — sometimes right, sometimes wrong. But each time someone says "that's a cat!" or "nope, not a cat," the baby's brain quietly adjusts. Over many tries, it gets better. Frank Rosenblatt built a machine that learns the same way.

His Perceptron is a single artificial neuron. It looks at inputs — say, pixel brightness values — and multiplies each by a "weight" measuring how much that input matters. It adds these up and fires a signal only if the total crosses a threshold, just like a real neuron firing when enough stimulation arrives.

Formally, the Perceptron outputs a decision $\hat{y}$ from inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with weights $w_1, \dots, w_n$:

$$\hat{y} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i + b \geq 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

When it's wrong, Rosenblatt's learning rule nudges the weights: $w_i \leftarrow w_i + \eta (y - \hat{y}) x_i$, where $\eta$ is a small learning rate. Wrong guesses shrink; right ones grow — the machine learns from its mistakes.

**Now you try:** Imagine weighing "sunny" and "warm" to decide "go to the park?" Adjust your own weights after a few wrong guesses — that's a Perceptron!

### ai_winter_1974 (0.0s)

## Ai Winter 1974

Imagine a bright inventor who builds a machine that can sort red balls from blue balls — perfect every time. People cheer, expecting it to soon sort *any* pattern. Then two mathematicians, Minsky and Papert, ask a harder question: can it tell whether balls are arranged in a connected clump or scattered apart? The machine fails, completely.

That's what happened to the perceptron. It worked beautifully on simple, straight-line divisions — but Minsky and Papert proved it could never solve problems like XOR, where the answer twists back on itself. No amount of training would fix this; it was a wall built into the machine's very shape, not a bug to patch.

The excitement popped like a balloon. Funders pulled their money, universities lost interest, and for over a decade the dream of thinking machines went quiet — a chilly stretch researchers later nicknamed the "AI winter."

**Now you try:** Can you think of a rule a straight line could *never* separate — like "inside a circle" versus "outside"?

### expert_systems_1980 (0.0s)

## Expert Systems 1980

Picture a wise doctor who never sleeps, never forgets, and follows a giant flowchart of "if-then" rules to diagnose illness. That's the dream that pulled AI out of its winter slump: instead of teaching machines to learn like a baby, teach them to *reason* like an expert, one rule at a time.

The intuition is simple: doctors already think in patterns — "if fever and stiff neck, then suspect meningitis." Programs like MYCIN captured thousands of these if-then rules from real specialists. XCON did the same for configuring computers. No learning required, no mystery — just a chain of logical steps a human could read and check, formally written as $\text{IF } P_1 \land P_2 \land \cdots \land P_n \text{ THEN } Q$, where each rule fires when its conditions match facts in a growing knowledge base, triggering further rules in a chain called *forward chaining*.

It worked — for narrow tasks. But rules multiplied fast: a few hundred grew to thousands, and every new rule risked breaking old ones.

**Now you try:** Write three if-then rules for deciding what to wear based on weather. Notice how quickly a fourth rule could conflict with your first!

### backprop_1986 (0.0s)

## Backprop 1986

Imagine you're playing a game where you toss a ball trying to hit a target, but you're blindfolded. A friend only tells you "too far left" or "too far right" after each throw. You'd adjust your aim a little each time, getting closer and closer. That's exactly the problem that stumped scientists building "thinking" networks made of many layers of artificial neurons: how do you tell each layer how to adjust when only the final answer is wrong?

In 1986, Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams showed how to pass that "you're off" signal backward through every layer, so each one learns its share of the blame and nudges its own dials.

Formally, if $E$ is the error and $w_{ij}$ a connection weight, the chain rule gives

$$\frac{\partial E}{\partial w_{ij}} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial a_j}\cdot\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial w_{ij}}$$

propagated layer by layer from output to input.

**Now you try:** Think of a game of "telephone" in reverse — instead of passing a message forward and losing it, you pass a *correction* backward until everyone knows how to say it right.

### deep_blue_1997 (0.0s)

## Deep Blue 1997

Picture this: a boy loses every checkers game to his grandfather, but writes down each loss. By his hundredth game, he's not smarter — he's just seen more. That's Deep Blue's secret. It didn't understand chess beauty; it looked ahead, brutally and fast.

Every chess position is a fork in a road, and each road forks again. Deep Blue explored this branching tree of possible moves — millions of positions per second — and picked the branch leading to the best future, assuming both players play their best. This is called **minimax search**: at each turn, you maximize your advantage while assuming your opponent minimizes it.

$$\text{score}(position) = \max_{\text{your move}} \min_{\text{opponent's move}} \dots \text{score}(\text{future position})$$

No learning, no intuition — just searching deeper than any human could hold in their head. In 1997, that depth beat World Champion Garry Kasparov.

**Now you try:** Play tic-tac-toe and count every possible next move before deciding. That counting *is* the search tree.

### imagenet_2012 (0.0s)

## Imagenet 2012

Picture a contest where computers had to look at a photo and guess what's in it — a dog, a bicycle, a mushroom. For years, machines were terrible at this, mixing up cats and toasters. Then in 2012, a team built a "seeing machine" called AlexNet, and it crushed the competition, guessing right far more often than anything before it.

What changed? Three things came together at once. The machine had *millions* of labeled example photos to learn from — like a kid who's seen a million dogs, not ten. It used special graphics chips (originally built for video games!) to think fast, trying billions of tiny adjustments. And it stacked many layers of pattern-detectors, each layer learning something a little more complex than the last: edges, then shapes, then whole objects.

The result was called a deep convolutional network — "deep" because of all those stacked layers. This single contest win convinced the world that deep learning wasn't just a curiosity — it worked, and it worked *big*.

**Now you try:** Look around your room. Could you describe an object using only simple building blocks — first edges, then shapes, then the whole thing?

### alphago_2016 (8.4s)

## Alphago 2016

Picture a game of Go: a grid of 361 points, more possible games than atoms in the universe. Chess-playing computers had won by checking ahead move by move, but Go had too many branches to search that way — everyone assumed only human intuition, a "feel" for the board, could handle it.

AlphaGo's trick: pair two networks. One, trained on millions of human games (building on the same idea from $[[imagenet\_2012]]$ — learn patterns from huge labeled data), guesses good moves like a hunch. The other estimates who's winning from any position, like sizing up a room at a glance. Together they prune the search: instead of checking everything, AlphaGo only follows its best hunches deeply — a smarter search guided by learned intuition, not raw computation.

In March 2016, it beat world champion Lee Sedol 4 games to 1.

**Now you try:** Next time you play tic-tac-toe, notice: do you check every move, or do some moves just "look right" first?
