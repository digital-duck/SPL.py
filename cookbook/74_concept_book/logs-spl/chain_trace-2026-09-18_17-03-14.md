## Observation

Observation is the deliberate act of noticing, measuring, and recording phenomena in the natural world. It is the foundational empirical act of science: before a hypothesis can be formed, tested, or discarded, something must first be seen, counted, timed, or otherwise registered as data. What separates scientific observation from casual noticing is systematicity — the observer specifies in advance what will be measured, how, and under what conditions, so that the record can be checked, repeated, and compared against other records.

Consider a simple worked example. A biology student wants to know whether a species of moth is more active at dusk than at midday. She sets up a fixed observation protocol: count moths landing on a lit trap every 15 minutes, for two hours at dusk and two hours at midday, on five separate days. The resulting counts are raw observational data — no interpretation yet, just numbers tied to a time and a condition. Only after this data exists can she ask whether the pattern is real or due to chance, which is a separate act of inference (statistics, later in this course) built on top of observation.

The problem-solving skill worth practicing here is designing an observation protocol that avoids two common failure modes: selection bias, where the method of observing systematically favors certain outcomes over others (e.g., only checking the trap when moths happen to be visible), and confounding, where an uncontrolled variable changes alongside the one being studied (e.g., dusk observations happening on cooler, calmer evenings than midday ones, so "time of day" and "temperature" are tangled together). A well-designed observation protocol fixes as many conditions as possible — same trap, same light, same recorder, same weather range — so that when a pattern shows up in the data, it can plausibly be attributed to the variable of interest rather than to how the data was collected.

Try this: suppose you are asked to observe whether traffic noise affects how long birds sing near a city park. Before recording a single measurement, write down exactly what you will count, how often, at which locations, and what you will hold constant. That written protocol — not the data itself — is the real test of whether your observation will support a meaningful conclusion later.

## Scientific Method

The scientific method is the disciplined cycle by which empirical knowledge is built and revised: it starts from an **observation** — a noticed regularity or anomaly in the world — and moves through question, hypothesis, prediction, experiment, analysis, and conclusion, with the loop repeating as new observations emerge. Its defining feature is not any single step but the requirement that a hypothesis be *falsifiable*: it must generate a specific, testable prediction that an experiment could, in principle, refute.

**Worked example.** Suppose you observe that a species of moth in a polluted industrial region is darker on average than the same species in unpolluted areas (observation). This prompts a question: does soot-darkened bark make dark moths harder for birds to spot, giving them a survival advantage? The hypothesis — "moth coloration shifts toward the shade that best matches local bark, driven by predation" — yields a testable prediction: if you pin equal numbers of light and dark moths to sooty tree trunks and to clean trunks, predation rates should be higher for the mismatched color in each setting. Running that experiment and counting survivors is the test; comparing survival rates statistically is the analysis; and concluding that camouflage-driven predation explains the color shift (or does not, if the data show no difference) completes the cycle.

**Problem-solving application.** The method's power lies in ruling out rival explanations through controlled comparison. If dark moths simply survived better everywhere — clean bark included — camouflage would be the wrong explanation, and you'd need a new hypothesis (perhaps dark pigmentation confers a metabolic advantage, unrelated to bark color). This is why an experiment needs a control group and a manipulated variable held constant except for the one factor being tested: without that isolation, a result cannot distinguish between competing causal stories. When you design your own investigation — in a lab, in data science, or in evaluating a claim in the news — the checklist is the same: state a hypothesis specific enough to fail, identify what evidence would falsify it, control for confounding variables, and let the data revise your belief rather than confirm it. A hypothesis that survives repeated, independent attempts to refute it is provisionally accepted, never proven with certainty — the method is a mechanism for approaching truth by systematically eliminating error, not a guarantee of it.
