## France Liberation 1945

By 1945, France had regained its sovereignty but not its footing. Four years of occupation, forced requisitions, and Allied bombing had wrecked its rail network, ports, and industrial base; national income stood at roughly half its 1938 level, and food and coal shortages persisted well past VE Day. The political vacuum left by the collapse of the Vichy regime was filled provisionally by Charles de Gaulle's Government of National Unity, but the deeper question — what kind of republic should replace the discredited Third Republic — remained unsettled. In October 1945, French voters elected a Constituent Assembly and, in the same referendum, decisively rejected returning to the pre-war constitutional order, mandating a rewritten frame of government that would become the Fourth Republic in 1946.

This moment illustrates a recurring pattern in postwar reconstruction: material collapse and constitutional redesign happen simultaneously, and each constrains the other. Rebuilding steel mills and rail lines required a functioning, legitimate state to coordinate investment and labor; but designing that state's institutions required political factions — Gaullists, Communists, and Socialists prominent among them — to negotiate while the economy they depended on for leverage and legitimacy was still in ruins. France's empire in Indochina and West/North Africa complicated this further: colonial resources and manpower were treated by Paris as assets essential to national recovery, even as nationalist movements in those territories increasingly viewed 1945 as their own moment for independence rather than continued subordination to a rebuilding metropole.

A useful way to analyze this kind of situation is to separate it into three linked problems that any liberated or post-conflict state faces: (1) restoring physical and economic capacity, (2) establishing a legitimate political order, and (3) resolving the status of dependent or contested territories. Applying this framework to France in 1945: the Monnet Plan (from 1946) addressed problem (1) through centralized investment in coal, steel, and electricity; the 1946 constitutional referendum addressed problem (2), though it produced a Fourth Republic criticized for chronic cabinet instability; and problem (3) was left largely unresolved, setting up the First Indochina War (from 1946) and, later, the Algerian War. Recognizing that these three problems were tackled unevenly — economic and constitutional questions prioritized over colonial ones — helps explain why French decolonization became so protracted and violent compared with, say, British withdrawal from India in the same period.

### uk_postwar_settlement_1945 (0.0s)

## Uk Postwar Settlement 1945

Britain emerged from World War II victorious but financially devastated. Six years of total war had drained the treasury, destroyed export industries, and left the country dependent on American loans just to keep importing food and fuel. In July 1945, in the general election held immediately after Germany's surrender, voters delivered a landslide to the Labour Party under Clement Attlee, rejecting Winston Churchill despite his wartime leadership. The "postwar settlement" refers to the broad political consensus this government built: a mixed economy combining private industry with state-owned utilities, a system of social insurance covering unemployment and old age, and — most enduringly — the National Health Service (NHS), launched in 1948, which provided medical care free at the point of use, funded through general taxation rather than individual payment or insurance premiums.

Consider the logic behind this shift. The 1942 Beveridge Report, commissioned during the war, had identified five social problems it called the "Giant Evils": want, disease, ignorance, squalor, and idleness. Attlee's government treated these as interconnected failures that private markets alone would not fix, and responded with matching institutions — social security against want, the NHS against disease, expanded education against ignorance, public housing against squalor, and a commitment to full employment against idleness. This was not socialism in the sense of abolishing private property; it was a settlement, a negotiated boundary between state provision and market activity that both major parties would largely accept for the next three decades.

The paradox worth examining is that this expansion of domestic welfare occurred while Britain still governed the largest colonial empire in the world, spanning territories from India to large parts of Africa. Resources and personnel were stretched between rebuilding at home and administering an empire already showing signs of strain — India would gain independence in 1947, just two years after the settlement began. A useful analytical exercise is to compare state capacity across these two projects: ask what fraction of Britain's shrinking postwar budget and administrative manpower went to funding domestic programs like the NHS versus maintaining colonial governance and military garrisons abroad, and consider how that allocation problem helped accelerate decolonization even as the welfare state consolidated at home.

### cold_war_bipolar_order (0.0s)

## Cold War Bipolar Order

**Definition.** After 1945, the international system collapsed into two dominant power centers rather than the multipolar arrangement of great powers that had characterized Europe since Westphalia. The United States led a bloc of market democracies (NATO, Japan, later many decolonizing states) while the Soviet Union led a bloc of communist and allied regimes (the Warsaw Pact, China until the Sino-Soviet split, various client states across Africa, Asia, and Latin America). Bipolarity means power concentrates in two poles rather than being distributed among many roughly equal actors — a structural condition, not just a rivalry. This mattered because each bloc's security depended on preventing the other's expansion, producing competition that extended into every region and domain: military alliances, proxy wars, ideology, economic aid, space exploration, and nuclear arms races.

**Worked example.** Consider the Berlin Blockade of 1948–49. The USSR cut off land access to West Berlin, an enclave of Western control deep inside Soviet-occupied East Germany. Rather than fighting directly — which risked nuclear escalation — the US and allies airlifted supplies for eleven months until the Soviets relented. This episode illustrates the defining logic of bipolarity: direct war between the poles was too costly given nuclear weapons, so confrontation shifted to indirect tests of resolve, economic pressure, and symbolic contests over contested territory. The same logic recurs in the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962), the Korean War (1950–53), and the Vietnam War (1955–75) — each a "hot" conflict fought through allies or proxies while the superpowers avoided direct combat.

**Problem-solving application.** When analyzing a Cold War-era conflict, ask three structural questions: (1) Which pole's security interests were threatened? (2) Did the conflict stay proxy/indirect, or did it risk direct superpower confrontation — and why? (3) How did nuclear deterrence shape the available options? Apply this to the Cuban Missile Crisis: Soviet missiles in Cuba threatened US security directly (question 1); Kennedy chose a naval blockade rather than invasion specifically to avoid direct war (question 2); both sides understood that miscalculation could trigger nuclear exchange, which is why back-channel diplomacy resolved it (question 3). This three-question framework generalizes to any bipolar-era crisis and helps distinguish bipolarity from multipolar systems, where shifting coalitions — rather than two fixed blocs — determine outcomes.

### france_indochina_defeat (0.0s)

## France Indochina Defeat

After liberation in 1945, France sought to restore its prewar empire, including Indochina—modern Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia. Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh, however, had already declared Vietnamese independence, setting the stage for the First Indochina War, which began in earnest in 1946. This conflict became a defining case study in twentieth-century decolonization: a European power with conventional military superiority steadily lost a war against a nationalist, communist-backed insurgency fighting on its own terrain.

For eight years, France poured troops and resources into a war it could not decisively win. The Viet Minh, led militarily by General Vo Nguyen Giap, avoided set-piece battles where French firepower dominated, instead building support in the countryside and receiving growing supplies of Chinese arms after the 1949 communist victory in China. The war's turning point came at Dien Bien Phu in 1954. France established a fortified base in a remote valley, intending to lure Viet Minh forces into open battle. Giap instead maneuvered heavy artillery onto the surrounding hills—terrain the French had judged inaccessible—and besieged the garrison for 57 days. The French position fell in May 1954, a catastrophic and symbolically devastating defeat.

The battle's outcome directly forced diplomacy: the Geneva Conference, already underway, produced the Geneva Accords of 1954, under which France withdrew and Vietnam was temporarily partitioned at the 17th parallel pending elections that were never held.

For problem-solving purposes, treat Dien Bien Phu as a case study in the mismatch between conventional and asymmetric warfare. Ask: why did superior technology and firepower fail to produce victory? The answer lies in logistics and terrain—France resupplied its garrison only by air, and once artillery closed the airstrip, the base was strategically doomed regardless of its defenders' bravery. This pattern—a militarily stronger power undone by an opponent's control of terrain, supply lines, and political endurance—recurs across decolonization conflicts. When analyzing similar cases (such as the later U.S. war in Vietnam), evaluate not just troop strength but each side's logistical resilience, political will to sustain losses, and this dependency on unbroken supply lines.

### germany_occupation_1945 (12.1s)

## Germany Occupation 1945

**Definition.** With Germany's unconditional surrender in May 1945, the four major Allied powers—the United States, the Soviet Union, Britain, and France—divided the country into four occupation zones, each administered by one power under the coordinating authority of the Allied Control Council. Berlin, though deep inside the Soviet zone, was itself split into four sectors. No peace treaty settled Germany's borders or sovereignty; occupation was meant as a transitional arrangement while the Allies decided the country's political future. That decision never came as a single agreement, because the wartime coalition was already splitting into two rival blocs.

**Worked example.** Consider the practical mechanics of occupation policy. At the Potsdam Conference (July–August 1945), the Allies agreed on the "Four D's": demilitarization, denazification, democratization, and decartelization. In principle these applied uniformly across zones. In practice, each occupying power interpreted and enforced them differently. The Soviets rapidly nationalized industry and suppressed political pluralism in their zone; the Americans, British, and French moved toward reconstructing market economies and multi-party local government in theirs. By 1947, the Western zones were merging administratively (first the American-British "Bizone," then Trizone with France), while the Soviet zone developed a separate, centrally planned economic and political system. This divergence in implementation—not any single treaty—is what actually split Germany.

**Problem-solving application.** When analyzing why occupation produced two Germanies rather than one, avoid treating it as an inevitable outcome of the 1945 agreements themselves; the agreements were deliberately vague about Germany's ultimate status precisely because the Allies disagreed. The analytical task is to trace how each zone's occupying power's domestic system and strategic interests shaped local policy choices—currency, land reform, media control, permitted political parties—and how those choices accumulated into incompatible economic and political structures. A useful exercise: given a specific 1945–1949 policy divergence (e.g., the 1948 currency reform introducing the Deutsche Mark in the West), work backward to identify which occupying power's interests it served and what Soviet countermeasure it triggered (the Berlin Blockade). This method—linking a discrete policy decision to the occupying power's strategic logic and its downstream reaction—is the core analytical skill for understanding how occupation hardened into division rather than resolving Germany's future.

### uk_decolonization_wave (0.0s)

## Uk Decolonization Wave

Decolonization refers to the process by which a colonial power formally transfers sovereignty to a colonized territory, ending direct political control. For Britain, this was not a single event but a two-decade cascade: India's independence in 1947, followed through the 1950s and 1960s by the departure from most colonies in Africa (Ghana in 1957, Nigeria in 1960, Kenya in 1963, and many others) and the Caribbean (Jamaica and Trinidad and Tobago in 1962, among others). By the early 1970s, an empire that had spanned roughly a quarter of the globe's land area had shrunk to a scattering of small dependencies.

**Worked example.** Consider India in 1947. The subcontinent had been under direct British Crown rule since 1858, following a century of East India Company dominance. Two forces converged to end this: first, a sustained nationalist movement, led by figures like Mohandas Gandhi and Jawaharlal Nehru, that had built mass political legitimacy through decades of civil disobedience; second, Britain's postwar exhaustion, documented in [[uk_postwar_settlement_1945]] — a war-depleted treasury, war-weary public, and a new Labour government less committed to imperial maintenance than its predecessors. The result was rapid: independence arrived in August 1947, accompanied by partition into India and Pakistan, a division that triggered mass displacement and communal violence on a scale that illustrates how decolonization's *timing* (rushed, under external and internal pressure) shaped its *human cost*.

**Problem-solving application.** When analyzing any decolonization case, ask three linked questions rather than treating "independence" as a single outcome: (1) What was the colonizer's capacity to hold on — military, financial, political will? (2) What was the colonized population's organized capacity to demand exit — parties, leaders, international allies? (3) What institutional handoff occurred — did the colonizer leave functioning administrative and legal structures, or a vacuum? Applying this framework across the British case explains the variation you'll see: India's negotiated but rushed transfer produced partition violence; Ghana's relatively orderly 1957 transition (guided by Kwame Nkrumah's disciplined party organizing) became a model for other African independence movements; Kenya's 1963 independence followed a more violent suppression of the Mau Mau uprising, leaving deeper scars in the settlement. The same causal engine — imperial overreach meeting rising nationalist capacity — produced different outcomes depending on local conditions, a pattern worth testing against any decolonization case you encounter.

### france_algerian_war (0.0s)

## France Algerian War

When the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu (1954) forced Paris to abandon Indochina, it exposed the fragility of the entire French colonial project — and within months, nationalist insurgents in Algeria launched their own armed uprising, convinced that France's imperial resolve was broken. But Algeria was not Indochina in French eyes. Officially, Algeria was not a colony at all but an integral part of France itself, divided into departments and home to over a million European settlers (the *pieds-noirs*) who had lived there for generations. This distinction made the war fundamentally different in character and stakes: losing Algeria meant losing what many French citizens considered French soil.

The war that followed, 1954–1962, combined guerrilla insurgency by the National Liberation Front (FLN), brutal counterinsurgency tactics by the French army — including widespread use of torture, later a subject of national reckoning — and terrorism on both sides that spilled into metropolitan France itself. The conflict grew so divisive that it toppled the French Fourth Republic in 1958: fearing the government would negotiate away Algeria, French army units and settlers in Algiers revolted, and Charles de Gaulle was recalled to power to prevent a military coup or civil war on the mainland. De Gaulle, initially trusted by pro-French Algeria advocates to keep Algeria French, gradually concluded that continued war was untenable and moved toward negotiated independence — a reversal that provoked an attempted army putsch in 1961 and a campaign of settler terrorism by the OAS (Secret Army Organization) aimed at assassinating de Gaulle and sabotaging any settlement.

The Evian Accords of March 1962 ended the war and granted Algeria independence. The human cost was severe: hundreds of thousands of Algerians killed, and in the war's final months, over a million *pieds-noirs* and Algerians who had served France (the *harkis*) fled to France, many abandoning everything, in one of the largest forced migrations of the postwar era. The war's legacy reshaped French politics, birthed the Fifth Republic's strong presidency, and left unresolved historical grievances — over torture, massacres, and the harkis' abandonment — that continue to surface in French public debate today.

### germany_division_and_wall (9.5s)

## Germany Division And Wall

The occupation zones drawn in 1945 hardened into permanent borders by 1949. In the west, the American, British, and French zones merged into the Federal Republic of Germany (FRG), a parliamentary democracy tied to NATO and the Marshall Plan economy. In the east, the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic (GDR), a one-party state aligned with Moscow and the Warsaw Pact. Berlin, though sitting entirely inside GDR territory, was itself split into West Berlin (linked administratively to the FRG) and East Berlin (the GDR capital), a geographic anomaly that made the city a pressure point for the entire Cold War.

Through the 1950s, the border between the two Germanys was porous enough that East Germans could simply walk into West Berlin and fly onward to the FRG. This became a slow hemorrhage: by 1961, roughly 3.5 million people — disproportionately young, skilled, and educated — had left East Germany this way, threatening the GDR's economic viability and the legitimacy of its political system. Unable to stop the flow through persuasion or economic improvement, the East German government closed the loophole by force. On the night of August 12–13, 1961, GDR troops sealed the border with barbed wire, which was rapidly rebuilt into a concrete wall, guard towers, and a mined "death strip." The Berlin Wall did not create the division of Germany; it converted an already-existing political and ideological split into a physical barrier designed to stop movement in one direction — out.

To apply this concept, distinguish the *cause* from the *trigger* when analyzing similar events: the division of Germany was caused by the incompatible occupation policies and alliance systems described in Germany's 1945 occupation and the bipolar order of the Cold War, while the Wall itself was a specific policy response to a specific problem (emigration) sixteen years later. A useful exercise is to ask, for any historical border wall or barrier, what problem it was built to solve and for whom — a habit that separates structural causes of conflict from the tactical decisions leaders make in response to them.

### uk_suez_humiliation (11.8s)

## Uk Suez Humiliation

In July 1956, Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal, which had been operated by a British- and French-owned company since the 1860s and remained a vital artery for European oil imports and imperial shipping. Britain and France, together with Israel (which had its own conflict with Egypt over border raids and shipping blockades), secretly coordinated a plan: Israel would invade the Sinai Peninsula, giving Britain and France a pretext to intervene militarily "to separate the combatants" and reoccupy the canal zone. The invasion began in late October 1956 and was militarily successful within days — but its political outcome delivered the sharpest blow to Britain's global standing since the Second World War.

The United States, under President Eisenhower, refused to support the operation and actively worked against it, threatening to sell off British government bonds and block emergency loans from the International Monetary Fund at a moment when Britain's currency reserves were already fragile. The Soviet Union simultaneously threatened intervention on Egypt's behalf. Facing a currency crisis with no American backing, Britain had no realistic option but to withdraw its forces, and did so within weeks, followed by France and Israel.

The significance of Suez lies less in the battlefield than in what the episode revealed: Britain could no longer act as an independent great power without American financial and diplomatic consent. This was a structural, not merely a political, humiliation — it exposed that the material basis of British global power (reserve currency stability, military logistics, alliance dependence) had shifted decisively to the United States. Prime Minister Anthony Eden resigned within months, and postwar British foreign policy afterward consistently deferred to Washington rather than acting unilaterally.

As a problem-solving exercise, treat Suez as a case study in power analysis: when assessing whether a state's foreign-policy signaling matches its actual capability, examine constraint variables — currency and reserve dependence, alliance leverage, and access to financing — since these, not troop numbers or initial battlefield success, determined the crisis's real outcome and accelerated the broader wave of decolonization already underway.

### france_fifth_republic_eu_integration (13.3s)

## France Fifth Republic Eu Integration

By 1958, the Fourth Republic's parliamentary system had collapsed under the strain of the Algerian War. Army officers in Algiers, fearing the government would negotiate away French rule, threatened a coup; the National Assembly, unable to govern or resolve the crisis, invited Charles de Gaulle back to power on the condition that he draft a new constitution. The result was the Fifth Republic, ratified by referendum in September 1958, which replaced a weak executive dependent on shifting parliamentary coalitions with a strong president elected to set foreign and defense policy directly, alongside a prime minister answerable to parliament for domestic affairs. This "semi-presidential" design was built specifically to survive the kind of crisis that had just destroyed its predecessor: a president insulated from day-to-day legislative maneuvering could make hard, unpopular decisions — including, eventually, Algerian independence in 1962 — without the government falling every time deputies objected.

The timing links two events that might otherwise look unrelated. One year before the Fifth Republic's founding, France had signed the Treaty of Rome (1957), creating the European Economic Community with West Germany, Italy, and the Benelux countries. This was not a coincidence of a shared world stage — it reflected a single underlying calculation. Postwar France faced a choice about where to invest its diminishing capacity for national assertion: sustaining an empire increasingly resistant to it, or building a tariff-free European market that would give French industry and agriculture guaranteed access to its most important trading partners. De Gaulle, despite his fierce nationalism and suspicion of supranational bodies, chose to work within the EEC framework rather than reject it, calculating that economic integration on French-influenced terms served national strength better than colonial retention did.

Consider this as a resource-allocation problem: a state with finite political and financial capital must choose where competing claims — colonial administration, military suppression of insurgency, industrial modernization, and regional partnership — are worth funding. France's decision to formalize European integration while cutting loose Algeria (and most of its African colonies by 1960) illustrates a broader pattern: declining powers often trade formal territorial control for embedded influence within cooperative institutions, betting that shared markets yield more durable leverage than force.

### germany_wirtschaftswunder (13.2s)

## Germany Wirtschaftswunder

**Definition.** The *Wirtschaftswunder* ("economic miracle") describes West Germany's rapid reconstruction from a bombed-out, occupied economy in 1945 into Western Europe's largest industrial power by the mid-1960s. Three institutional pillars anchored this recovery. First, the 1948 currency reform replaced the worthless Reichsmark with the Deutsche Mark and paired it with Ludwig Erhard's near-total abolition of Nazi-era price controls and rationing, which immediately restored incentives to produce and trade. Second, NATO membership in 1955 gave West Germany a security guarantee that let it rearm modestly under Allied oversight while devoting the bulk of national investment to civilian industry rather than a large standing military. Third, founding membership in the European Economic Community (1957) opened tariff-free access to a market of over 170 million consumers, letting German manufacturers—already efficient from currency and price liberalization—scale exports of machinery, chemicals, and automobiles across the continent.

**Worked example.** Consider West Germany's GDP growth trajectory as a case of compounding: real GNP grew at roughly 8% annually through the 1950s. Applying the compound growth relationship $A = P(1+r)^t$ with $P$ as 1950 output, $r = 0.08$, and $t = 10$ years gives $A \approx P(1.08)^{10} \approx 2.16P$—output more than doubled in a decade. This is not a coincidence of raw industriousness; it reflects the interaction of the three pillars: currency stability enabled investment, security guarantees freed capital from defense spending, and EEC market access absorbed the resulting output. Marshall Plan aid (roughly $1.4 billion to West Germany) provided initial capital, but the multiplier came from institutional reform, not aid volume alone—France and Britain received comparable or larger sums with slower recovery.

**Problem-solving application.** When analyzing any postwar recovery case, ask three diagnostic questions modeled on this example: (1) Was the currency stabilized and price signals restored, so producers had incentive to sell rather than barter or hoard? (2) Did a security arrangement (alliance, treaty, occupation guarantee) reduce the recovering state's own defense burden? (3) Did the state gain preferential access to a large export market? A state satisfying only one or two of these criteria—compare Weimar Germany's 1920s stabilization, which lacked a durable security framework and collapsed under the Depression—should be expected to recover more slowly or reverse. This three-factor framework generalizes beyond Germany to evaluate other rapid postwar or post-crisis recoveries, such as Japan's contemporaneous "miracle" under similar U.S. security guarantees and market access.

### uk_eec_membership (12.8s)

## Uk Eec Membership

By 1973, Britain's imperial-era economic model was defunct. The trauma of the Suez Crisis had already exposed the limits of independent British power abroad; two decades later, the search for a new economic foundation led to the same conclusion many former colonial subjects reached about their own futures — go it alone, or bind yourself to a larger structure. Britain chose the latter, formally joining the European Economic Community (EEC) on January 1, 1973, under Prime Minister Edward Heath.

This was not a first attempt. Britain had applied in 1963 and again in 1967, and both times French President Charles de Gaulle vetoed entry. De Gaulle's objections were pointed: he viewed Britain as insufficiently "European," too economically dependent on the United States, and too attached to Commonwealth trade preferences to be a reliable partner in a continental project. He also suspected — correctly, in some British political circles — that London saw the EEC primarily as a free-trade zone rather than a step toward political union, a divergence in vision that would resurface in British-EU relations for the next five decades. Only after de Gaulle's resignation in 1969 did the diplomatic path open, and Heath's government negotiated the terms of accession that took effect in 1973.

Applying this concept requires connecting economic motive to political consequence. By the early 1970s, Commonwealth trade was shrinking as a share of British commerce, while Western Europe's economies were growing rapidly under the EEC's common market. Membership offered British exporters tariff-free access to a large, wealthy, and geographically close market — a more promising growth engine than a loosely connected empire-turned-Commonwealth. But the decision also meant accepting the Common Agricultural Policy, contributing to a shared budget, and submitting to supranational rules, all of which became recurring sources of domestic political friction.

A useful analytical exercise: compare Suez (1956) and EEC entry (1973) as two responses to the same underlying problem — the end of British global primacy. Suez was a failed attempt to act unilaterally as a great power; EEC membership was an acceptance that Britain's economic security now depended on regional integration rather than independent action. Students should be able to explain why the second choice, however contested at the time, proved more durable than the first.

## Economic Integration Pattern

An economic integration pattern describes a recurring situation in historical timelines in which states with sharply different starting conditions converge on membership in the same supranational institution, because the institution solves a problem that each state faces for a different reason. In the German, French, and British chains, this pattern surfaces around the postwar EEC/EU trajectory: occupied and divided Germany needed integration to regain sovereignty and legitimacy without reviving fears of German power; France, having lost its colonial empire and global reach, needed integration to project continued influence through a new institutional channel; and Britain, victorious but financially and industrially exhausted, needed integration to access markets and capital it could no longer command through empire or independent strength. Three different problems, one shared solution.

This is a genuinely historical-structural concept, not a mathematical one, so the right tool is comparative reasoning rather than formal notation: read each country's chain for its 1945 starting condition, its stated motive for approaching the institution, and the date of accession, then ask what functional need the institution met in each case.

Worked example: Compare accession motives across the three chains. Germany joins early (as a founding member of the ECSC/EEC) primarily to rebuild trust and export capacity under multilateral oversight — pooling coal and steel production made rearmament visible and monitorable, addressing neighbors' security concerns while restoring German industrial legitimacy. France, as co-founder, uses the same institution to lock in cheap German coal and to retain a leadership role in European affairs that empire could no longer supply. Britain, by contrast, declines to join at the founding moment, still oriented toward the Commonwealth and the "special relationship" with the US, and only applies later (1961, then again in 1967, succeeding in 1973) once its trade patterns and relative economic weight make continental markets unavoidable. The convergence point is identical; the paths and timing diverge according to each country's distinct postwar constraints.

Problem-solving application: When you encounter multiple national chains converging on one institution, don't just note the convergence — reconstruct each nation's independent motive by checking three data points in its chain: the immediate postwar condition, the specific resource or legitimacy deficit that condition created, and how the institution's stated function addresses that deficit. This lets you distinguish surface-level convergence (same institution, same date range) from deeper explanation (different problems, same institutional solution), which is the actual analytical payoff of comparing parallel country chains rather than reading them in isolation.
