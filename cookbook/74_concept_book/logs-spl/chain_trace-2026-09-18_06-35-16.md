## 木

Picture a tree: a trunk with branches spreading out at the top and roots below. That's the shape behind 木 (mù), a pictograph — a character drawn to look like the thing it means. The horizontal stroke is the branches, the vertical stroke is the trunk, and the two slanting strokes at the bottom are the roots digging in.

But 木 does more than sit alone meaning "tree" or "wood." It's a building block — a radical — and it plays two very different jobs depending on where it lands in a character.

**Job 1: lending meaning.** When 木 shows up as a meaning-radical, it tells you the character is connected to trees, wood, or plants in some way. Look at 林 (lín), "forest" or "woods":

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{林}_{\text{forest}}$$

Two trees standing together, literally becoming a forest. Or take 休 (xiū), "to rest":

$$\underbrace{亻}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{休}_{\text{rest}}$$

A person leaning against a tree — resting in its shade.

**Job 2: lending sound.** In other characters, 木 isn't about meaning at all — it just contributes a sound. In 沐 (mù), "to bathe" or "wash," the water radical (氵) carries the meaning, while 木 simply tells you how to pronounce it: mù, the same sound as the tree itself.

So one brick, two roles: sometimes 木 paints a picture of meaning, sometimes it hums a note of sound. Learning to tell which job it's doing is a real skill of reading Chinese.

**Practice:** Look at 相 (xiāng) — it contains 木. Can you find another radical inside it? Guess whether 木 is likely working for meaning or sound here, and explain your reasoning in one sentence.
