## Physiology

Physiology is the study of how living systems function — the mechanical, chemical, and electrical processes that let cells, organs, and organ systems carry out the business of staying alive. Where anatomy asks "what is this structure?", physiology asks "what does it do, and how does the body keep it working within safe limits?" That second question points to the field's central organizing idea: homeostasis, the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite a constantly changing external one, enforced by a single recurring mechanism — the negative feedback loop.

A feedback loop works like this: a sensor detects a deviation from a target value (the *set point*), a control center (often the brain, particularly the hypothalamus) compares the reading to that target, and an effector produces a response that pushes the variable back toward normal, which in turn shuts off the original signal. This one pattern — sensor, control center, effector, shutoff — is what regulates nearly every stable variable in the body: core temperature near $37^\circ\text{C}$, blood glucose near 70–100 mg/dL fasting, blood pH within 7.35–7.45. Cross these ranges by much and enzymes misfold, nerve signaling fails, or cells die.

Worked example: consider thermoregulation. If core temperature rises above $37.5^\circ\text{C}$, thermoreceptors in the skin and hypothalamus detect the change. The hypothalamus triggers two effector responses — sweat glands increase secretion (evaporative cooling) and skin blood vessels dilate (vasodilation, radiating heat outward). As temperature falls back toward $37^\circ\text{C}$, the deviation shrinks, sweating and vasodilation taper off, and the loop settles.

Problem-solving application: once you know the sensor→control center→effector→shutoff pattern, you can reason in reverse — given a malfunction, identify which link broke. Suppose a patient sweats normally in a hot room but their skin never flushes or feels warm to the touch: is the sensor failing to detect the temperature rise, is the hypothalamus failing to send the vasodilation signal, or are the blood vessels themselves not responding to that signal? Sorting a failure into sensor, control-center, or effector is the same three-part checklist physiologists apply to any homeostatic disorder, whether it involves temperature, glucose, or blood pressure — the question is always: where, specifically, did the feedback loop break?
