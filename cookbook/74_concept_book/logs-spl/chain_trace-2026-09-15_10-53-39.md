### 子 (6.0s)

## 子

Picture a baby wrapped snugly in a blanket, only its little head poking out, arms tucked in safe and warm. That's exactly what the character 子 once looked like — a tiny picture of a swaddled infant! You can almost see the round head on top and the wrapped-up body below.

Long ago, this character simply meant "child" or "baby." But here's something interesting: over time, people started using 子 for another job too. It became a helpful little ending that turns other words into nouns — the name of a *thing*.

Think of 子 like a small tag you stick onto a word, saying, "this is officially an object now!" For example, when 子 joins 桌 (table-idea), it helps form 桌子, the everyday word for "table." The pieces build together like this:

$$
\underbrace{桌}_{\text{table}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{noun-suffix}} = 桌子 \; \text{(a table)}
$$

Now you try: can you think of two things around your house that might get their own "-子" name tag?

### 女 (6.8s)

## 女

Long, long ago, someone looked at a woman kneeling gracefully, hands folded in front of her, and decided to draw exactly that. Picture a person tucking her knees under her, arms crossed gently at the waist — that curved, folded shape became 女.

Why does this shape feel right? Because it captures a pose, not just an idea. Ancient artists didn't write "woman" as a label — they sketched a moment, a posture you could recognize at a glance, the way a stick figure sketch captures "person running."

This character rarely stands entirely alone in stories about families. It becomes a building block — a seed that grows into other characters about mothers, daughters, and relationships, because 女 is the anchor piece that carries the meaning of "woman" or "female" into them.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman, kneeling}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 好 \; (\text{good})
$$

A woman with a child — together, that felt like goodness itself to the people who invented this writing.

**Now you try**: Draw a simple kneeling stick figure. Can you see 女 hiding inside your sketch?

### 宀 (9.1s)

## 宀

Imagine a tiny roof drawn with just two strokes — a dot on top, then a wide curve reaching down like eaves spreading over a house. This is not a whole house yet, just the roof, the shelter, the part that keeps rain and wind away from whatever lives underneath.

Why does this feel right? Think of a rooftop shape you'd doodle over a drawing of a home — a peak, then slanting sides. That's exactly what this radical looks like: a roof floating above empty space, waiting for something to move in.

On its own, 宀 rarely stands alone. It's a helper piece, always sitting on top of other characters, quietly telling you: "whatever is under me is inside a home, protected, settled, or contained." Characters built with this roof often mean things like house, peace, or a place of safety — because a roof is what turns open space into a home.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{piece\ below}_{meaning} = \text{a sheltered thing}$$

Now you try: Draw the roof shape by itself. Then imagine — what would you put underneath it to make it feel like *your* home?

### 好 (5.4s)

## 好

Imagine you peek into a home three thousand years ago. What do you see that means "everything is right with the world"? A mother sitting with her child. That's it. That's the picture ancient Chinese writers chose for the idea of "good."

Why does this feel right? Because a mother and child together is one of the oldest, most universal pictures of safety and happiness. No danger, no hunger, no worry — just two people who belong together. The character 好 simply glues these two pictures side by side: 女 (woman) sits next to 子 (child).

$$
\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = 好 \ (good)
$$

Notice the character isn't showing you a definition — it's showing you a scene, and trusting you to feel why that scene means "good."

**Now you try:** Look at 好 again. Can you spot the "woman" part and the "child" part inside it? Next time you see this character, picture that little family — that's the secret it's been holding for three thousand years.

### 字 (7.3s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny child, safe under a roof at night. That's the picture behind the word for "character" itself: a roof over a child. Why this picture? Because the people who invented Chinese writing thought of new characters the way you'd think of a baby — something small that gets born, and then grows up cared for by the family already living in the house.

That's the whole idea of 字: characters aren't just symbols someone drew once and froze forever. They're "born" from older, simpler pieces, the way a child is born into a family and raised using what the family already has — habits, tools, love. A roof gives shelter; a child grows inside it. A new character is sheltered and grown from pieces that already existed.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字 \text{ (character, born and raised)}$$

Now you try: look at a character you know well. Can you guess which older pieces it might have been "born" from?

### vocabulary_compounds (5.9s)

## Vocabulary Compounds

Imagine you already know two LEGO bricks: a little tree and a little tree again. Alone, each one means "tree." But snap two trees together and something new appears — a grove. Snap three together and you get a whole forest, thick and shadowy. That is exactly what happens with 森林 (forest): 木 (tree) plus 木 becomes 林 (grove), and then 林 plus another 木 becomes 森 (many trees, a forest). Characters are bricks made of strokes — words are bricks made of *characters*. It is the same building game, just one floor up.

This is why learning Chinese feels like snapping together LEGO of LEGO: 明天 (tomorrow) is 明 "bright" plus 天 "day/sky" — a bright day yet to come. 好人 (good person) is simply 好 "good" plus 人 "person." 安全 (safety) is 安 "peaceful" plus 全 "whole/complete."

$$\underbrace{安}_{\text{peaceful}} + \underbrace{全}_{\text{whole}} = \text{安全 (safety)}$$

**Now you try:** Take 大 (big) and 人 (person). What word do you think they build together?
