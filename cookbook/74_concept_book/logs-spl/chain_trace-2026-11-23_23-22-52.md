### kinetic_energy (0.6s)

## Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses because it is moving. For an object of mass $m$ translating at speed $v$ (with $v \ll c$, so relativistic effects are negligible), the kinetic energy is

$$KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

This expression follows directly from the work-energy theorem: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, $W_{net} = \Delta KE$. Starting from Newton's second law, $F = ma$, and integrating force over displacement,

$$W = \int F\,dx = \int ma\,dx = \int m\frac{dv}{dt}\,dx = \int mv\,dv = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2$$

using the substitution $dx/dt = v$. This derivation shows that $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ is not an arbitrary definition — it is the quantity whose change exactly tracks the work done by a net force, which is why kinetic energy is so useful for solving motion problems without tracking time explicitly.

**Worked example.** A 1200 kg car accelerates from rest to 25 m/s. Its kinetic energy at that speed is

$$KE = \frac{1}{2}(1200\text{ kg})(25\text{ m/s})^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1200)(625) = 375{,}000\text{ J} = 375\text{ kJ}$$

By the work-energy theorem, this is exactly the net work the engine (net of friction and drag) must have delivered to reach that speed, regardless of how long the acceleration took or whether it was uniform.

**Problem-solving application.** Kinetic energy's real power is in problems where forces are known but time is not. Suppose the same 1200 kg car, moving at 25 m/s, brakes to a stop under a constant friction force of 6000 N. Rather than solving for acceleration and time separately, apply the work-energy theorem directly: the net work done by friction over stopping distance $d$ must equal the negative of the car's kinetic energy, $-Fd = 0 - KE_i$. Solving,

$$d = \frac{KE_i}{F} = \frac{375{,}000\text{ J}}{6000\text{ N}} = 62.5\text{ m}$$

This illustrates a key problem-solving strategy: whenever a force and a change in speed are known but the time of interaction is not, relate them through work and kinetic energy rather than through kinematics. Also note the quadratic dependence on $v$: doubling the initial speed would quadruple $KE_i$, and therefore quadruple the stopping distance for the same braking force — a fact central to vehicle safety engineering, since it explains why small increases in speed produce disproportionately larger stopping distances and collision severity.

### temperature (12.8s)

## Temperature

**Definition.** Temperature is the quantity a thermometer measures. Operationally, two objects have the same temperature if no net heat flows between them when they are placed in thermal contact (the zeroth law of thermodynamics). A thermometer exploits this. It reaches equilibrium with the object, and a physical property that varies with temperature, such as the length of a mercury column, then gives a reading.

Microscopically, temperature measures the average random translational kinetic energy of particles. For an ideal monatomic gas:

$$\langle K \rangle = \tfrac{3}{2} k_B T$$

where $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the absolute temperature in kelvin. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, so $T_K = T_C + 273.15$. Use kelvin in any formula where $T$ appears directly.

Temperature is not the same as heat or total thermal energy. Temperature is an intensive quantity that does not depend on how much material you have. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

**Worked example.** Find the average kinetic energy of a helium atom at room temperature, $27^\circ\text{C}$.

1. Convert to kelvin: $T = 27 + 273.15 \approx 300\ \text{K}$.
2. Apply the formula:

$$\langle K \rangle = \tfrac{3}{2}(1.381\times10^{-23})(300) \approx 6.2\times10^{-21}\ \text{J}$$

This is about $0.039\ \text{eV}$, tiny per atom, but a mole of atoms carries thousands of joules.

**Problem-solving application.** Because $\langle K \rangle = \tfrac12 m\langle v^2\rangle$, the formula also gives a typical particle speed. The root-mean-square speed is

$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}}$$

*Problem:* Compare $v_{\text{rms}}$ for helium ($m = 6.64\times10^{-27}$ kg) and nitrogen ($m = 4.65\times10^{-26}$ kg) at 300 K.

Helium:

$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(1.381\times10^{-23})(300)}{6.64\times10^{-27}}} \approx 1.37\times10^{3}\ \text{m/s}$$

Nitrogen:

$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(1.381\times10^{-23})(300)}{4.65\times10^{-26}}} \approx 517\ \text{m/s}$$

At the same temperature the two gases have the same average kinetic energy, but the lighter helium atoms move about 2.6 times faster. Since $v_{\text{rms}}\propto\sqrt{1/m}$, the speed ratio is $\sqrt{28/4}\approx 2.6$, which matches. This is why helium escapes Earth's atmosphere far more readily than nitrogen.

**Check your understanding.** If the Kelvin temperature doubles, by what factor does $\langle K\rangle$ change, and by what factor does $v_{\text{rms}}$ change? (Answer: $\langle K\rangle$ doubles, and $v_{\text{rms}}$ increases by $\sqrt{2}$.) Note that doubling from $20^\circ\text{C}$ to $40^\circ\text{C}$ does *not* double the kinetic energy. You must convert to kelvin first, and $293\ \text{K}\to313\ \text{K}$ is only a 7% increase.

### volume (0.9s)

## Volume

**Volume** is the amount of three-dimensional space an object or substance occupies. It is measured in cubic units (m³, cm³, L), where 1 L = 1000 cm³ = 10⁻³ m³. Volume is the three-dimensional analog of area: it counts how many unit cubes fit inside a region.

**Volume by slicing.** Suppose a solid lies along an axis from $x = a$ to $x = b$, and the cross-sectional area $A(x)$ at each position $x$ is known. Stack thin slices of thickness $dx$ and add their volumes:

$$V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx.$$

This is Cavalieri's principle expressed as an integral.

**Standard formulas.**

| Solid | Volume |
|---|---|
| Rectangular box | $V = \ell w h$ |
| Cylinder | $V = \pi r^2 h$ |
| Cone | $V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ |
| Sphere | $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ |

### Worked example: deriving the sphere

Place a sphere of radius $R$ centered at the origin. Slicing perpendicular to the $x$-axis gives a circular cross-section of radius $\sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$, so

$$A(x) = \pi (R^2 - x^2).$$

Then

$$V = \int_{-R}^{R} \pi (R^2 - x^2)\,dx = \pi\left[R^2 x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-R}^{R} = \pi\left(2R^3 - \frac{2R^3}{3}\right) = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3.$$

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** A cylindrical tank of radius 2 m and height 5 m is filled with water. How many liters does it hold, and how much does the water weigh? (Water density is 1000 kg/m³.)

1. Volume: $V = \pi r^2 h = \pi (2)^2(5) = 20\pi \approx 62.8\ \text{m}^3$.
2. Convert units: $62.8\ \text{m}^3 \times 1000\ \text{L/m}^3 \approx 62{,}800\ \text{L}$.
3. Mass: $m = \rho V = 1000 \times 62.8 \approx 62{,}800\ \text{kg}$.

**Strategies for any volume problem:**

- **Decompose.** Split irregular objects into boxes, cylinders, cones, or spheres, then add (or subtract for hollow parts).
- **Check units.** Lengths must share a unit before multiplying. Scaling every length by $k$ scales volume by $k^3$, so doubling a radius makes a sphere 8 times larger.
- **Use displacement.** An irregular object's volume equals the volume of fluid it displaces when fully submerged. This is Archimedes' method.
- **Sanity-check.** A sphere's volume should be less than its bounding cube's, $(2R)^3 = 8R^3$. Here $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \approx 4.19R^3$, which passes.

### thermal_expansion (14.7s)

## Thermal Expansion

### Definition

Thermal expansion is the change in a material's size or volume when its temperature changes. Heating increases the average spacing between vibrating atoms, so most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled. For small temperature changes, the change is proportional to $\Delta T$.

**Linear expansion** (length of rods, rails, wires):
$$\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \,\Delta T$$

**Area expansion** (plates): $\Delta A = 2\alpha A_0\,\Delta T$

**Volume expansion** (solids and liquids):
$$\Delta V = \beta V_0 \,\Delta T, \qquad \beta \approx 3\alpha \text{ for isotropic solids}$$

Here $\alpha$ is the coefficient of linear expansion (units $\text{K}^{-1}$ or $^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$) and $\beta$ is the coefficient of volume expansion. Typical values: steel $\alpha \approx 12\times10^{-6}\,\text{K}^{-1}$, aluminum $\approx 23\times10^{-6}\,\text{K}^{-1}$.

The $3\alpha$ relation follows from $V = L^3$. Expanding $(L_0(1+\alpha\Delta T))^3$ and dropping terms of order $(\alpha\Delta T)^2$ and higher gives $V \approx V_0(1+3\alpha\Delta T)$.

### Worked example

A steel bridge span is $L_0 = 200\ \text{m}$ long at $-10^\circ\text{C}$. How much longer is it at $40^\circ\text{C}$?

$$\Delta T = 40-(-10) = 50\ \text{K}$$
$$\Delta L = (12\times10^{-6})(200)(50) = 0.12\ \text{m}$$

The span grows by 12 cm. This is why bridges have expansion joints. Without them, the steel would try to expand and be blocked by its supports.

### Problem-solving application

**Constrained expansion produces stress.** If a rod is held rigidly so it cannot expand, the thermal strain $\alpha\Delta T$ is converted into stress through Young's modulus $E$:
$$\sigma = E\,\alpha\,\Delta T$$

For steel with $E = 200\ \text{GPa}$ and $\Delta T = 50\ \text{K}$:
$$\sigma = (200\times10^{9})(12\times10^{-6})(50) = 1.2\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa} = 120\ \text{MPa}$$

This is a large fraction of the yield strength of structural steel, so a fully constrained span would risk buckling.

**Liquids in containers.** A glass flask holds $500\ \text{mL}$ of ethanol ($\beta = 1.1\times10^{-3}\,\text{K}^{-1}$) and is heated by $20\ \text{K}$. Ignoring the flask's expansion, the liquid volume increases by
$$\Delta V = (1.1\times10^{-3})(500)(20) = 11\ \text{mL}.$$
This is why containers are never filled completely. The same effect makes thermometers work.

**Bimetallic strips.** Two bonded metals with different $\alpha$ values (for example, steel and brass) bend when heated, because the metal with the larger $\alpha$ lengthens more. Thermostats use this bending to open or close a circuit.

**Strategy for problems:**
1. Identify which dimension matters (length, area, or volume) and choose $\alpha$ or $\beta$ accordingly.
2. Compute $\Delta T$ with a consistent sign: a temperature drop gives a negative $\Delta L$.
3. Check that $\alpha\Delta T \ll 1$, which validates the linear approximation.
4. If the object is constrained, convert the blocked strain to stress with $\sigma = E\alpha\Delta T$.

**Caveat:** Water is anomalous. Between $0^\circ\text{C}$ and $4^\circ\text{C}$ it contracts on heating, reaching maximum density near $4^\circ\text{C}$. This is why lakes freeze from the top down.

### pressure (0.8s)

## Pressure

**Pressure** is the force per unit area acting perpendicular to a surface:

$$P = \frac{F_\perp}{A}$$

The SI unit is the pascal: $1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2$. Pressure is a scalar. It has no direction of its own, but the force it produces on a surface always acts perpendicular to that surface. Only the component of force perpendicular to the surface counts.

Pressure measures how concentrated a force is, which is why the same force can do very different things. A person pressing a thumbtack pushes with the same force on both ends. The point has a tiny area, so the pressure there is enormous.

### Worked example

A 60 kg person stands on one foot, and the contact area is $A = 0.020\ \text{m}^2$. Find the pressure on the floor.

The force is the person's weight:

$$F = mg = (60)(9.8) = 588\ \text{N}$$

$$P = \frac{588\ \text{N}}{0.020\ \text{m}^2} \approx 2.9\times10^{4}\ \text{Pa}$$

Now suppose the same person wears a stiletto heel with contact area $A = 1.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$, and all the weight rests on that heel:

$$P = \frac{588}{1.0\times10^{-4}} = 5.9\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}$$

The pressure is about 200 times larger, because the area is 200 times smaller. This is why stiletto heels dent wooden floors that bare feet don't mark.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** A pressure of $1.96\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ acts on a circular viewing window of radius $0.05\ \text{m}$ on a diver's equipment. Find the force on the window.

**Step 1: Area.** Use consistent SI units.

$$A = \pi r^2 = \pi(0.05)^2 = 7.85\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$$

**Step 2: Force.** Rearrange the definition to $F = PA$:

$$F = (1.96\times10^{5})(7.85\times10^{-3}) \approx 1.5\times10^{3}\ \text{N}$$

This is about 1500 N, roughly the weight of a 160 kg mass, pressing on a window the size of a small coaster. A strategy that works for most pressure problems:

1. Identify the force and the surface it acts on. Use only the component perpendicular to the surface.
2. Find the area in $\text{m}^2$. Convert from $\text{cm}^2$ by multiplying by $10^{-4}$.
3. Use $P = F/A$ to find pressure, or $F = PA$ to find force.
4. Check units: $\text{N}/\text{m}^2$ gives pascals.

Note how the two forms connect. If the same 1500 N were spread over a window ten times larger in area, the pressure would drop to one tenth. Comparing two situations by their area ratio, as in the stiletto example, is often faster than computing each pressure separately.

### linear_thermal_expansion (13.1s)

## Linear Thermal Expansion

**Definition.** When a solid is heated, its atoms vibrate with larger amplitude and, because the interatomic force is asymmetric, their average spacing increases. For a small temperature change, the resulting change in length is proportional to both the original length and the temperature change:

$$\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \,\Delta T$$

Here $L_0$ is the original length, $\Delta T = T_f - T_i$ is the temperature change, and $\alpha$ is the **coefficient of linear expansion**. Its units are $\text{K}^{-1}$ (equivalently $^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$), and its value depends on the material. Typical values are about $12\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for steel, $23\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for aluminum, and $3\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for Pyrex glass. The final length is $L = L_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)$. If $\Delta T<0$, the object contracts. The linear relation holds well only when $\alpha\Delta T \ll 1$, which is true for ordinary temperature ranges.

**Worked example.** A steel bridge girder is $L_0 = 40.0\ \text{m}$ long at $-10^\circ\text{C}$. How much longer is it at $35^\circ\text{C}$?

$$\Delta T = 35-(-10) = 45\ \text{K}$$

$$\Delta L = (12\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1})(40.0\ \text{m})(45\ \text{K}) = 2.16\times10^{-2}\ \text{m} \approx 2.2\ \text{cm}$$

The girder grows by about 2 cm, which is only 0.05% of its length. That is small, but it is large enough to buckle a rigid structure.

**Problem-solving application: constrained expansion.** Engineers leave expansion gaps in bridges and railway tracks. Suppose a steel rail of length $L_0$ is clamped so it cannot expand. The thermal strain it would have had is

$$\frac{\Delta L}{L_0} = \alpha\,\Delta T,$$

and the wall must compress the rail by that same fractional amount. Using Young's modulus $Y$, the stress is

$$\frac{F}{A} = Y\,\alpha\,\Delta T.$$

For steel, $Y \approx 2.0\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}$. With $\Delta T = 45\ \text{K}$:

$$\frac{F}{A} = (2.0\times10^{11})(12\times10^{-6})(45) \approx 1.1\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa}.$$

That is about 108 MPa, a large fraction of steel's yield strength. This is why rails buckle on hot days if they are not pre-stressed or given gaps.

**Strategy for any problem:**
1. Identify $L_0$ and compute $\Delta T$ with its sign.
2. Look up $\alpha$ for the material.
3. Compute $\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T$, or the final length $L=L_0+\Delta L$.
4. If the object is constrained, convert the blocked expansion into stress with $Y\alpha\Delta T$.
5. Check the answer. $\Delta L$ should be tiny compared with $L_0$.

**Related case: two materials.** When two materials with different $\alpha$ are bonded, as in a bimetallic strip, heating makes the higher-$\alpha$ side lengthen more, so the strip bends toward the lower-$\alpha$ side. This is the principle behind mechanical thermostats.

### thermal_stress (14.3s)

## Thermal Stress

### Definition

When a material is heated, it expands; when cooled, it contracts. If it is free to move, no stress develops. **Thermal stress** arises when that expansion or contraction is *constrained*, for example by rigid walls, clamps, or neighboring material at a different temperature. The constraint forces the material to deform mechanically by the amount it would otherwise have changed thermally, and that mechanical deformation is stress.

For a temperature change $\Delta T$, the free (unconstrained) fractional length change is

$$\frac{\Delta L}{L} = \alpha\,\Delta T,$$

where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of linear thermal expansion. If the ends are fixed so the net length change is zero, the mechanical strain must cancel the thermal strain, $\varepsilon = -\alpha\,\Delta T$. With Young's modulus $E$ (Hooke's law, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$):

$$\sigma = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T.$$

The negative sign means heating a constrained rod produces *compressive* stress; cooling produces *tensile* stress.

**Volume version.** For a material confined on all sides, the volumetric expansion is $\Delta V/V = \beta\,\Delta T$, with $\beta \approx 3\alpha$ for isotropic solids. The bulk modulus $B$ relates pressure change to fractional volume change, $\Delta p = -B\,\Delta V/V$. If the volume is held fixed, the confined material must be compressed by $-\beta\Delta T$, so

$$\Delta p = B\,\beta\,\Delta T.$$

### Worked example

A steel rail ($E = 200\ \text{GPa}$, $\alpha = 12\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) is laid tightly between fixed anchors at $10^\circ\text{C}$. On a summer day it reaches $40^\circ\text{C}$. Find the stress.

$$\sigma = -E\alpha\,\Delta T = -(200\times10^{9})(12\times10^{-6})(30) = -7.2\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}$$

The rail carries about $72$ MPa of compression. Since steel's yield strength is a few hundred MPa, this is survivable, but it helps explain why long rails buckle sideways on hot days.

### Problem-solving application

**Sealed liquid.** A rigid container is completely filled with water at $20^\circ\text{C}$ and sealed. Water has $\beta \approx 2.1\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ and $B \approx 2.2\ \text{GPa}$. The temperature rises by $10\ \text{K}$. Estimate the pressure rise.

Ignoring the container's own expansion,

$$\Delta p = B\beta\,\Delta T = (2.2\times10^{9})(2.1\times10^{-4})(10) \approx 4.6\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa} \approx 46\ \text{atm}.$$

A modest 10 K warming produces a pressure that can rupture pipes or tanks, which is why sealed hydraulic systems include expansion tanks or relief valves.

**General strategy:**
1. Decide whether the constraint is one-dimensional (use $E$, $\alpha$) or all-around (use $B$, $\beta$).
2. Compute the free thermal strain, $\alpha\Delta T$ or $\beta\Delta T$.
3. Require the total strain to match the constraint (usually zero); the mechanical strain is the negative of the thermal strain.
4. Multiply by the appropriate modulus and compare with the material's yield or fracture limit.

For partial constraint, such as a rod between slightly flexible supports or a gap of width $g$, subtract the allowed movement first: $\varepsilon = -(\alpha\Delta T - g/L)$. Engineers use expansion joints in bridges and gaps in railway track for exactly this reason: allowing the free thermal strain removes the stress.

### expansion_joints (13.9s)

## Expansion Joints

**Definition.** An expansion joint is a deliberate gap, or a flexible connection, built into a structure so that parts can change length with temperature without generating destructive stress. Most solids expand when heated. For a length $L_0$ whose temperature changes by $\Delta T$, the change in length is

$$\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \,\Delta T,$$

where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of linear thermal expansion. Steel has $\alpha \approx 12\times10^{-6}\ {}^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1}$, and concrete is similar. If the ends of a member are fixed so it cannot expand, it develops a thermal stress instead:

$$\sigma = E\,\alpha\,\Delta T,$$

where $E$ is Young's modulus. This holds because the strain the material is prevented from taking, $\varepsilon = \alpha\Delta T$, is converted to stress by Hooke's law, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$.

**Worked example.** A steel bridge deck is 100 m long. It is built at 15 °C and must survive temperatures from $-25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $+45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, a range of 70 °C.

$$\Delta L = (12\times10^{-6})(100\ \mathrm{m})(70\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}) = 0.084\ \mathrm{m} = 84\ \mathrm{mm}.$$

The joints must absorb about 84 mm of total movement. Now suppose the deck were fully restrained and heated by 40 °C, with $E = 200$ GPa:

$$\sigma = (200\times10^{9})(12\times10^{-6})(40) = 96\ \mathrm{MPa}.$$

Structural steel yields near 250 MPa. A modest temperature swing already uses up a large fraction of the material's strength, and in a slender member, compression like this can cause buckling well before yield.

**Problem-solving application.** Sizing a gap follows a fixed procedure:

1. Find the extreme temperatures the structure will see, and the installation temperature.
2. Compute $\Delta L$ for the heating case and the cooling case separately.
3. Add a safety margin for construction tolerance, usually 10–25%.
4. Choose a joint type that spans that range, such as a finger joint, a sliding plate, or a rubber seal.

*Example:* Rails are 25 m long and laid at 20 °C. The hottest rail temperature is 60 °C, so $\Delta T = 40\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$:

$$\Delta L = (12\times10^{-6})(25)(40) = 0.012\ \mathrm{m} = 12\ \mathrm{mm}.$$

A gap of at least 12 mm at installation is therefore needed to keep the rail from pushing against its neighbor at the hottest temperature. At $-20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the rail shrinks by $(12\times10^{-6})(25)(40) = 12$ mm, so the gap widens to roughly 24 mm. This is why the gap must be set for the installation temperature, not for the hottest day alone. Modern continuous welded rail removes most gaps. It relies instead on pre-tensioning the rail and anchoring it firmly, and it keeps expansion joints only at the ends of long welded sections.

**Check your understanding.** A 50 m concrete span ($\alpha = 10\times10^{-6}\ {}^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1}$) sees a 60 °C seasonal swing. Show that it needs about 30 mm of movement capacity before any safety margin is added.
