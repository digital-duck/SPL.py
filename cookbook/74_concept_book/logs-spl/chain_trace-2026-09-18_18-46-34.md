## Probability Amplitude

A quantum system does not carry a probability for each possible configuration the way a die carries a $\frac{1}{6}$ chance for each face. Instead, it carries a **probability amplitude** — a complex number $\psi$ assigned to each configuration (a position, a spin orientation, a path through a double slit). The amplitude itself is not observable; what connects it to experiment is **Born's rule**:

$$P = |\psi|^2 = \psi^* \psi$$

Squaring the modulus converts a complex number into a non-negative real number that behaves like a probability, and when you sum $|\psi|^2$ over all mutually exclusive outcomes, the total equals 1 (the amplitudes are "normalized").

**Worked example.** Suppose a particle can reach a detector via two paths, with amplitudes $\psi_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\psi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} e^{i\theta}$. If only path 1 is open, $P_1 = |\psi_1|^2 = \frac{1}{2}$. If only path 2 is open, $P_2 = |\psi_2|^2 = \frac{1}{2}$. Classically, with both paths open, you'd expect $P_1 + P_2 = 1$. Quantum mechanically, you must add the *amplitudes* first, then square:

$$P = |\psi_1 + \psi_2|^2 = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + 2\,\text{Re}(\psi_1^* \psi_2) = 1 + \cos\theta$$

The cross term $2\,\text{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2)$ is the interference term, and it exists only because $\psi$ carries a phase — an imaginary part. If amplitudes were forced to be real and non-negative, this term would vanish and quantum mechanics would collapse into ordinary probability theory. The imaginary part is not a bookkeeping convenience; it is the mathematical carrier of relative phase, and relative phase is what makes interference constructive ($\theta = 0$, $P=2$) or destructive ($\theta = \pi$, $P=0$) at a given detector location.

**Problem-solving application.** To predict an experimental outcome: (1) identify every indistinguishable path or configuration leading to that outcome, (2) assign each a complex amplitude, (3) sum the amplitudes if the paths are indistinguishable (interference applies), or sum the probabilities if a measurement has distinguished them (interference is destroyed), and (4) apply Born's rule at the end, never before. This ordering — amplitudes first, modulus-squared last — is the single most common point of error when translating a quantum scenario into a calculation.

### quantum_state (0.0s)

## Quantum State

The complete description of a quantum system is a vector $|\psi\rangle$ in a complex vector space called Hilbert space. This vector — the state vector — encodes everything that can, even in principle, be known about the system. Unlike a classical particle, whose state is a definite position and momentum, a quantum state is generally a superposition: a linear combination of possible outcomes, written

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i |i\rangle$$

where the $|i\rangle$ are basis states (e.g., possible measurement outcomes) and the complex coefficients $c_i$ are probability amplitudes. The state is normalized, $\sum_i |c_i|^2 = 1$, since the outcome probabilities $|c_i|^2$ must sum to 1. Before measurement, the system does not "secretly" have one definite value hidden from us — it genuinely exists in the superposition itself.

**Worked example.** Consider a qubit — the quantum analog of a bit — with basis states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. The state

$$|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle$$

is a valid quantum state because $\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 = 1$. Measuring this qubit yields $0$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ and $1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ — not because the qubit was secretly one or the other, but because measurement forces the superposition to "collapse" into one basis outcome, with the amplitudes fixing the odds.

**Problem-solving application.** Given a proposed state $|\phi\rangle = a|0\rangle + b|1\rangle$ with $a = 0.6$ and $b = 0.9$, is it physically valid? Check normalization: $a^2 + b^2 = 0.36 + 0.81 = 1.17 \neq 1$. It fails — this is not a legitimate quantum state, and no measurement probabilities could be computed from it as written. To fix it, rescale by dividing both coefficients by $\sqrt{1.17}$, restoring $\sum |c_i|^2 = 1$. This normalization check is the first diagnostic step in any problem involving quantum states: before computing measurement probabilities, expectation values, or how a state evolves under an operator, confirm the vector is properly normalized, since every physical prediction is built on top of it.

### observable (0.0s)

## Observable

An observable is a physical quantity that can, in principle, be measured on a quantum system — position, momentum, energy, spin, angular momentum. In classical mechanics an observable is just a function of the system's state that returns a number. In quantum mechanics an observable is represented by a **Hermitian operator** $\hat{A}$ acting on the system's state vector $|\psi\rangle$ in Hilbert space. Hermiticity ($\hat{A} = \hat{A}^\dagger$) is not a formal nicety — it is what guarantees that the operator's eigenvalues are real numbers, since a measurement outcome (a meter reading, a detector click) must be a real number, never a complex one.

The eigenvalue equation for an observable is

$$\hat{A}|a_i\rangle = a_i|a_i\rangle$$

where $|a_i\rangle$ is an eigenstate of $\hat{A}$ and $a_i$ is the corresponding eigenvalue. The postulate of quantum measurement states that the *only* possible results of measuring $\hat{A}$ are its eigenvalues $a_i$, and that immediately after a measurement yielding $a_i$, the system's state collapses into the eigenstate $|a_i\rangle$.

**Worked example.** The energy observable is the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$. For a particle in an infinite square well of width $L$, solving $\hat{H}|\psi_n\rangle = E_n|\psi_n\rangle$ gives the discrete eigenvalues

$$E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Any energy measurement on this particle must return one of these $E_n$ values — never something in between. This is the direct link between the mathematical spectrum of $\hat{H}$ and the physically observed quantization of energy.

**Problem-solving application.** Given an operator, the practical task is to find its eigenvalues, since those are the measurable predictions. For the spin-$z$ observable $\hat{S}_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_z$ with $\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$, solving $\det(\hat{S}_z - \lambda I) = 0$ gives $\lambda = \pm\hbar/2$: the two possible outcomes of a spin measurement, matching the "spin-up/spin-down" results seen experimentally. This procedure — diagonalize the Hermitian operator, read off the eigenvalues as possible outcomes — is the general method for predicting what any observable can yield.

### superposition (0.1s)

## Superposition

A qubit's state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ is not a statement of ignorance about which value the system "really" has — it is the system's actual, complete description while unmeasured. This is the defining departure from classical bits, and it follows directly from a postulate: the space of quantum states is a Hilbert space, and Hilbert spaces are linear. If $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are valid states, then any complex-weighted sum $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ is also a valid state, provided normalization holds: $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. Superposition is simply what linearity looks like once you accept that quantum states are vectors.

The subtlety that trips up most learners is what happens when two superposed paths recombine. Classically, if an outcome can occur via two independent routes with probabilities $p_1$ and $p_2$, the total probability is $p_1 + p_2$. Quantum mechanically, you add the *amplitudes* first, then square: if a final state is reached via two paths with amplitudes $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$, the combined amplitude is $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, and the probability is $|\alpha_1 + \alpha_2|^2 = |\alpha_1|^2 + |\alpha_2|^2 + 2\,\text{Re}(\alpha_1^*\alpha_2)$. That cross term is interference — it can be positive (constructive) or negative (destructive), and it has no classical analog because classical probabilities are never negative or complex.

**Worked example.** Consider the balanced state $|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle$, produced by a Hadamard gate acting on $|0\rangle$. Measuring in the computational basis gives $0$ with probability $|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $1$ with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ — a fair coin flip, but one where the "coin" carries phase information a real coin cannot.

**Problem-solving application.** Interference is what makes quantum algorithms outperform classical ones: an algorithm engineers amplitudes so that paths leading to *wrong* answers cancel (destructive interference) while paths leading to the *correct* answer reinforce (constructive interference). Grover's search and Shor's factoring both hinge on this amplitude-steering trick, not on "trying all answers at once" — a common but misleading shorthand for superposition.

### measurement (0.0s)

## Measurement

Measurement is the quantitative determination of a physical property, expressed as a numerical value paired with a unit. Every measurement takes one of two forms: a **direct measurement**, in which an instrument compares the property to a standard (using a ruler to find length, a balance to find mass), or a **derived measurement**, in which the value is calculated from other measured quantities using a known relationship. Density, for instance, is never read directly off a device — it is computed from measured mass and volume via $\rho = m/V$.

**Worked example.** Suppose you measure the mass of a metal block as $m = 47.6\ \text{g}$ using a balance, and its dimensions as $2.0\ \text{cm} \times 3.0\ \text{cm} \times 4.0\ \text{cm}$ using calipers. The volume is a derived quantity:

$$
V = 2.0 \times 3.0 \times 4.0 = 24\ \text{cm}^3
$$

The density is then a second derived quantity, built from the first:

$$
\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{47.6\ \text{g}}{24\ \text{cm}^3} \approx 1.98\ \text{g/cm}^3
$$

Comparing this to a reference table, you'd identify the metal as magnesium ($\rho \approx 1.74$–$1.98\ \text{g/cm}^3$ depending on alloy), illustrating how a chain of measurements supports an inference.

**Problem-solving application.** A crucial practical skill is tracking how uncertainty propagates through derived measurements. If each caliper reading carries an uncertainty of $\pm 0.1\ \text{cm}$, that uncertainty does not vanish when the dimensions are multiplied — it compounds. For a quantity $Q$ computed as a product or quotient of measured values, the relative uncertainties add:

$$
\frac{\delta Q}{Q} \approx \frac{\delta x}{x} + \frac{\delta y}{y} + \frac{\delta z}{z}
$$

Here each dimension has a relative uncertainty around $0.1/2.0 = 5\%$ to $0.1/4.0 = 2.5\%$, so the volume's relative uncertainty is roughly their sum — about $10\%$ — meaning the reported density should be written as $1.98 \pm 0.20\ \text{g/cm}^3$, not as a bare number. This is the discipline measurement demands: a value without a stated precision is scientifically incomplete, because it hides whether an apparent difference between two measurements is real or merely instrument noise. Recognizing when a derived result is trustworthy — and when it merely reflects accumulated measurement error — is a skill that recurs across every quantitative science.

### entanglement (0.0s)

## Entanglement

A two-particle quantum system lives in a combined state space, and the vast majority of states in that space cannot be factored into independent pieces. A state $|\psi\rangle$ is **entangled** when it cannot be written as a product $|\psi_A\rangle \otimes |\psi_B\rangle$ for any single-particle states $|\psi_A\rangle$ and $|\psi_B\rangle$. When entanglement is present, the two particles have no individual, well-defined states of their own — only the joint system does, even if the particles are light-years apart.

**Worked example.** Consider the singlet state of two spin-1/2 particles:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|{\uparrow}\rangle_A|{\downarrow}\rangle_B - |{\downarrow}\rangle_A|{\uparrow}\rangle_B\big)
$$

Try to factor this as $(a|{\uparrow}\rangle_A + b|{\downarrow}\rangle_A)\otimes(c|{\uparrow}\rangle_B + d|{\downarrow}\rangle_B)$. Expanding the product gives terms in $|{\uparrow}\rangle_A|{\uparrow}\rangle_B$ and $|{\downarrow}\rangle_A|{\downarrow}\rangle_B$ with coefficients $ac$ and $bd$. Since $|\psi\rangle$ has no such terms, we'd need $ac = 0$ and $bd = 0$, forcing either $a$ or $b$ to be zero (and likewise $c$ or $d$), which contradicts having nonzero coefficients on both remaining terms. No choice of $a,b,c,d$ works — the state is provably not a product state.

**Problem-solving application.** Measure particle A in the $\{|\uparrow\rangle, |\downarrow\rangle\}$ basis. Because $|\psi\rangle$ is a superposition of only $|\uparrow\rangle_A|\downarrow\rangle_B$ and $|\downarrow\rangle_A|\uparrow\rangle_B$, the outcome for A perfectly determines B's state: if A collapses to $|\uparrow\rangle$, B is instantly $|\downarrow\rangle$, and vice versa, with 50% probability for each branch. This holds no matter the distance separating them.

This does *not* allow faster-than-light signaling. The experimenter holding particle A cannot choose which outcome occurs — only that it's random — so no controllable bit of information passes to B's side. Only when the two measurement records are later compared (via an ordinary, light-speed-limited channel) does the correlation become verifiable. This distinction — instantaneous correlation without instantaneous *control* — is what separates entanglement from a science-fiction communicator, and it's the foundation for real applications like quantum key distribution and quantum teleportation protocols.

### quantum_cryptography (12.1s)

## Quantum Cryptography

Classical cryptography rests on computational hardness — RSA is secure only because factoring large integers is believed to be hard, not proven so. A sufficiently powerful computer (or a quantum computer running Shor's algorithm) could break it. Quantum cryptography replaces this assumption with a guarantee derived from physical law: the no-cloning theorem and the disturbance caused by quantum measurement.

**BB84**, the first quantum key distribution protocol, exploits *measurement disturbance*. Alice encodes each bit of a secret key in the polarization of a single photon, chosen randomly from two conjugate bases — say rectilinear ($\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$) and diagonal ($\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$). Bob measures each photon in a randomly chosen basis. If Eve intercepts a photon to read it, she must guess a basis; when she guesses wrong (50% of the time), her measurement collapses the state and introduces detectable errors when Alice and Bob later compare a public subset of their results. Any eavesdropping raises the error rate above the noise floor, exposing Eve's presence before the key is ever used.

**E91**, proposed by Artur Ekert, instead uses *entanglement correlations*. Alice and Bob share pairs of entangled photons in the state
$$
|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\big).
$$
Each measures their photon in one of several randomly chosen bases. Because the pair is entangled, their outcomes are correlated in a way no classical, "pre-agreed" local strategy can reproduce — a fact confirmed by testing Bell's inequality on a subset of the data. Any eavesdropping that disturbs the entanglement weakens these correlations, again exposing Eve.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose Alice sends 1,000 BB84 photons and Alice/Bob's bases match on average half the time, giving ~500 sifted key bits. If Eve intercept-resends every photon, she introduces errors on roughly 25% of the sifted bits (she guesses the right basis half the time, and even then may still disturb the state on measurement-basis mismatches). Comparing a sample of, say, 100 sifted bits publicly, Alice and Bob expect near-zero errors if the channel is secure; an observed error rate near 25% is a statistically overwhelming signal of eavesdropping, prompting them to discard the key and retry. This is the crucial distinction from classical cryptography: security here is a theorem of quantum mechanics — no-cloning and the collapse postulate — not a conjecture about computational difficulty, so a future quantum computer cannot retroactively break a key already agreed upon this way.
