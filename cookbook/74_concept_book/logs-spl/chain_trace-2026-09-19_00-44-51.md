## Electrical Current

Electrical current, $I$, is the rate at which electric charge flows past a point in a conductor. Formally,

$$
I = \frac{dQ}{dt}
$$

where $Q$ is charge measured in coulombs and $t$ is time in seconds. The SI unit of current is the ampere (A), defined as one coulomb per second. When charge flows at a constant rate over an interval, this simplifies to $I = \Delta Q / \Delta t$. Conventional current is defined as flowing in the direction that positive charge would move, even in metallic conductors where the actual charge carriers are negative electrons drifting the opposite way — a historical convention that remains standard in circuit analysis.

**Worked example.** Suppose a copper wire carries a steady current of 2 A. How much charge passes through a cross-section of the wire in 5 minutes? Convert time to seconds: $t = 300\ \text{s}$. Since $I = Q/t$ for constant current, rearrange to solve for charge:

$$
Q = I \cdot t = (2\ \text{A})(300\ \text{s}) = 600\ \text{C}
$$

This also tells us the number of electrons involved: dividing 600 C by the elementary charge $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ gives approximately $3.75 \times 10^{21}$ electrons — an enormous number, which is why current feels continuous rather than discrete at the macroscopic scale.

**Problem-solving application.** Current is rarely constant in real circuits, so the definition $I = dQ/dt$ becomes essential when charge flow varies with time, such as in AC circuits or during capacitor charging. For instance, if the charge on a capacitor is given by $Q(t) = Q_0(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ during charging, the current at any instant is found by differentiating:

$$
I(t) = \frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{Q_0}{\tau} e^{-t/\tau}
$$

This shows current is largest at $t = 0$ and decays exponentially as the capacitor approaches full charge — a result you cannot obtain from the simple ratio form and that directly explains why charging current drops off over time in RC circuits.
