## Matrix Multiplication

Matrix multiplication is a fundamental arithmetic operation that combines two matrices, $A$ and $B$, to produce a third matrix, $C$. If $A$ has dimensions $m \times n$ (meaning $m$ rows and $n$ columns) and $B$ has dimensions $p \times q$, the product $C = A B$ is only defined if the number of columns in $A$ equals the number of rows in $B$; that is, we must have $n=p$. The resulting matrix $C$ will then have dimensions $m \times q$.

The core operation involves calculating the dot product. Each element $c_{ij}$ in the resulting matrix $C$ is the dot product of the $i$-th row of $A$ and the $j$-th column of $B$. Mathematically, this is expressed as:
$$ c_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj} $$

Consider matrices $A$, $B$, and their product $C$:
If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$, both are $2 \times 2$. The resulting matrix $C$ will also be $2 \times 2$.

To find the element $c_{11}$ (row 1 of $A$, column 1 of $B$), we compute:
$$ c_{11} = (1)(5) + (2)(7) = 5 + 14 = 19 $$

Similarly, for $c_{12}$:
$$ c_{12} = (1)(6) + (2)(8) = 6 + 16 = 22 $$
This process is repeated for all four elements, yielding:
$$ C = A B = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix} $$

The critical application of matrix multiplication lies in its role as a representation of linear transformations. If $A$ is an $m \times n$ transformation matrix, multiplying $A$ by an $n$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{x}$ (which can be viewed as an $n \times 1$ matrix) transforms $\mathbf{x}$ into a new $m$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{y}$. This structure is essential in fields ranging from computer graphics to machine learning, where the transformation of data points through successive linear operations is modeled entirely by sequential matrix multiplications.

For instance, if you are applying a geometric rotation defined by an orthogonal matrix $R$ (e.g., rotating coordinates 90 degrees clockwise), and $\mathbf{v}$ is your vector, the new coordinates $\mathbf{v}'$ are calculated as $\mathbf{v}' = R \mathbf{v}$. The rigor of this operation ensures that the transformation preserves linear relationships in the coordinate space, making matrix multiplication the cornerstone of multivariable linear algebra.
