### limit (0.2s)

## Limit

A **limit** describes the value a function approaches as its input gets arbitrarily close to some point — not necessarily the value *at* that point. Formally, we write

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

and mean: for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. In plain terms: we can make $f(x)$ as close to $L$ as we like by keeping $x$ close enough to $a$. The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition is not just bookkeeping — it is what allows limits to exist at points where the function is undefined or discontinuous, which is exactly the situation that makes limits useful.

**Worked example.** Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$$

At $x = 1$, the denominator is zero, so $f(1)$ is undefined. But we can factor:

$$\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x + 1, \quad x \neq 1.$$

As $x \to 1$, $f(x)$ approaches $1 + 1 = 2$. The limit exists and equals 2 even though the function has a hole at that point. The graph would look like the line $y = x + 1$ with a single missing dot at $(1, 2)$.

**Problem-solving application.** Limits are the engine behind derivatives and integrals. When you compute a derivative,

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$

you are asking what the difference quotient *approaches* as $h$ shrinks, not what it equals at $h = 0$ (where it is $\tfrac{0}{0}$). The practical move is always the same: simplify algebraically to cancel the offending factor, then evaluate. For instance, $f(x) = x^2$ gives

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(2a + h) = 2a.$$

When algebra alone does not cancel the singularity, tools like L'Hôpital's rule or Taylor series take over. The key habit: before reaching for those tools, always try factoring or rationalizing — most limit problems at this level collapse with one algebraic step.

### continuity_at_a_point (17.5s)

## Continuity At A Point

A function $f$ is **continuous at a point** $x = a$ when three conditions all hold simultaneously: the function is defined at $a$, the limit of $f(x)$ as $x \to a$ exists, and that limit equals the function's value. Formally:

$$f \text{ is continuous at } a \iff \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$$

Intuitively, continuity at a point means you can draw through that point without lifting your pen — no holes, no jumps, no vertical blowup exactly at $a$.

**Worked Example**

Consider $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$. Is $f$ continuous at $x = 2$?

Check each condition:

1. **Defined?** Substituting $x = 2$ gives $\frac{0}{0}$, so $f(2)$ is undefined. The first condition fails immediately.

$f$ is *not* continuous at $x = 2$ — it has a removable discontinuity there. Notice, however, that $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 2}(x + 2) = 4$ exists. The discontinuity is removable because defining $f(2) = 4$ would restore all three conditions.

Now consider $g(x) = x^2 + 3$ at $x = 2$.

1. Defined: $g(2) = 7$. ✓  
2. Limit exists: $\lim_{x \to 2}(x^2 + 3) = 7$. ✓  
3. Limit equals value: $7 = 7$. ✓  

$g$ is continuous at $x = 2$.

**Problem-Solving Application**

When a problem asks you to find constants that make a piecewise function continuous at a junction point, apply all three conditions as a system. Suppose

$$h(x) = \begin{cases} ax + 1 & x < 3 \\ x^2 - b & x \geq 3 \end{cases}$$

and you need continuity at $x = 3$. The left-hand limit is $3a + 1$, the right-hand limit (and the value, since $x = 3$ falls in the second piece) is $9 - b$. Setting them equal:

$$3a + 1 = 9 - b \implies 3a + b = 8$$

This single equation gives a family of solutions; a second constraint (such as $h(3) = 7$) pins down both constants. The strategy generalizes: **translate "continuous at $a$" into an equation by equating left-hand limit, right-hand limit, and function value**, then solve algebraically.

This pattern appears throughout calculus — in the intermediate value theorem, differentiability arguments, and integral bounds — so internalizing the three-condition checklist pays dividends far beyond this definition alone.

### continuity_over_an_interval (17.9s)

## Continuity Over An Interval

A function can be continuous at a single point without being well-behaved everywhere. The more useful idea is **continuity over an interval** — a guarantee that a function has no breaks, jumps, or holes across an entire stretch of its domain.

**Definition.** A function $f$ is continuous on an **open interval** $(a, b)$ if it is continuous at every point $c \in (a, b)$, meaning

$$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$$

for each such $c$. For a **closed interval** $[a, b]$, two extra conditions are added at the endpoints: $f$ must be **right-continuous** at $a$ (i.e., $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)$) and **left-continuous** at $b$ (i.e., $\lim_{x \to b^-} f(x) = f(b)$). This prevents the function from "flying off" at either boundary.

**Why it matters.** Continuity on a closed interval unlocks two major theorems used constantly in calculus and analysis:

- **Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT):** If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $k$ is any value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = k$.
- **Extreme Value Theorem (EVT):** If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, it must attain both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on that interval.

**Worked example.** Is $f(x) = \sqrt{4 - x^2}$ continuous on $[-2, 2]$?

On the open interior $(-2, 2)$, the expression under the radical is strictly positive, so $f$ is defined and continuous everywhere there. At $x = -2$: $\lim_{x \to -2^+} \sqrt{4 - x^2} = 0 = f(-2)$ ✓. At $x = 2$: $\lim_{x \to 2^-} \sqrt{4-x^2} = 0 = f(2)$ ✓. So $f$ is continuous on $[-2, 2]$.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose you know $g(0) = -3$ and $g(5) = 7$, and $g$ is continuous on $[0, 5]$. Does $g$ ever equal zero? By the IVT, yes — since $0$ lies between $-3$ and $7$, there must be some $c \in (0, 5)$ where $g(c) = 0$. This is the foundation of root-finding algorithms like bisection search: the IVT tells you a root exists; repeated halving of the interval narrows down where it is. Without confirmed continuity on the full interval, neither conclusion holds.

### intermediate_value_theorem (18.9s)

## Intermediate Value Theorem

A continuous function cannot jump. If you draw its graph without lifting your pen, it must pass through every height between its starting value and its ending value. That intuition is the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT), one of the foundational guarantees of calculus.

**Theorem (Intermediate Value Theorem).** Let $f$ be continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$. If $k$ is any real number satisfying $f(a) \leq k \leq f(b)$ (or $f(b) \leq k \leq f(a)$), then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = k$.

The proof relies on the completeness of the real numbers — the idea that there are no "gaps" on the number line — but the theorem's power lies in what you can do with it, not the proof machinery.

**Worked example.** Show that $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ has a root in $(1, 2)$.

Compute the endpoints:
$$f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1 < 0$$
$$f(2) = 8 - 2 - 1 = 5 > 0$$

Since $f$ is a polynomial (continuous everywhere) and $0$ lies between $f(1) = -1$ and $f(2) = 5$, the IVT guarantees a $c \in (1, 2)$ with $f(c) = 0$. The root exists — even though we haven't found its exact value.

**Problem-solving application.** The IVT is the theoretical backbone of root-finding algorithms like bisection search. The strategy: if $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, a root is trapped in $[a, b]$. Evaluate the midpoint $m = \frac{a+b}{2}$, then check which half still contains the sign change, and repeat. Each iteration halves the interval, so after $n$ steps the root is located to within $\frac{b-a}{2^n}$.

A key caution: the theorem requires continuity. The function $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ satisfies $f(-1) = -1$ and $f(1) = 1$, yet it never equals $0$ — because it has a discontinuity at $x = 0$, the IVT's hypothesis fails.

When applying IVT on an exam or in a proof, always verify three things: (1) $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, (2) the target value $k$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, and (3) your conclusion states existence, not uniqueness — the IVT guarantees at least one $c$, but there may be more.
