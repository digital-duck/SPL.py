## Uk 1846 Repeal Corn Laws

The Corn Laws represented a classic example of protectionism, where tariffs (or taxes) were placed on imported agricultural goods to shield domestic producers from foreign competition. Economically, these laws artificially restricted market equilibrium by elevating the cost of living through government-mandated barriers. The repeal in 1846 marked Britain’s decisive shift toward free trade principles, allowing grain prices to adjust freely based solely on global supply and demand dynamics. Understanding this transition requires analyzing how tariffs affect market efficiency using standard microeconomic models.

Consider a linear model for the grain market where $P$ is price and $Q$ is quantity. The domestic supply curve ($S$) represents the cost of production, while the global demand curve ($D$) reflects consumer willingness to pay. Before the repeal, the Corn Laws imposed a tariff, $T$, which effectively increased the minimum price required for imports, shifting the market equilibrium away from its natural state $(P_E, Q_E)$.

To model this mathematically, the imposition of a specific tariff $T$ on imported goods causes the effective supply curve to shift upward or leftward. The initial equilibrium occurs where $S(Q) = D(Q)$, yielding optimal quantity $Q_E$ and price $P_E$. When the government imposes a tariff, the new effective transaction cost forces the market into an inefficient equilibrium $(P_{Tar}, Q_{Tar})$, where the resulting welfare loss is measured by the area of the triangle formed between the supply/demand curves at the constrained quantity.

A worked example demonstrates the efficiency gains from repeal. If initial prices were $P_{Tar}$ and the optimal price was $P_E$, the removal of the law allows consumption to increase from $Q_{Tar}$ to $Q_E$. The total welfare gain ($\Delta W$) is precisely equal to the area of the triangle defined by the tariff barrier:

$$ \Delta W = \frac{1}{2} \times (P_{Tar} - P_E) \times (Q_E - Q_{Tar}) $$
