### 木 (8.8s)

## 木

You already know 木 as a picture: a trunk with branches spreading out and roots settling below. It's one of the oldest and simplest characters in the writing system, and that simplicity is exactly why it's so useful — 木 doesn't just stand alone as "tree" or "wood." It shows up inside other characters, doing two very different jobs depending on where it sits.

Sometimes 木 brings its *meaning*. Look at 林, which is just 木 written twice:

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = 林 \;\; \text{(woods, grove)}$$

Two trees together naturally suggest a small forest — the meaning of the parts builds the meaning of the whole. The same thing happens in 休 (to rest), where 木 pairs with 人 (a person) to picture someone leaning against a tree.

But 木 can also show up for its *sound* instead of its meaning. In 沐 (to wash, to bathe), the water radical 氵 carries the meaning, while 木 is borrowed just because it sounds close to the word for "wash." The tree has nothing to do with washing — it's lending its pronunciation, not its picture.

So the rule to remember: a component like 木 doesn't always play the same role twice. Ask yourself, in each character, "is this piece here for what it *means*, or for how it *sounds*?"

**Practice:** 林 and 沐 both contain 木. Which one uses 木 for meaning, and which one uses it for sound? Explain your reasoning using each character's other component.

### 女 (8.1s)

## 女

You already know 女 as "woman" — but look closer at its shape, and you'll see it's actually a picture. The character began as a drawing of a kneeling figure, arms crossed or folded in front of the body. Ancient scribes weren't writing an abstract idea; they were sketching a person in a specific pose, one you'd recognize from old artwork showing seated or kneeling figures at rest.

That pictorial root is why 女 isn't just a standalone word — it's a building block. In Chinese writing, characters that share a meaning often share a piece, called a radical, and 女 is one of the busiest radicals in the whole system. Any character connected to women, family relationships, or certain qualities tends to carry 女 somewhere in its structure. For example:

$$\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = \underbrace{好}_{good}$$

A woman beside a child forms 好, meaning "good" — an old idea that having a child nearby was something favorable. That's the pattern to watch for: 女 rarely appears alone in a compound; it's usually paired with another piece to create a new meaning that builds on "woman" as its semantic anchor.

Once you notice this, you'll start spotting 女 everywhere — in characters for "mother," "sister," "marriage," "peace," and dozens more. It's less a single word than a signal flag: whenever you see it embedded in a character, you can guess the meaning has something to do with women, family, or relationships.

**Practice:** Find two other characters that contain 女 as a component (you can search online or in a dictionary). Write each one down and guess how 女 might connect to its meaning before checking.

### 人 (8.3s)

## 人

You've likely already met 人 as "the little walking figure" — two legs, one stride, meaning *person*. That intuition is correct, and it's also the reason 人 matters more than almost any other character in the writing system: it doesn't just mean "person" on its own, it shows up *inside* hundreds of other characters as a building block.

When 人 stands alone, it's a full word. But when it becomes part of another character, it usually shrinks and shifts to the left side, turning into the compact form 亻— you'll see this called a "radical." The meaning stays the same (something about a person or human action), but the shape adapts to make room for whatever sits beside it.

$$
\underbrace{亻}_{\text{person (radical form)}} + \underbrace{尔}_{\text{sound: er}} = \underbrace{你}_{\text{you}}
$$

Here, 亻 tells you the character has something to do with a person, while 尔 mostly just contributes the pronunciation. This is a common pattern: one piece carries meaning, the other carries sound. Once you notice 亻 in a character, you can guess it likely involves a human action, role, or relationship — words like 他 (he), 们 (plural marker for people), and 做 (to do/make) all lean on this same piece.

**Practice:** Look up the characters 休 and 信. Both contain 亻. Based on what you know about 人/亻 as a "person" clue, write one sentence guessing what each character might relate to before checking a dictionary.

### 子 (7.4s)

## 子

You already know 子 as a picture of a swaddled baby — look closely and you can still see the wrapped bundle and the little arms poking out. Originally it just meant "child," but over thousands of years its job grew far beyond that.

Today 子 does double duty. On its own, it still means child or son — as in 儿子 (son) or 孩子 (child). But far more often, you'll find it quietly riding along at the end of other words, where it doesn't mean "child" at all. Instead, it acts like a grammatical suffix, turning a root idea into a solid, nameable noun. Think of it as a small tag that says "this is a thing."

$$\underbrace{桌}_{table (root)} + \underbrace{子}_{noun suffix} = 桌子 \text{ (table)}$$

The same pattern shows up again and again:

$$\underbrace{椅}_{chair (root)} + \underbrace{子}_{noun suffix} = 椅子 \text{ (chair)}$$

$$\underbrace{儿}_{son} + \underbrace{子}_{noun suffix} = 儿子 \text{ (son)}$$

Notice the pattern: in each case, the first character carries the real meaning, and 子 just confirms "yes, this is a noun, an object you can point to." It's a bit like adding "-er" in English to turn "teach" into "teacher" — except 子 doesn't add meaning so much as give a word its final, complete shape.

**Practice:** The character 猴 (hóu) means "monkey." Following the pattern above, what word would 猴子 form, and what job is 子 doing in it?

### 宀 (14.3s)

## 宀

Think of a tent pitched over a small space — that's the picture behind 宀. It's a roof radical, drawn like a peaked cover with two short strokes hanging down the sides, as if walls were dropping from the eaves. On its own, 宀 isn't a word you'd ever say out loud. It's a *component* — a building block that shows up inside other characters to signal one thing: something or someone is now under cover, protected, contained, or settled in place.

What's interesting is how much emotional weight a roof can carry once you look at what sits beneath it. A pig under a roof (豕) becomes 家, "home" — because in ancient farming life, a house wasn't complete without livestock sheltered inside it too. A woman under a roof (女) becomes 安, "peace" or "safety" — the idea that a household is calm when its members are gathered and secure.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{豕}_{\text{pig}} = 家 \;(\text{home})
$$

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = 安 \;(\text{peace, safety})
$$

The pattern: whenever you see 宀 sitting at the top of a character, ask "what's underneath, and why does it need shelter?" That question usually unlocks the character's meaning. Sometimes the thing sheltered is physical, like a pig or a person; sometimes it's more abstract, like an idea being protected and given form.

**Practice:** The character 字 (meaning "character" or "written word") is built from 宀 over 子 ("child"). Using what you just learned, explain in one sentence why a roof over a child might come to mean "a written word."

### 马 (13.2s)

## 马

You already know 马 as the character for "horse" — you might even picture its old pictograph roots, a horse's mane and legs flattened into strokes. But 马 has a second job in the writing system, one that has nothing to do with animals at all: it's a **sound-lender**. In certain characters, 马 shows up not because the meaning involves a horse, but because it tells you how to pronounce the character — "ma."

Look at 妈 (mother). It pairs 马 with a meaning-giving radical, 女 (woman), which tells you the meaning is female/family-related. 马 itself contributes nothing about meaning here — it just supplies the sound "ma."

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: ma}} = 妈 \ (\text{mother})
$$

This is the same "meaning + sound" pattern you may have already noticed in other characters — one piece carries the category of meaning, the other piece carries the pronunciation. When you spot 马 inside an unfamiliar character, don't assume horses are involved — check whether it's paired with a meaning radical from some other category (like 女, or 口 for mouth/speech). If it is, 马 is almost certainly just lending its sound, the same way it does in 妈.

**Practice:** The character 吗 (a question particle used at the end of sentences) pairs 马 with 口 (mouth). Based on what you just learned about 马's two possible jobs, explain why 吗 is pronounced "ma" and why 口 makes sense as its meaning radical.

### 从 (9.0s)

## 从

Picture two people on a path — one in front, one just behind, matching every step. That's 从 (cóng), "to follow." The character isn't a symbol standing in for the idea; it's a small drawing of it. Look closely and you'll see one 人 (person) walking, and a second 人 right behind, trailing the first like a shadow.

$$\underbrace{人}_{person\ walking} + \underbrace{人}_{person\ following} = 从 \text{ (follow)}$$

This is what makes 从 worth pausing on: it's built from a doubled idea, not a combined one. Many characters pair two *different* pieces — one hinting at meaning, one at sound. But 从 repeats the same piece twice, and the repetition itself creates the new meaning. One person alone is just a person. Two people, one positioned behind the other, becomes an action — the act of following.

You'll find this trick elsewhere in Chinese: doubling a person (人人) suggests "everyone," and other repeated-radical characters lean on the same logic — sameness or multiplication of a unit builds a new concept. 从 is one of the clearest, oldest examples, because the doubling isn't abstract — it's staged like a tiny scene, almost a comic panel of two figures on a road.

Today 从 shows up constantly: 从来 (cónglái, "all along"), 从此 (cóngcǐ, "from this point on"), 服从 (fúcóng, "to obey" — literally to yield and follow). In every case, the core picture — someone trailing someone else — still quietly powers the meaning.

**Practice:** Draw two simple figures, one behind the other, to represent 从. Then write one sentence (in English) describing a real-life situation where "following" fits — literally or figuratively — and note which figure in your drawing represents you.

### 口 (14.0s)

## 口

Look at the character 口. It's just a box — four strokes forming a square. That simple shape is one of the oldest and busiest pieces in the Chinese writing system: a picture of an open mouth, seen straight on.

But 口 isn't only a mouth. Over thousands of years, it picked up three overlapping jobs, and telling them apart is the real skill here.

First, it can *be* the word for mouth itself — the body part you eat and speak with.

Second, it shows up as a building block inside other characters, quietly contributing meaning. When 口 sits next to another piece, it often signals "something to do with the mouth" — speaking, eating, calling out, or an opening of some kind.

$$\underbrace{口}_{mouth} + \underbrace{未}_{sound: wei} = \underbrace{味}_{taste}$$

Here 口 supplies the meaning (you taste with your mouth), while 未 lends its sound. This pattern — one piece for meaning, one for sound — appears in hundreds of characters, and 口 is one of the most common "meaning" partners.

Third, 口 works as a measure word, counting people or mouthfuls: 一口人 ("one member of the family," literally "one mouth of person") or 一口水 ("a mouthful of water").

**Simple rule:** when you see 口 attached to a character, ask: is it giving *sound* or *meaning*? If the character relates to speaking, eating, or openings, 口 is likely doing meaning-work.

### 日 (7.7s)

## 日

You already know 日 as "sun" — maybe you've seen it in words like 日本 (Japan, "sun origin") or 明日 (tomorrow). But look closer at the character itself, and it tells its own small story about how Chinese writing turns pictures into symbols.

Thousands of years ago, 日 was drawn as a circle with a dot in the middle — a simple sketch of the sun, glowing. Over centuries, as scribes wrote faster with brushes on bamboo strips and paper, round shapes got harder to make quickly. Circles became squares, and the dot stretched into a short horizontal line. The sun didn't change — but the *tool* did, and the character followed the tool.

$$
\underbrace{\bigcirc}_{\text{circle: the sun's shape}} + \underbrace{\cdot}_{\text{dot: its light}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{日}_{\text{squared by the brush}}
$$

This matters because 日 isn't just a word — it's a *building block*. Many characters about time, brightness, or weather contain 日 as a piece, because they all trace back to the sun somehow. Once you can spot 日 inside a more complicated character, you already have a clue about its meaning before you even know how to read it.

**Simple rule**: When you see 日 tucked inside a character, ask "does this word have something to do with light, time, or the sky?" It often does.

**Practice**: Look up the characters 明 and 星. Can you find 日 hiding inside each one? Write down what each character means, and guess how 日's presence might connect to that meaning.

### 月 (7.6s)

## 月

You already know 月 as the moon — that curved, tilted shape is a picture of a crescent hanging in the sky. It's one of the oldest symbols in the writing system, and once you start looking for it, you'll find it everywhere: not just in sky words, but tucked inside characters about the human body.

Here's the twist: when 月 sits on the left side of a character, it's usually not talking about the moon at all. It's a worn-down version of 肉 (ròu, "meat" or "flesh"), used as a radical to mark body parts and organs. Over centuries of writing, the character for flesh got simplified until it looked exactly like the character for moon — same shape, two completely different family trees.

You can see this pattern at work:

$$\underbrace{月}_{\text{flesh radical}} + \underbrace{半}_{\text{sound: bàn}} = 胖 \; \text{(pàng, "fat")}$$

$$\underbrace{月}_{\text{flesh radical}} + \underbrace{土}_{\text{clay, mound}} = 肚 \; \text{(dù, "belly")}$$

The simple rule: if 月 appears alone or clearly linked to sky, weather, or time, it means "moon." If it appears squeezed into the left slot of a character whose meaning involves the body — organs, flesh, physical states — it's quietly standing in for 肉.

**Practice problem:** The character 脑 (nǎo, "brain") contains 月 on the left. Based on what you just learned, is this 月 acting as "moon" or as the flesh radical? Explain your reasoning in one sentence.

### 林 (7.7s)

## 林

You already know 木 means "tree." Now picture two of them standing side by side, sharing the same patch of ground. That's 林 (lín) — "woods" or "grove." No new radical to memorize, no fresh idea to master. Just tree, tree.

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林 \text{ (woods)}$$

This is the simplest kind of character-building move in Chinese: doubling. One tree is just a tree. Two trees together become something new — not "two trees" exactly, but a *place* where trees grow, a small wooded area. The meaning shifts from naming a single object to describing a cluster, a habitat, a collection. That's the pattern worth noticing: in Chinese, repeating a character often doesn't just mean "more of the same thing" — it can create a new concept built from the *relationship* between the repeated pieces.

Keep this rule in your pocket, because it's not unique to 林. Chinese does this doubling trick elsewhere too — repetition to build intensity, plurality, or a new category entirely. Once you spot the pattern in 林, you'll start noticing it in other characters, and you'll be able to guess at meanings before you ever look them up.

### 水 (10.0s)

## 水

You already know 水 as water — picture a stream twisting between rocks, its current splitting and rejoining. The character itself grew out of that picture: an old pictograph of a central channel with droplets splashing off either side, later straightened into the strokes you write today.

Look closer at its structure, though, and 水 does something more interesting: it doesn't just stay whole, it transforms when it moves into a supporting role. When 水 sits to the left of another character, it shrinks into a compressed form, 氵, three short strokes standing in for the full character. This shorthand is called a "radical" — a piece that signals meaning without needing to carry the whole word by itself.

$$
\underbrace{水}_{\text{water, standalone}} \xrightarrow{\text{compress}} \underbrace{氵}_{\text{water radical}}
$$

The rule is simple: whenever you see 氵 on the left side of a character, expect the meaning to involve liquid — rivers, oceans, tears, sweat, even abstract "flow" like in 法 (law, originally tied to water finding a level course) or 洗 (to wash). The radical acts like a label glued onto the character's meaning, even though the sound usually comes from the other half.

### 休 (8.8s)

## 休

You've probably already noticed that 休 looks like a little scene: a person standing next to a tree. That picture *is* the meaning — 休 (xiū) means "to rest," and the character shows you exactly what resting looked like to the people who invented it: someone pausing their walk to lean against a tree trunk in the shade.

What's worth noticing now is *how* this picture got built. Chinese characters like this one are often made by snapping two simpler pictures together, and the new meaning comes from imagining the scene they create side by side. Here, the two pieces are 人 (person) and 木 (tree):

$$
\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 休 \;(\text{rest})
$$

Notice the pieces don't just sit next to each other randomly — 人 is drawn leaning into 木, almost like a stick figure propped against a trunk. That's the pattern to look for: in these combined characters, the position and posture of the pieces often hints at the story, not just their meanings alone.

This is a useful rule to carry forward: whenever you meet an unfamiliar character made of two familiar ones, try picturing them interacting rather than just adding their meanings like a math problem. Ask "what scene would these two objects make together?" Often the answer is the character's meaning.

### 众 (13.4s)

## 众

You already know that stacking three of the same character multiplies the meaning of the original into something bigger. You saw it with 森, where three 木 (tree) pile up into "forest." Now the same trick shows up with people.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = 众 \; (\text{crowd})
$$

One 人 is just a person, walking alone. Two people together might just be a pair, a friendship, a conversation. But three — arranged one on top and two beneath, like a small triangle of bodies — stops being "a few individuals" and becomes something else entirely: a group with its own identity. That's the tripling rule at work again: Chinese characters often treat "three" as shorthand for "many," turning a single unit into a mass, a multitude, a whole population.

Notice the layout, too. In 森, the three 木 sit side by side and stacked, evenly balanced, because trees don't need a leader. But 众 often gets written with one 人 on top and two below — a small hint that a crowd isn't just individuals standing around, but people arranged, maybe even watching or gathering around something (or someone) at the center.

The pattern to remember: three of a meaningful character = "many" of that thing, and the arrangement can tell you something about *how* they relate to each other.

**Practice:** Now try the pattern on a character built the same way, from a piece you already know: 品 is built from three 口 (mouth). Based on the tripling rule, what do you think 品 might mean? Write one sentence explaining your guess before checking.

### 吗 (16.8s)

## 吗

You already know that adding 吗 to the end of a sentence turns a statement into a yes-or-no question — 你好 ("you're good") becomes 你好吗? ("are you good?"). Now let's look at why this one small character does that job, and how its own parts hint at its function.

吗 is built from two pieces stacked side by side:

$$\underbrace{口}_{\text{mouth — spoken sound}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{horse — sound "ma"}} = 吗 \text{ (question particle "ma")}$$

The 口 radical is a signal, not a picture of meaning. It tells you: this character is mostly about *sound*, not about anything to do with mouths or speaking literally. 马 supplies that sound — it's borrowed purely because it's pronounced "mǎ," close enough to become the light, neutral "ma" tacked onto questions.

This is the core trick behind thousands of Chinese characters: a sound-piece plus a meaning-hint. The meaning-hint is called a radical, and it tells you which "world" a character belongs to — 口 points to sound or speech, for instance. Swap out the radical on 马 and you get a totally different character with a totally different meaning, even though the sound stays the same. Once you can spot this sound-plus-radical pattern, you can start guessing at unfamiliar characters instead of memorizing each one cold.

### 好 (13.3s)

## 好

You already know 好 (hǎo) means "good" — but look closely at what the character actually shows: a woman, 女, standing right next to a child, 子. That's it. That simple pairing became one of the most common characters in the entire Chinese writing system.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 好 \text{ (good)}$$

Why would ancient scribes picture "good" this way? Chinese characters that combine two whole pictures like this are called compound ideographs — the meaning comes from the *relationship* between the pieces, not just what each piece shows alone. A woman and a child together suggested family, care, continuity — the basic things a society needs to thrive. Three thousand years ago, that image of togetherness *was* the concept of goodness. The character hasn't changed its logic since.

This is a useful pattern to watch for: when a character shows two people or objects placed side by side, ask what relationship between them the scene is describing. 好 isn't "woman" plus "child" as separate ideas — it's the *scene* of them together that carries the meaning. Once you start noticing 女 in one character, you'll start spotting it in others too — it shows up again and again as a building block across the writing system.

### 妈 (7.4s)

## 妈

You already know 妈 (mā) means "mother." Now let's look at how the character itself tells you that.

Chinese characters are often built from two kinds of pieces: one piece hints at the *meaning*, and another piece hints at the *sound*. 妈 is a clean example of this pattern. On the left is 女, the character for "woman" or "female" — it shows up in lots of characters related to women (姐 "older sister," 好 "good," and others). On the right is 马, which means "horse" — but here, it isn't doing anything horse-related at all. It's borrowed purely for its sound, *mǎ*, to give 妈 its pronunciation, *mā*.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{female person}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sounds like } ma} = \underbrace{妈}_{\text{mother}}$$

This is one of the most common patterns in Chinese writing, called a *phono-semantic* character: one part carries meaning, the other part carries sound. Once you notice it in 妈, you'll start spotting it everywhere — many characters with 女 on the left are about women, and many characters that sound like "ma," "ba," or other syllables borrow a sound-piece from somewhere else, unrelated to its original meaning.

**Simple rule:** meaning-piece + sound-piece = new character. The meaning-piece narrows the category; the sound-piece tells you how to say it.

### 字 (13.6s)

## 字

You already know 字 means "character" — but look closer, and this one character is telling you exactly what it is. Pull it apart, and you find a roof over a child.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字 \text{ (character)}$$

Why a child under a roof? Think about what a roof does: it's a place where something is sheltered, fed, and allowed to grow. Ancient scribes weren't just doodling — they were describing how new characters actually come into the world. A brand-new character isn't invented out of nothing. It's "born" from pieces that already exist, then "raised" — combined, adjusted, given a home — inside the writing system, the way a family raises a child inside a house. That's the whole idea packed into one picture.

This is also why 字 gives its name to *ZiNets* — networks of 字. If every character is a child with parents (the simpler pieces it's built from), then the entire writing system is really a giant family tree. Trace any complex character back far enough, and you'll find the same small set of ancestor pieces showing up again and again, each one having "raised" dozens of descendants.

**The pattern to remember:** when you see a character built from smaller parts, don't just memorize the combination — ask whose child it is. What older pieces gave birth to it, and what meaning did they pass down?

### 安 (13.7s)

## 安

You already know 安 as peace — the feeling in 平安 (safety and well-being) and 安全 (security). But look closer at the character itself, and you'll find that peace, in Chinese, has a very specific picture built into it.

The character is made of two parts: 宀, a roof, and 女, a woman. Together, they show a woman sheltered under a roof — settled, protected, at home.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = \underbrace{安}_{\text{peace, settled}}$$

Why this image, specifically? In the world where this character took shape, a home with someone safely inside it was the basic unit of a peaceful life. No danger outside, no threat inside — just a person, sheltered, undisturbed. That's what "peace" meant at the most concrete level, before it ever became an abstract idea like "world peace" or "peace of mind." The character keeps that original picture frozen inside it, even today.

This is a pattern worth noticing: many Chinese characters build abstract ideas out of everyday scenes. A roof plus a person gives you shelter; shelter becomes safety; safety becomes the broader idea of peace. Once you see this move — concrete scene → abstract meaning — you'll start noticing it in other characters too.

**Practice:** The character 字 (meaning "character" or "word," as in 汉字) is also built from 宀 (roof) on top of 子 (child) — the same roof you just learned in 安. ### 明 (9.8s)

## 明

You already know that 明 (míng) means "bright," and that it's built from two characters you recognize on sight: 日 (sun) and 月 (moon). But it's worth pausing on *why* this pairing works so well, instead of just memorizing it.

Think about what sun and moon have in common: they are the two sources of light in the sky. During the day, the sun lights the world; at night, the moon does. A character-maker thousands of years ago needed a way to say "bright" or "shining," and instead of inventing a brand-new symbol, they reached for the two things everyone already associated with light and put them side by side. The character doesn't just mean "sunlight" or "moonlight" — it means brightness in general, drawn from combining both.

$$
\underbrace{日}_{sun} + \underbrace{月}_{moon} = \underbrace{明}_{bright}
$$

This is a common strategy in Chinese character-building: take two things that share a quality, and stack them to represent that quality itself. Once you notice this pattern, you'll start recognizing it elsewhere — characters built by combining two ideas that overlap in meaning, rather than one part giving sound and the other giving meaning.

It also helps explain why 明 shows up in words like 明白 (míngbai, "to understand clearly") or 明天 (míngtiān, "tomorrow," literally "the next bright day"). Brightness, clarity, and understanding are cousins in this character's logic.

**Practice:** Look up the character 岩 (yán, "rock"). What two smaller characters is it composed of, and how does their combination hint at the meaning "rock"? Write it out as a decomposition equation like the one above.

### 森 (14.5s)

## 森

You already know 木 is a single tree, and 林 doubles it into a grove. Now 森 takes the pattern one step further, adding a third tree to build the character for *forest* (sēn) — a place with so many trees that it feels endless, layered, and a little mysterious.

Look at how the three characters stack meaning as they stack trees:

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} \xrightarrow{+木} \underbrace{林}_{grove} \xrightarrow{+木} \underbrace{森}_{forest}
$$

Notice the pattern isn't random — it's a *gradient*. One tree is a single object. Two trees form a small cluster, close enough that you'd naturally see them together. Three trees pushes past "a few" into "many, everywhere you look" — the brick count itself carries the meaning upward, like turning up a dial. This is different from most radical combinations, where adding a piece changes the *category* of meaning (like adding water 氵 to get a word about rivers). Here, adding the same piece again just changes the *intensity* of the same idea: more trees, denser and wilder.

This tripling trick isn't unique to trees. Once you know it, you can look for it anywhere the same small piece repeats three times in a row — the meaning almost always climbs from "one" to "a few" to "so many it's everywhere." That's a fast way to guess at a new character's meaning before you even look it up.

**Practice:** Using the same logic as 木→林→森, what do you think three 火 (fire) stacked together might mean? Write down your guess and the reasoning behind it before checking a dictionary.

### 沐 (8.2s)

## 沐

You already know that 木 (mù) means "tree." But in 沐, something different happens. The water radical 氵 sits on the left, and 木 sits on the right — yet the word doesn't mean "watery tree" or anything close to it. It means **to wash**, specifically to wash one's hair or bathe. So what is 木 doing there at all?

This is the moment to notice a shift. In every character you've studied so far, each piece contributed a piece of the *picture* — a hand, a sun, a tree, something you could see. In 沐, 木 isn't drawing a picture anymore. It's lending a *sound*. 木 is pronounced "mù," and so is 沐. The water radical 氵 tells you the character has something to do with liquid — washing, bathing — while 木 simply tells you how to say it.

$$
\underbrace{氵}_{\text{meaning: water}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{sound: "mù"}} = \text{沐 (mù) — to wash}
$$

This is called a **sound-meaning** character, and it's actually the most common type of Chinese character by far — over 80% of all characters work this way. One piece (called the *radical*) tells you the general category of meaning. The other piece (the *phonetic*) tells you how to pronounce it, often with no connection to meaning at all.

Once you notice this pattern, you'll start finding it everywhere.

**Practice:** The character 沙 (shā, "sand") also uses 氵on the left. Based on what you just learned, what job do you think the right-hand piece is doing — giving meaning, or giving sound? Look up its pronunciation to check your guess.

### phonetic_borrowing (15.8s)

## Phonetic Borrowing

You already know that some pieces of a Chinese character carry meaning — they're called radicals. But characters need sound too, and that's where phonetic borrowing comes in. A character can lend its *pronunciation* to build a brand-new character, even if the meaning has nothing to do with the original.

Take 马 (mǎ, "horse"). Add the mouth radical 口 and you get 妈 (mā, "mother") — same sound, tone shifted slightly, totally different meaning. 口 isn't contributing "mouth" here at all; it's just marking that this is a new word, while 马 quietly supplies the sound.

Add 口 to 马 again, but combine them a different way, and you get 吗 (ma), the little question-word tacked onto the end of sentences. Same two bricks, two different jobs: one time they build a family word, another time they build a grammar tool — and in both cases 马 is the one doing sound duty.

$$\underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: mǎ}} + \underbrace{口}_{\text{marker}} = 妈 \text{ (mother)}$$
$$\underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: mǎ}} + \underbrace{口}_{\text{marker}} = 吗 \text{ (question particle)}$$

**The pattern:** when you see a familiar character tucked inside a new one, don't assume it's contributing meaning. Check whether it's just lending its sound while a different part carries the meaning or grammar job.

**Practice:** The character 青 (qīng) means "blue-green" and is pronounced *qīng*. It appears inside 情 (qíng, "feeling"), 清 (qīng, "clear"), and 请 (qǐng, "please"). For each one, identify which radical supplies the meaning, and explain what job 青 is doing.

### semantic_composition (18.1s)

## Semantic Composition

You already know that pictographs like 木 (tree) or 人 (person) draw a single thing. But what happens when a character needs to express an *idea* instead of an object? Ancient scribes found a clever trick: combine two or more meaning-pictures so their meanings blend into something new. This method is called 会意 (huì yì), literally "meeting of meanings."

Look at 休 (rest). It's built from a person leaning against a tree:

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \underbrace{休}_{rest}$$

A person resting by a tree — the image *is* the idea. The same logic explains why 好 (good) combines 女 (woman) with 子 (child):

$$\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = \underbrace{好}_{good}$$

A woman with a child suggests something cherished — "good."

Semantic composition also lets you stack the *same* picture to intensify meaning. Take the tree you already know, 木:

$$木 \xrightarrow{+木} 林(woods) \xrightarrow{+木} 森(forest)$$

One tree becomes woods, then a whole forest — more of the same thing, piled up, until it becomes something bigger. This "repeat and intensify" pattern shows up across many characters once you know to look for it.

**The rule:** when two or more meaning-elements combine, don't just add their dictionary definitions — picture the *scene* they create together, or notice when one element repeating simply means "more of it." That scene or pattern becomes the new meaning.

**Practice:** 石 means "rock" and 皮 means "skin/surface." If you combined 石 + 皮 the huì yì way, what everyday object might the resulting character represent? (Hint: think about something with a hard, rock-like outer layer.)

### phono_semantic_principle (13.4s)

## Phono Semantic Principle

You already know two tricks: a meaning piece hints at what a character is *about*, and a sound piece hints at how it's *pronounced*. Now put them together, and you unlock the biggest secret in the entire Chinese writing system.

Over 80% of all Chinese characters — tens of thousands of them — are built the exact same way: one semantic piece + one phonetic piece, fused into a single character. This is called the phono-semantic principle (形声, *xíngshēng*, "shape-sound"). It's not a rare trick. It's the *default* method for building new characters, and has been for over 2,000 years.

$$\underbrace{氵}_{\text{water}} + \underbrace{青}_{\text{qīng, sound}} = 清 \; (\text{qīng, "clear"})$$

$$\underbrace{氵}_{\text{water}} + \underbrace{生}_{\text{shēng, sound}} = 渑 \; (\text{shéng, a river name})$$

Here's why this matters so much: you don't need to memorize thousands of characters one at a time. You need two smaller sets — roughly 200 semantic and phonetic "bricks" — and one rule for snapping them together. Once you recognize 氵means water and 青 sounds like "qīng," you can *guess* both the meaning and pronunciation of a character you've never seen before, like 清, 情, or 晴. That's the LEGO way: a small kit of pieces, endlessly recombined.

**Practice:** The character 妈 (mā, "mom") combines 女 (woman) with 马 (mǎ, "horse" — sound only). Using the same logic, guess what 骂 (mà) might mean, given that it combines 口 (mouth) with 马 (horse, sound). Write one sentence explaining your guess.

### reading_unseen_characters (9.2s)

## Reading Unseen Characters

By now you know that Chinese characters are built from smaller, reusable pieces — radicals and phonetic components — the way words are built from prefixes and roots. Reading unseen characters is where that knowledge stops being trivia and becomes a tool. When you meet a character you've never studied, you don't have to guess blindly. You interrogate its bricks.

Take 湖 (hú, "lake"). Even without knowing this exact character, you can decompose it:

$$
\underbrace{水}_{\text{water — meaning clue}} + \underbrace{胡}_{\text{hú — sound clue}} = 湖
$$

The left piece, 水 (shrunk to ⺡), is a *semantic radical* — it narrows the meaning down to "something related to water." The right piece, 胡, is a *phonetic component* — it tells you the character probably sounds like "hú." Put the two guesses together: "some water-related thing pronounced roughly hú." That's often enough to recognize the word in context, even before you check a dictionary.

This works because most Chinese characters (roughly 80%) follow this semantic-phonetic pattern. The rule of thumb: **left or top piece often hints at meaning; right or bottom piece often hints at sound** — though there are exceptions, so treat both guesses as hypotheses, not certainties.

**Practice:** The character 河 (river) is built the same way: $\underbrace{水}_{\text{water}} + \underbrace{可}_{\text{kě}} = 河$. Based on the bricks alone, what meaning and what approximate sound would you guess for 河 — and does it match what you already know?
