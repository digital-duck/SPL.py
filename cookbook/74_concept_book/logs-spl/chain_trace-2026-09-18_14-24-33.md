## Physiology

Physiology is the study of how living systems function — the mechanical, chemical, and electrical processes that let cells, organs, and organ systems carry out the business of staying alive. Where anatomy asks "what is this structure?", physiology asks "what does it do, and how does the body keep it working within safe limits?" That second question points to the field's central organizing idea: homeostasis, the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite a constantly changing external one, enforced by a single recurring mechanism — the negative feedback loop.

A feedback loop works like this: a sensor detects a deviation from a target value (the *set point*), a control center (often the brain, particularly the hypothalamus) compares the reading to that target, and an effector produces a response that pushes the variable back toward normal, which in turn shuts off the original signal. This one pattern — sensor, control center, effector, shutoff — is what regulates nearly every stable variable in the body: core temperature near $37^\circ\text{C}$, blood glucose near 70–100 mg/dL fasting, blood pH within 7.35–7.45. Cross these ranges by much and enzymes misfold, nerve signaling fails, or cells die.

Worked example: consider thermoregulation. If core temperature rises above $37.5^\circ\text{C}$, thermoreceptors in the skin and hypothalamus detect the change. The hypothalamus triggers two effector responses — sweat glands increase secretion (evaporative cooling) and skin blood vessels dilate (vasodilation, radiating heat outward). As temperature falls back toward $37^\circ\text{C}$, the deviation shrinks, sweating and vasodilation taper off, and the loop settles.

Problem-solving application: once you know the sensor→control center→effector→shutoff pattern, you can reason in reverse — given a malfunction, identify which link broke. Suppose a patient sweats normally in a hot room but their skin never flushes or feels warm to the touch: is the sensor failing to detect the temperature rise, is the hypothalamus failing to send the vasodilation signal, or are the blood vessels themselves not responding to that signal? Sorting a failure into sensor, control-center, or effector is the same three-part checklist physiologists apply to any homeostatic disorder, whether it involves temperature, glucose, or blood pressure — the question is always: where, specifically, did the feedback loop break?

## Receptor

A receptor is a protein—usually embedded in the cell membrane, though some sit in the cytoplasm or nucleus—that binds a specific chemical signal, called a ligand, and converts that binding event into a change inside the cell. The ligand might be a hormone like insulin, a neurotransmitter like dopamine, or a drug like morphine. The receptor itself does no chemistry on the ligand; instead, binding changes the receptor's own shape, and that shape change triggers downstream effects: an ion channel opens, an enzyme activates, a gene turns on. This is the process of signal transduction, and the receptor is the point of translation between an outside message and an inside response.

Selectivity is the receptor's defining property. A receptor's binding site has a specific three-dimensional shape and chemical character—clusters of charged, hydrophobic, or hydrogen-bonding residues arranged in a particular geometry—so only ligands with a complementary shape and chemistry bind well, much as a key must match a lock. This is why morphine, structurally similar to the body's own endorphins, can occupy opioid receptors and produce pain relief, while a molecule with the wrong shape simply won't fit and produces no effect no matter how much is given. Selectivity also explains side effects: many drugs bind their intended receptor well but also bind related receptor subtypes weakly, producing unintended physiological consequences.

Receptors also exhibit saturability: a cell has a finite number of receptor molecules, so as ligand concentration rises, more receptors become occupied until nearly all are bound and the response plateaus—adding more ligand beyond that point produces little additional effect. This saturation curve is why doubling a drug dose does not always double its effect, and it sets a practical ceiling on how strong a receptor-mediated response can get.

Consider a clinical problem: a new drug is designed to lower blood pressure by activating a receptor on blood vessel cells that triggers vessel relaxation. Before testing it in patients, researchers must ask whether the drug binds that receptor with high selectivity, since weak binding to receptors in the heart or kidney could cause dangerous side effects—illustrating why receptor identity, not just the drug's chemical structure, determines a treatment's safety profile.

## Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug once it enters — its journey through absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME). Absorption moves the drug from its site of administration (gut, muscle, lung) into the bloodstream. Distribution carries it from blood into tissues and organs, including the target site. Metabolism, mostly in the liver, chemically transforms the drug — often deactivating it — and excretion, mainly via the kidneys, removes it from the body. Together these four processes determine how much drug reaches its target and for how long.

Because drug concentration changes continuously over time, pharmacokinetics is modeled with compartmental differential equations. In the simplest one-compartment model, the drug is assumed to distribute instantly and evenly through the body, and its concentration $C$ declines at a rate proportional to how much is present:

$$
\frac{dC}{dt} = -k_e C
$$

where $k_e$ is the elimination rate constant. Solving this equation gives exponential decay, $C(t) = C_0 e^{-k_e t}$, from which the half-life — the time for concentration to drop by half — is $t_{1/2} = \ln(2)/k_e$. This single equation is the mathematical backbone for several clinically important quantities: $C_{\max}$ (the peak concentration reached), $T_{\max}$ (the time at which that peak occurs), and the area under the curve, AUC (the integral of $C(t)$ over time, representing total drug exposure).

**Worked example.** A drug is given as a rapid intravenous dose with an initial concentration $C_0 = 80\ \text{mg/L}$ and an elimination rate constant $k_e = 0.35\ \text{hr}^{-1}$. The half-life is $t_{1/2} = \ln(2)/0.35 \approx 2.0$ hours. After 6 hours, roughly three half-lives have passed, so the concentration falls to about $80 \times (1/2)^3 = 10\ \text{mg/L}$.

**Problem-solving application.** These parameters guide real dosing decisions. If a drug's therapeutic window requires blood levels to stay above 20 mg/L, a clinician can use the decay equation to calculate exactly when the next dose must be given before the level drops too low — for the example above, solving $20 = 80 e^{-0.35t}$ gives $t \approx 4$ hours, setting the dosing interval. This same compartmental logic, extended to multiple linked compartments, underlies dosing schedules for chronic medications, IV infusion rates, and drug-interaction warnings.
