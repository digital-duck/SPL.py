## Deformation

Deformation is any change in an object's shape or size caused by an applied force. When you squeeze a rubber ball, stretch a spring, or bend a diving board, the material's internal structure shifts in response to the load. If the force is small enough and the object returns exactly to its original shape once the force is removed, the process is called **elastic deformation**. If the force exceeds a material's capacity to recover — its *elastic limit* — the object remains permanently reshaped, which is called **plastic deformation**.

For most solid materials, elastic deformation obeys **Hooke's Law**: the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium,

$$
F = -kx
$$

where $x$ is the deformation (extension or compression) and $k$ is the spring constant, a measure of stiffness specific to the material and geometry of the object. The negative sign indicates the force opposes the deformation, pulling the object back toward its natural shape. This relationship holds only within the *elastic region* — a limited range of $x$ beyond which the linear relationship breaks down and permanent deformation begins.

**Worked example.** A spring with $k = 250\ \text{N/m}$ is stretched by an applied force of $10\ \text{N}$. How far does it stretch, and what happens when the force is released?

Using $F = kx$ (magnitude form), solve for $x$:

$$
x = \frac{F}{k} = \frac{10\ \text{N}}{250\ \text{N/m}} = 0.04\ \text{m} = 4\ \text{cm}
$$

Since $4\ \text{cm}$ is well within the spring's elastic range, the spring returns to its original length once the $10\ \text{N}$ force is removed — this is elastic deformation.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose the same spring is instead loaded until it stretches $x = 0.30\ \text{m}$, and after the force is removed the spring only relaxes back to $x = 0.05\ \text{m}$ rather than $x = 0$. This tells you the spring exceeded its elastic limit at some point during loading, undergoing plastic deformation for the portion of stretch beyond recovery. In engineering design — bridges, aircraft components, medical implants — identifying the elastic limit of a material is essential: structures must be sized so that expected loads keep deformation strictly within the elastic region, ensuring the object always returns to its functional shape rather than accumulating permanent damage over repeated use.

## Stress

Stress is the body's nonspecific, stereotyped physiological response to any demand for adaptation — physical, psychological, or environmental. The word "nonspecific" is key: whether the stressor is a predator, an exam, a burn, or an argument, the underlying physiological machinery is largely the same. This idea was formalized by Hans Selye, who described the **General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)**, a three-stage response: alarm, resistance, and exhaustion.

**Alarm** is the immediate mobilization phase. The hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system and triggers the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The adrenal medulla releases epinephrine and norepinephrine within seconds, producing the classic "fight-or-flight" signs — elevated heart rate, dilated pupils, redirected blood flow toward skeletal muscle. Slower-acting, the adrenal cortex releases cortisol, which raises blood glucose and suppresses non-urgent processes like digestion and immune activity, conserving energy for the perceived threat.

**Resistance** follows if the stressor persists. The body maintains an elevated state of readiness — cortisol stays high, glucose mobilization continues — allowing sustained coping, but at a metabolic cost. Systems not essential to immediate survival (reproductive, digestive, immune) remain dampened.

**Exhaustion** occurs when the stressor is chronic and resources are depleted. The adaptive systems that were protective in the short term become damaging: chronically elevated cortisol contributes to immune suppression, hypertension, impaired memory formation, and increased vulnerability to disease. This is why *acute* stress (a brief exam) and *chronic* stress (ongoing financial insecurity) have such different health consequences despite sharing the same initial alarm response.

Consider a concrete problem: a student pulls repeated all-nighters before finals week. Night one triggers alarm — cortisol spikes, alertness increases, performance may even improve short-term. By night four, the student is in resistance — sustained but fragile — showing irritability and slower recall. By the following week, exhaustion sets in: the student gets sick, a direct consequence of immune suppression from prolonged cortisol exposure. Applying GAS to this scenario explains not just why finals week is exhausting, but why the crash afterward is a predictable physiological outcome, not a personal failing — and why interventions (sleep, pacing, recovery periods) work by interrupting the transition from resistance into exhaustion rather than eliminating the alarm response itself.

## Tensile Strength

Tensile strength is the maximum stress a material can withstand while being stretched or pulled before it fails — either by permanent (plastic) deformation or outright fracture. Because it is defined as a stress, it inherits stress's units and definition directly: force per unit cross-sectional area,

$$
\sigma = \frac{F}{A}
$$

where $F$ is the applied force and $A$ is the original cross-sectional area of the material. Tensile strength, often denoted $\sigma_{UTS}$ (ultimate tensile strength), is the peak value of $\sigma$ measured on a stress–strain curve before the material necks down and breaks.

**Worked example.** A steel rod with a cross-sectional area of $A = 5 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$ ($5\ \text{cm}^2$) is pulled in a tensile-testing machine. The rod fractures when the applied force reaches $F = 2 \times 10^5\ \text{N}$. Its tensile strength is

$$
\sigma_{UTS} = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{2 \times 10^5\ \text{N}}{5 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2} = 4 \times 10^8\ \text{Pa} = 400\ \text{MPa}
$$

This value is a material property — it does not depend on the rod's length or the specific force used in the test, only on the ratio of force to area at the point of failure. It can be compared directly against tabulated values (mild steel: ~400–550 MPa; aluminum: ~90–200 MPa; carbon fiber composites: >1000 MPa) to assess whether steel was the right choice.

**Problem-solving application.** Tensile strength lets engineers size a component before it is ever loaded to failure. Suppose a cable must support a maximum working load of $F_{max} = 10^4\ \text{N}$, and the material has $\sigma_{UTS} = 500\ \text{MPa}$. To avoid failure, engineers apply a safety factor $n$ (commonly 2–4) so the working stress stays well below $\sigma_{UTS}$:

$$
A \geq \frac{n \cdot F_{max}}{\sigma_{UTS}}
$$

With $n = 3$: $A \geq \dfrac{3 \times 10^4}{5 \times 10^8} = 6 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2$, meaning the cable's cross-section must be at least $60\ \text{mm}^2$. This inequality — rearranged from the stress definition — is the core design tool linking a measured material property to a required physical dimension.
