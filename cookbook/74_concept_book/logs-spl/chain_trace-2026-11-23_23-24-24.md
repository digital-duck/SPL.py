### temperature (1.0s)

## Temperature

**Definition.** Temperature is the quantity a thermometer measures. Operationally, two objects have the same temperature if no net heat flows between them when they are placed in thermal contact (the zeroth law of thermodynamics). A thermometer exploits this. It reaches equilibrium with the object, and a physical property that varies with temperature, such as the length of a mercury column, then gives a reading.

Microscopically, temperature measures the average random translational kinetic energy of particles. For an ideal monatomic gas:

$$\langle K \rangle = \tfrac{3}{2} k_B T$$

where $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the absolute temperature in kelvin. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, so $T_K = T_C + 273.15$. Use kelvin in any formula where $T$ appears directly.

Temperature is not the same as heat or total thermal energy. Temperature is an intensive quantity that does not depend on how much material you have. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

**Worked example.** Find the average kinetic energy of a helium atom at room temperature, $27^\circ\text{C}$.

1. Convert to kelvin: $T = 27 + 273.15 \approx 300\ \text{K}$.
2. Apply the formula:

$$\langle K \rangle = \tfrac{3}{2}(1.381\times10^{-23})(300) \approx 6.2\times10^{-21}\ \text{J}$$

This is about $0.039\ \text{eV}$, tiny per atom, but a mole of atoms carries thousands of joules.

**Problem-solving application.** Because $\langle K \rangle = \tfrac12 m\langle v^2\rangle$, the formula also gives a typical particle speed. The root-mean-square speed is

$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}}$$

*Problem:* Compare $v_{\text{rms}}$ for helium ($m = 6.64\times10^{-27}$ kg) and nitrogen ($m = 4.65\times10^{-26}$ kg) at 300 K.

Helium:

$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(1.381\times10^{-23})(300)}{6.64\times10^{-27}}} \approx 1.37\times10^{3}\ \text{m/s}$$

Nitrogen:

$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(1.381\times10^{-23})(300)}{4.65\times10^{-26}}} \approx 517\ \text{m/s}$$

At the same temperature the two gases have the same average kinetic energy, but the lighter helium atoms move about 2.6 times faster. Since $v_{\text{rms}}\propto\sqrt{1/m}$, the speed ratio is $\sqrt{28/4}\approx 2.6$, which matches. This is why helium escapes Earth's atmosphere far more readily than nitrogen.

**Check your understanding.** If the Kelvin temperature doubles, by what factor does $\langle K\rangle$ change, and by what factor does $v_{\text{rms}}$ change? (Answer: $\langle K\rangle$ doubles, and $v_{\text{rms}}$ increases by $\sqrt{2}$.) Note that doubling from $20^\circ\text{C}$ to $40^\circ\text{C}$ does *not* double the kinetic energy. You must convert to kelvin first, and $293\ \text{K}\to313\ \text{K}$ is only a 7% increase.

### heat (28.8s)

## Heat

**Heat** is the spontaneous transfer of thermal energy between two objects (or between an object and its surroundings) caused by a temperature difference. Energy flows from the hotter object to the colder one until they reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium). Heat is a *process quantity*: an object does not "contain" heat. It contains internal energy, and heat is the energy in transit. We write $Q$ for heat, measured in joules (J), with $Q>0$ when energy enters the object.

**Key relation.** For an object that stays in one phase (no melting or boiling),

$$Q = mc\,\Delta T,$$

where $m$ is mass, $\Delta T = T_f - T_i$, and $c$ is the **specific heat capacity**. This is the energy needed to change 1 kg of the material by 1 K. A material with a large $c$, such as water, changes temperature slowly when energy is added or removed.

**Conservation of energy.** If no energy escapes, the heat lost by the hot object equals the heat gained by the cold one:

$$\sum Q_i = 0.$$

### Worked example

A $0.200\ \text{kg}$ aluminum block at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$ is dropped into $0.500\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20\,^\circ\text{C}$. Find the final temperature $T_f$. Use $c_{\text{Al}} = 900\ \text{J/(kg·K)}$ and $c_{\text{w}} = 4186\ \text{J/(kg·K)}$, and assume no energy escapes.

Both objects end at $T_f$, and $Q_{\text{Al}} + Q_{\text{w}} = 0$:

$$(0.200)(900)(T_f - 100) + (0.500)(4186)(T_f - 20) = 0$$

$$180\,T_f - 18000 + 2093\,T_f - 41860 = 0$$

$$2273\,T_f = 59860 \;\Rightarrow\; T_f \approx 26.3\,^\circ\text{C}.$$

The result is close to the water's starting temperature. The water's $mc = 2093$ J/K is far larger than the metal's $180$ J/K, so the water's temperature changes much less.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** A $0.150\ \text{kg}$ sample of an unknown metal is heated to $95.0\,^\circ\text{C}$ and dropped into $0.400\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20.0\,^\circ\text{C}$. The system settles at $23.0\,^\circ\text{C}$. Identify the metal's specific heat capacity, assuming no energy escapes.

**Strategy.** Compute the heat the water gains, which is fully known. Then use conservation of energy to find the unknown $c$.

1. Water gains: $Q_{\text{w}} = (0.400)(4186)(23.0 - 20.0) = 5023\ \text{J}$.
2. The metal must lose the same amount: $Q_{\text{m}} = -5023\ \text{J}$.
3. Write the metal's heat in terms of $c$:
$$Q_{\text{m}} = (0.150)\,c\,(23.0 - 95.0) = -10.8\,c.$$
4. Solve:
$$-10.8\,c = -5023 \;\Rightarrow\; c \approx 465\ \text{J/(kg·K)}.$$

This is close to the value for iron ($\approx 450$ J/(kg·K)), so iron or steel is a likely candidate. The difference could come from small heat losses to the container.

**Checklist for heat problems:**

1. List every object with its mass, specific heat, and initial temperature.
2. Write $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ for each object, always using $T_f - T_i$.
3. Apply $\sum Q = 0$ for an isolated system and solve for the unknown.
4. Check the signs. The hotter object must have $Q<0$ and the colder object $Q>0$. The final temperature must lie between the two starting temperatures.

### specific_heat (13.8s)

## Specific Heat

**Specific heat** (or specific heat capacity), $c$, is the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of $1.00\ \text{kg}$ of a substance by $1.00\ ^\circ\text{C}$. It is a material property: different substances need different amounts of energy to change temperature by the same amount. The heat $Q$ that changes a mass $m$ by a temperature change $\Delta T$ is

$$Q = m\,c\,\Delta T,$$

so $c = \dfrac{Q}{m\,\Delta T}$, with SI units of $\text{J/(kg·K)}$. Because a change of $1\ ^\circ\text{C}$ equals a change of $1\ \text{K}$, the two units give the same value of $c$. Liquid water has $c \approx 4186\ \text{J/(kg·K)}$, aluminum about $900$, and copper about $387$. A large $c$ means the material absorbs a lot of heat for a small temperature change. A small $c$ means it heats and cools quickly.

The formula applies only while the substance stays in one phase. During melting or boiling, heat changes the phase and the temperature stays constant. That case uses latent heat, not specific heat.

### Worked example

How much heat raises $2.00\ \text{kg}$ of water from $20.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $60.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$?

$$Q = (2.00)(4186)(60.0-20.0) = 3.35\times10^{5}\ \text{J}.$$

The same heat applied to $2.00\ \text{kg}$ of copper gives

$$\Delta T = \frac{Q}{mc} = \frac{3.35\times10^{5}}{(2.00)(387)} \approx 433\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$

Copper warms about $10.8$ times as much as water, which is the ratio $4186/387$.

### Problem-solving application: finding a final temperature

An isolated system exchanges no heat with its surroundings, so heat lost by the hot object equals heat gained by the cold one:

$$Q_{\text{lost}} = Q_{\text{gained}}.$$

**Problem.** A $0.200\ \text{kg}$ aluminum block at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is dropped into $0.500\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Find the final equilibrium temperature $T_f$, ignoring losses to the container and air.

1. Set the heat lost by the aluminum equal to the heat gained by the water:
$$m_{\text{Al}}\,c_{\text{Al}}(100 - T_f) = m_w\,c_w\,(T_f - 20.0).$$
2. Substitute values:
$$(0.200)(900)(100 - T_f) = (0.500)(4186)(T_f - 20.0).$$
3. Simplify:
$$180(100 - T_f) = 2093(T_f - 20.0)$$
$$18000 - 180\,T_f = 2093\,T_f - 41860.$$
4. Solve:
$$T_f = \frac{59860}{2273} \approx 26.3\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$

**Check.** The final temperature lies between $20.0$ and $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and is much closer to the water's starting temperature. That makes sense, because the water has far more mass and a much larger $c$, so it changes temperature less. Write each heat as a positive quantity, using (hot − final) and (final − cold). This avoids sign errors.

### Why it matters

Water's large specific heat explains why coastal climates are milder than inland ones and why water works well as a coolant. Metals' small values explain why a metal pan reaches cooking temperature quickly. In a calorimetry experiment, measuring $Q$, $m$ and $\Delta T$ lets you solve for $c$ and identify an unknown material.

### heat_capacity_equation (13.4s)

## Heat Capacity Equation

### Definition

When energy is transferred as heat to or from an object, the object's temperature changes by an amount that depends on how much material there is and what the material is made of. This relationship is

$$Q = mc\,\Delta T, \qquad \Delta T = T_f - T_i$$

where $Q$ is the heat transferred (J), $m$ is the mass (kg or g), $c$ is the **specific heat capacity** (J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹ or J·g⁻¹·°C⁻¹), and $\Delta T$ is the change in temperature. The specific heat $c$ is the energy needed to raise 1 kg (or 1 g) of a substance by 1 K. Water has a large value, $c = 4.18\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$, while copper has a small one, $c = 0.385\ \text{J/(g·°C)}$.

Sign convention: $Q > 0$ when the object gains energy ($\Delta T > 0$), and $Q < 0$ when it loses energy. The equation applies only when the substance stays in one phase. During melting or boiling, energy goes into changing phase and the temperature stays constant.

A related quantity is the **heat capacity** $C = mc$, which describes a whole object, so that $Q = C\,\Delta T$. Because a change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C, $\Delta T$ may be computed in either unit.

### Worked example

How much heat is needed to warm 250 g of water from 20.0 °C to 60.0 °C?

$$Q = (250\ \text{g})(4.18\ \text{J/(g·°C)})(60.0 - 20.0\ ^\circ\text{C}) = 4.18\times10^{4}\ \text{J}$$

About 41.8 kJ is required. The same energy given to 250 g of copper would raise its temperature by

$$\Delta T = \frac{Q}{mc} = \frac{4.18\times10^{4}}{(250)(0.385)} \approx 434\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$

This shows why metal pans heat up quickly while water heats slowly.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** A 100 g piece of copper at 95.0 °C is dropped into 200 g of water at 20.0 °C in an insulated cup. Find the final equilibrium temperature $T_f$.

**Strategy.** With no energy lost to the surroundings, the heat lost by the copper equals the heat gained by the water:

$$Q_{\text{Cu}} + Q_{\text{water}} = 0.$$

Substituting the heat equation for each:

$$m_{\text{Cu}}c_{\text{Cu}}(T_f - 95.0) + m_w c_w (T_f - 20.0) = 0$$

$$(100)(0.385)(T_f - 95.0) + (200)(4.18)(T_f - 20.0) = 0$$

$$38.5\,T_f - 3657.5 + 836\,T_f - 16720 = 0$$

$$874.5\,T_f = 20377.5 \;\Rightarrow\; T_f \approx 23.3\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$

**Check.** The result lies between the two starting temperatures. It is much closer to the water's starting value, as expected, because the water has a much larger heat capacity ($mc = 836$ J/°C versus $38.5$ J/°C).

**General approach.**
1. Identify which objects gain energy and which lose it.
2. Write $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ for each, always using $T_f - T_i$.
3. Apply energy conservation, $\sum Q = 0$, for an isolated system.
4. Solve for the unknown and check that the answer is physically reasonable and lies between the initial temperatures.

### thermal_equilibrium (24.8s)

## Thermal Equilibrium

**Definition.** Two objects in thermal contact exchange energy as heat until their temperatures are equal. At that point, the net heat transfer between them is zero, and they are in *thermal equilibrium*. This is the idea behind the **zeroth law of thermodynamics**: if $A$ is in equilibrium with $C$, and $B$ is in equilibrium with $C$, then $A$ and $B$ are in equilibrium with each other. This law is why temperature is a meaningful, measurable property, and why a thermometer works.

Equilibrium is dynamic rather than static. Molecules still collide and exchange energy, but the flow from hot to cold exactly balances the flow from cold to hot, so no net change occurs.

**Finding the final temperature.** Suppose the system is isolated, so no energy escapes. Energy lost by the hotter object equals energy gained by the colder one. Heat absorbed by an object of mass $m$ and specific heat capacity $c$ is $Q = mc(T_f - T_i)$, where $T_i$ is its initial temperature and $T_f$ is the common final temperature. Because the heat lost and gained must cancel,

$$\sum_i m_i c_i (T_f - T_i) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_f = \frac{\sum_i m_i c_i T_i}{\sum_i m_i c_i}$$

So $T_f$ is a heat-capacity-weighted average of the initial temperatures. This holds as long as no phase change occurs.

**Worked example.** A $0.200\ \text{kg}$ aluminum block at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$ ($c = 900\ \text{J/kg·K}$) is dropped into $0.500\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20\,^\circ\text{C}$ ($c = 4186\ \text{J/kg·K}$) inside an insulated container. Find $T_f$.

The products $mc$ are
- Aluminum: $0.200 \times 900 = 180\ \text{J/K}$
- Water: $0.500 \times 4186 = 2093\ \text{J/K}$

Then

$$T_f = \frac{(180)(100) + (2093)(20)}{180 + 2093} = \frac{18000 + 41860}{2273} \approx 26.3\,^\circ\text{C}$$

The result lies between $20$ and $100\,^\circ\text{C}$ and is much closer to the water's temperature. This makes sense because the water's $mc$ is more than ten times larger.

**Problem-solving application.** Use this checklist for equilibrium problems:

1. Confirm the system is isolated, or account for any heat lost to the surroundings.
2. Confirm that no melting or boiling occurs. If it could, this method does not apply directly and extra terms are needed.
3. Write $\sum m c (T_f - T_i) = 0$ with one term per object. Signs take care of themselves: the hot object's term is negative and the cold object's term is positive.
4. Solve for $T_f$, then check that it lies between the extreme initial temperatures. If it doesn't, an error has crept in.

*Check yourself:* If $1.00\ \text{kg}$ of water at $80\,^\circ\text{C}$ is mixed with $3.00\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20\,^\circ\text{C}$, the specific heats cancel. This gives $T_f = (1.00 \times 80 + 3.00 \times 20)/4.00 = 35\,^\circ\text{C}$.

**Why it matters.** Equilibrium explains why a cup of coffee cools to room temperature, why calorimetry can measure unknown specific heats, and why engineers use heat sinks to carry heat away from electronics. Each of these situations follows the same rule: heat flows until the temperatures match.

### calorimetry (13.5s)

## Calorimetry

**Calorimetry** is the measurement and calculation of heat transfer between bodies. Its central technique applies when two bodies at different temperatures are placed in thermal contact inside an isolated system, meaning no net heat is lost to the surroundings. Heat flows from the hotter body to the cooler one until both reach a common **equilibrium temperature** $T_f$.

### Definition and governing relations

For a body of mass $m$ and specific heat capacity $c$, the heat absorbed (or released) when its temperature changes by $\Delta T = T_f - T_i$ is

$$Q = mc\,\Delta T.$$

$Q$ is positive when the body warms and negative when it cools. Energy conservation in an isolated system requires

$$\sum Q_i = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q_{\text{gained}} = -Q_{\text{lost}}.$$

For two bodies, $m_1c_1(T_f - T_1) + m_2c_2(T_f - T_2) = 0$. Solving for $T_f$ gives

$$T_f = \frac{m_1c_1T_1 + m_2c_2T_2}{m_1c_1 + m_2c_2}.$$

So $T_f$ is a weighted average of the initial temperatures, with weights equal to each body's **heat capacity** $C = mc$. The body with the larger $C$ pulls the result closer to its own starting temperature. This holds only if neither body changes phase.

### Worked example

A $200\ \text{g}$ block of copper ($c = 0.385\ \text{J/g·°C}$) at $100\ \text{°C}$ is dropped into $300\ \text{g}$ of water ($c = 4.18\ \text{J/g·°C}$) at $20\ \text{°C}$. Find $T_f$.

Heat capacities: $C_{\text{Cu}} = 200(0.385) = 77\ \text{J/°C}$ and $C_{\text{w}} = 300(4.18) = 1254\ \text{J/°C}$.

$$T_f = \frac{77(100) + 1254(20)}{77 + 1254} = \frac{7700 + 25080}{1331} \approx 24.6\ \text{°C}.$$

Check: the copper loses $77(75.4) \approx 5.8\ \text{kJ}$, and the water gains $1254(4.6) \approx 5.8\ \text{kJ}$. The water's large heat capacity keeps its temperature nearly unchanged.

### Problem-solving application

Calorimetry also works backward. A common lab task is finding an unknown specific heat. Suppose a $50.0\ \text{g}$ metal sample at $95.0\ \text{°C}$ is placed in $100\ \text{g}$ of water at $22.0\ \text{°C}$, and the system settles at $26.0\ \text{°C}$. Setting $Q_{\text{metal}} + Q_{\text{water}} = 0$:

$$50.0\,c_m(26.0 - 95.0) + 100(4.18)(26.0 - 22.0) = 0$$

$$c_m = \frac{1672}{50.0 \times 69.0} \approx 0.485\ \text{J/g·°C}.$$

**A reliable procedure:**

1. Identify the isolated system and confirm there is no phase change. If there is one, add latent-heat terms such as $mL$.
2. Write $Q = mc(T_f - T_i)$ for every body. Always use final minus initial, so the signs take care of themselves.
3. Set the sum of all $Q$ values to zero and solve for the unknown.
4. Sanity check: $T_f$ must lie between the initial temperatures, and the hotter body's heat loss must equal the cooler body's heat gain in magnitude.

In real experiments, the calorimeter cup absorbs some heat. This is handled by adding a term $C_{\text{cal}}(T_f - T_i)$ to the sum. Ignoring it makes the measured $T_f$ come out slightly low, because the cup absorbs part of the heat.
