## 人

**Definition.** 人 (rén) denotes "person, human being." Graphically it derives from a pictograph of a human figure viewed in profile — two strokes suggesting legs in a walking stance, historically drawn with a slight forward lean. As a standalone character it is among the most frequent morphemes in the modern lexicon; as a semantic component (radical 人, and its compressed left-side form 亻, "rénzìpáng") it is one of the most productive classifiers in the entire writing system, appearing in several hundred characters that denote people, social roles, actions performed by people, or states experienced by people.

**Mechanism.** The component operates in two distinct structural positions. In the first, 人 stands alone or as a stacked/doubled element, as in 从 (two people, one following another → "to follow") or 众 (three people → "crowd, multitude"). In the second, far more common position, 人 compresses to 亻 and occupies the left-hand slot of a left-right compound, pairing with a phonetic or secondary semantic component on the right — for example:

$$\underbrace{亻}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{也}_{\text{phonetic}} = 他 \;(\text{"he/him"})$$

$$\underbrace{亻}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{尔}_{\text{phonetic}} = 你 \;(\text{"you"})$$

$$\underbrace{亻}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{言}_{\text{speech}} = 信 \;(\text{"trust, letter"})$$

Note that the semantic contribution of 人/亻 is not uniform: in pronouns it marks the referent as human; in 信 it contributes an inferential meaning ("a person's word is trustworthy") rather than a bare taxonomic label. This variability is itself the open question the component raises.

**Literature context.** Traditional 六书 (liùshū) analysis classifies most 亻-compounds as 形声 (phono-semantic) characters, treating 亻 as a semantic determinative analogous to Egyptian hieroglyphic determinatives or Akkadian classifiers in cuneiform. Boltz (1994) and DeFrancis (1989) debate how reliably such determinatives predicted meaning for a fluent reader versus how much they functioned as post-hoc disambiguators added during script standardization. More recent psycholinguistic work (Taft & Zhu, 1997; Williams & Bever, 2010) uses priming experiments to ask whether the 亻 radical is activated as a distinct semantic unit during real-time reading, independent of the whole-character gestalt — a question with direct bearing on models of orthographic processing in logographic scripts.

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could compile a corpus of the ~200 most frequent 亻-initial characters, classify each by the semantic relation between radical and meaning (taxonomic "is-a-person," relational "performed-by-a-person," or metaphorical extension, as in 信), and quantify the proportion falling into each class. A complementary experiment: run a lexical-decision or radical-priming task with native-reader participants to test whether processing speed correlates with the semantic-relation class identified in the corpus study.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the classification scheme explicitly with worked examples for each category, report the corpus-derived proportions, and — if the experimental arm is included — present reaction-time data with appropriate statistical treatment. The discussion should address whether the traditional determinative account or a graded, associative-semantics account better fits the combined corpus and behavioral evidence, and should flag where the classification scheme itself required judgment calls that a second coder should verify.

## 从

**Definition.** 从 (cóng) is a pictographic-derivational verb meaning "to follow," built from two identical copies of 人 (person), one behind the other. Unlike compound characters that fuse semantically distinct radicals into a new meaning (a semantic-compound, 会意字), 从 belongs to a narrower subclass: repetition-as-depiction, where doubling a single pictograph enacts a relationship — following, accompanying, succession — that neither copy carries alone. The composition is:

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person (leading)}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person (trailing)}} = \text{从 (to follow)}$$

**Mechanism.** The oracle-bone and bronze forms show two human silhouettes in profile, one positioned directly behind the other along the same line of movement — the graphic equivalent of a single frame excerpted from a walking sequence. The character does not depict "two people" as a plural noun (that role belongs to 众, three people, or to reduplication strategies elsewhere in the script); rather, doubling encodes a *relational* verb by spatial arrangement, not by adding a semantic classifier (radical) to a phonetic component, which is how the majority of Chinese characters (formed as phono-semantic compounds, 形声字) are built. 从 is thus a fossil of an older, iconic stratum of the script, predating the systematic phono-semantic method that dominates from the Han dynasty onward. Its simplified form retains both 人 units, unlike many simplifications that merge or drop radicals, which suggests scribes and reformers treated the doubling as load-bearing for legibility of meaning rather than as redundant strokes to prune.

**Literature context.** Chinese paleography (Qiu Xigui's *Chinese Writing* remains the standard synthesis) classifies characters like 从 under 会意 (associative compounds), but within that category there is ongoing discussion about whether "doubling" constructions (从, 比, 北) deserve their own typological label distinct from associative compounds that combine *different* pictographs (e.g., 休 = person + tree = "rest"). Some paleographers argue doubling is a distinct iconic strategy — depicting a relation through repeated identical units — closer to a diagrammatic notation than to associative semantics. This taxonomic boundary is not fully settled, particularly for character families where the "doubled" component later diverges physically from its single form (compare 从 to 林, forest, from two 木).

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could compile a corpus of doubling-derived characters (从, 比, 北, 林, 森, 众, 多) from oracle-bone through modern forms and test whether they pattern together across three variables: (1) resistance to simplification-era radical merging, (2) semantic domain (spatial/relational vs. quantity/plural), and (3) frequency of paired usage in classical versus modern corpora. Comparative work against another script with iconic reduplication (e.g., Egyptian hieroglyphic pluralization by tripling) would sharpen the typological claim.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should establish the character's attested graphic forms across the historical record (with sourced images or standard paleographic catalogs, not reconstructed guesses), state clearly why the case for "doubling as its own iconic subclass" is or is not more persuasive than folding it into the ordinary associative-compound category, and report the corpus findings as evidence for or against that typological claim — not as a settled classification.

## 众

**Definition.** 众 (zhòng) denotes a crowd, a multitude, or the general mass of people — as in 群众 (qúnzhòng, "the masses"), 观众 (guānzhòng, "audience"), and 众所周知 (zhòngsuǒzhōuzhī, "as everyone knows"). Structurally, 众 is formed by tripling a single graphic unit, 人 (rén, "person"), to signal plurality beyond simple duality:

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = \underbrace{众}_{\text{crowd, the masses}}$$

**Mechanism.** This is an instance of a broader principle in Chinese script formation sometimes called *tripling* or *triplication*: repeating a component three times derives a character meaning "many X" or "abundant X," distinct from the doubled form, which typically denotes a smaller or more literal plural. A close parallel is 森 (sēn, "forest"), which triples 木 (mù, "tree") — moving from one tree, to a pair (林, "grove"), to a forest — by the identical compositional logic 众 applies to 人. Beyond 人 and 木, the same triplication pattern recurs with a small closed set of other components (water, fire, stone, among them), but these are lexical curiosities rather than independently productive primitives for this discussion, and are set aside here. The historical graphic form of 众 in oracle-bone and bronze script is even more explicit than the modern character suggests: early forms show three human figures stacked or arrayed beneath a sun radical (日), commonly glossed as "people laboring together under the sun" — the character's earliest attested meaning is closer to "the multitude of laborers" than an abstract "crowd." The modern form drops the sun element, leaving the bare tripled 人, which is what survives in simplified and traditional script alike today.

**Literature context.** Triplication as a productive word-formation device is well documented in classical philology (Xu Shen's *Shuowen Jiezi*, 2nd century CE, already groups 森, 众, and related forms under this pattern), but its cognitive status — whether modern readers actually parse 众 as "three people" during real-time reading, or treat it as an unanalyzed whole — remains an open empirical question in psycholinguistics of Chinese reading. Work on radical-based lexical decision (e.g., studies following Taft & Zhu's componential models of Chinese character recognition) has tested single-radical priming extensively but has given comparatively little attention to triplicated characters specifically, where the same component repeats three times rather than two different components combining.

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could run a radical-priming lexical-decision task comparing triplicated characters (众, 森) against structurally matched two-component compounds and single-component controls, measuring whether repetition of a single radical produces stronger, weaker, or qualitatively different priming than combination of two distinct radicals. A complementary corpus study could quantify how often 众-headed compounds compositionally preserve the "many people" semantic (群众, 大众, 众人) versus how often the meaning has drifted (观众 as "audience," where 众 no longer straightforwardly means "crowd" but rather pluralizes the viewing act).

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should distinguish three separate claims that are easy to conflate: the etymological claim (what the oracle-bone form meant), the synchronic compositional claim (whether the modern radical structure is still semantically transparent), and the psycholinguistic claim (whether fluent readers actually decompose the character during processing). Evidence for one does not entail the others, and the investigation's contribution should be located precisely among these three levels.
