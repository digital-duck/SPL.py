## Suffix

You already know that a suffix rides at the end of a word like a small engine bolted onto the back, and that it can quietly change a word's job in the sentence. Now let's look at what that engine actually does: it doesn't just decorate a word — it reclassifies it, switching its part of speech.

Take the stem *act*. On its own, it's a verb — something you do. Attach the suffix *-ion*, and it becomes a noun:

$$
\underbrace{act}_{\text{verb: to do}} + \underbrace{\text{-ion}}_{\text{makes a noun}} = \underbrace{action}_{\text{the act itself}}
$$

Notice what happened: the stem *act* still means "to do," but the suffix *-ion* answers a new question — "what happened?" — and drags the whole word into noun territory. The suffix doesn't care what stem you feed it; *-ion* almost always builds a noun, whether you attach it to *act*, *create*, or *celebrate*. That's why suffixes are sometimes called the "part-of-speech engine" of English: swap the suffix, and you swap the grammatical role, even though the core meaning of the stem stays put.

This is different from a prefix, which usually just adjusts meaning (like *re-* meaning "again") without changing the word's category.

Other suffixes work the same way but build different jobs — *-or* tends to build a noun naming "the one who does it," and *-able* tends to build an adjective meaning "capable of being done." Same engine, different label.

**Practice:** Take the stem *predict* (a verb) and attach *-ion* to build a new noun. Then write one sentence using *predict* as a verb and one sentence using your new word as a noun.
