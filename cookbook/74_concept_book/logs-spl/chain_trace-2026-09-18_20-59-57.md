### perceptron_1958 (0.0s)

## Perceptron 1958

The perceptron, introduced by Frank Rosenblatt at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, is a linear threshold classifier trained by an online error-correction rule, and it is the first learning algorithm for which a convergence guarantee was proven for a nontrivial hypothesis class. Given an input $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the perceptron computes an output $\hat{y} = \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b)$, where $\mathbf{w}$ is a weight vector and $b$ a bias term (absorbed into $\mathbf{w}$ by appending a constant $1$ to $\mathbf{x}$). Given a labeled example $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ with $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$, the weight update rule fires only on misclassification:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t + \eta \, y_i \, \mathbf{x}_i \quad \text{if } y_i(\mathbf{w}_t^\top \mathbf{x}_i) \le 0
$$

**Mechanism.** The rule is a stochastic, mistake-driven procedure: the algorithm scans the training set (in Rosenblatt's original hardware, the Mark I Perceptron, this was implemented with 400 photocell "retina" units and motorized potentiometers standing in for weights), and it adjusts $\mathbf{w}$ only when a mistake occurs, nudging the decision boundary $\{\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} = 0\}$ toward correctly separating the offending point. The Perceptron Convergence Theorem (proved by Rosenblatt in 1958 and given a clean geometric proof by Novikoff in 1962) states that if the training data is linearly separable with margin $\gamma = \min_i y_i (\mathbf{w}^{*\top}\mathbf{x}_i) / \|\mathbf{w}^*\|$ for some unit-norm separator $\mathbf{w}^*$, and $R = \max_i \|\mathbf{x}_i\|$, then the algorithm makes at most $R^2/\gamma^2$ mistakes before converging to a separating hyperplane — a bound independent of the dimension $d$ and of the total number of training examples, depending only on the geometry of separability.

**Literature context.** The theorem's dimension-free bound is precisely what fueled the initial optimism (and the 1958 *New York Times* coverage promising machines that could "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence"), but Minsky and Papert's *Perceptrons* (1969) demonstrated the flip side: single-layer perceptrons cannot represent functions that are not linearly separable, the canonical example being XOR, and no amount of training data or margin fixes this — it is a representational ceiling, not an optimization failure. This result is widely credited with precipitating the first AI winter in neural network research, and it set up the motivation, two decades later, for multilayer networks trained via backpropagation, which are universal function approximators precisely because they lift inputs into representations where linear separability becomes achievable.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement the perceptron update rule from scratch (not via an ML library's fit method) on a synthetic 2D dataset with controllable margin $\gamma$, and empirically verify the $R^2/\gamma^2$ mistake bound by plotting observed mistake counts against the theoretical bound as $\gamma \to 0$. Extend the experiment to the XOR dataset to observe non-convergence directly, then compare against a two-layer network on the same data to demonstrate the representational gap Minsky and Papert identified.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the convergence bound as a testable hypothesis, report the empirical mistake counts against varying margins with statistical variation across random initializations and example orderings, and explicitly connect the XOR failure case to the representational (not optimization) argument in *Perceptrons* — establishing that the limitation is a property of the hypothesis class, not of the training procedure.

### ai_winter_1974 (0.0s)

## Ai Winter 1974

**Definition.** The 1974 AI winter denotes the sharp, internationally correlated contraction in research funding and institutional confidence in artificial intelligence that followed the publication of Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's *Perceptrons* (1969) and, in the UK, the Lighthill Report (1973). It is not a single event but a funding-and-sentiment regime shift: DARPA redirected support away from unconstrained basic AI research toward mission-specific, evaluable programs, and the UK Science Research Council curtailed AI funding at all but a few universities. The episode is best treated as a case study in how a technical critique of one architecture — the single-layer [[perceptron_1958]] — propagated into a field-wide credibility collapse lasting roughly from the mid-1970s to the early 1980s.

**Mechanism.** The causal chain runs through three distinct layers, and disentangling them is the analytical task. First, the *technical* layer: Minsky and Papert proved that single-layer perceptrons cannot compute functions that are not linearly separable, most famously XOR, and argued (without formal proof) that this limitation would generalize to multilayer extensions given the computational tools then available for training them. Second, the *rhetorical* layer: this technical result was read — by funders and by some researchers — as evidence against neural approaches to intelligence generally, despite multilayer networks with nonlinear units facing no such in-principle barrier, a fact obscured by the absence of an efficient training procedure until backpropagation's popularization in the mid-1980s. Third, the *institutional* layer: overpromising in earlier funded work (machine translation after ALPAC in 1966, general problem-solving systems) had already primed funding bodies toward skepticism, so the perceptron critique served as a legible, citable justification for cuts that institutional actors may have wanted to make regardless.

**Literature context.** Historians of science (Crevier 1993; Nilsson 2009; Olazaran 1996) dispute how much explanatory weight the Minsky–Papert result alone can bear versus concurrent economic and political pressures (the Mansfield Amendment's demand for military relevance, post-Vietnam budget contraction). Olazaran's sociological analysis is notable for treating *Perceptrons* itself as a rhetorical artifact — its mathematical content was narrower than its reception. This remains genuinely contested: there is no consensus quantitative accounting of how much of the funding decline is attributable to the book versus to prior credibility erosion.

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable graduate project is a citation- and funding-history analysis: assemble DARPA and SRC funding records for AI-labeled projects from 1965–1980, and separately trace citation counts and citation *sentiment* (supportive vs. critical) for *Perceptrons* over the same window, testing whether the funding inflection point in the data precedes, coincides with, or lags the book's publication and its uptake in review articles and funding-agency reports. A complementary technical component: reimplement the XOR counterexample and demonstrate empirically that a two-layer network with a differentiable nonlinearity, trained by gradient descent, resolves it — clarifying exactly which claim in the original critique was mathematically correct (single-layer inseparability) versus which was an unsupported extrapolation (multilayer intractability).

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should keep the mathematical claim (linear separability) and the historical/sociological claim (field-wide funding collapse) in clearly separated sections, state explicitly which parts of the causal narrative are supported by primary funding data versus secondary historical interpretation, and avoid the common conflation — found in many popular accounts — of "perceptrons cannot solve XOR" with "neural networks cannot solve XOR," since only the first is a proven mathematical statement.

### expert_systems_1980 (0.0s)

## Expert Systems 1980

An expert system is a program that separates domain-specific propositional knowledge, encoded as a **knowledge base** of production rules, from a domain-independent **inference engine** that applies those rules to derive conclusions from case data. The canonical form is a rule of the shape IF *conjunction of conditions* THEN *conclusion or action*, often annotated with a **certainty factor**: MYCIN, developed by Edward Shortliffe at Stanford (1972–76) to recommend antibiotic therapy, attached CF values in $[-1,1]$ to roughly 600 rules and combined them via an ad hoc calculus (not Bayesian probability, deliberately — Shortliffe argued clinicians' hedged judgments did not decompose into consistent priors and likelihoods). XCON (originally R1), built by John McDermott at CMU and deployed by Digital Equipment Corporation from 1980, instead used forward-chaining production rules (in OPS5) to configure VAX computer orders — some 10,000 rules by the mid-1980s — and is usually cited as the first AI system with unambiguous, audited commercial ROI: DEC reported it was saving on the order of $25M/year by 1986 by eliminating configuration errors.

The mechanism has two chaining directions. Backward chaining (MYCIN) starts from a goal — "does the patient have organism $X$?" — and recursively seeks rules whose conclusion matches the goal, querying the user or working memory for unmet antecedents; this suits diagnosis, where the space of goals is small relative to the space of possible evidence. Forward chaining (XCON) starts from asserted facts and fires any rule whose antecedents are satisfied, adding new facts to working memory (a form of the Rete match algorithm, introduced by Charles Forgy in 1979 to make antecedent matching sub-linear in rule count rather than recomputed each cycle); this suits synthesis and configuration tasks, where facts accumulate toward a constructed artifact. Both architectures make the rule base declarative and separately editable — the sales pitch of "knowledge engineering" as a discipline distinct from programming.

The open question the literature never resolved before the field's second AI winter (circa 1987) is the **knowledge acquisition bottleneck**: eliciting rules from experts is slow, rules interact combinatorially so adding rule $n+1$ can silently invalidate conclusions licensed by rules $1..n$, and certainty-factor combination lacks the axiomatic guarantees of probability theory (Heckerman later showed CF-style combination functions correspond to Bayesian updating only under restrictive independence assumptions). Whether hand-coded symbolic rules or statistically learned models generalize better on realistic case mixes was the empirical crux of the "knowledge vs. learning" bet named in this concept, and it remained unsettled by argument alone.

A concrete project: reconstruct a small MYCIN-style or XCON-style rule base (50–200 rules) for a narrow, well-documented domain — e.g., antibiotic selection from a published clinical guideline, or a simplified hardware-configuration task — implement both backward- and forward-chaining inference engines from scratch (no framework), and instrument rule-firing traces to measure how conclusions change as rules are added or reordered. Compare the rule base's coverage and error rate against a simple statistical or ML classifier trained on the same case data, using a shared held-out test set.

A write-up should report: the branching factor and average proof-tree depth actually observed in backward chaining versus the number of forward-chaining cycles to fixpoint; the frequency and character of rule interactions (which additions changed prior conclusions, and why); a quantified comparison of expert-system accuracy versus the statistical baseline, including cases where each approach fails and why; and an explicit account of the knowledge-acquisition cost (elicitation time, rule count, maintenance burden under simulated rule changes) as a first-class result, not an appendix — since that cost, not raw accuracy, is what the historical record suggests ultimately governed the technology's adoption and decline.

### backprop_1986 (0.0s)

## Backprop 1986

**Definition.** Backpropagation is an algorithm for computing the exact gradient of a scalar loss function with respect to every weight in a feedforward neural network, using a single forward pass and a single backward pass whose combined cost is $O(W)$ for a network with $W$ weights — the same order as evaluating the network once. Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams's 1986 *Nature* paper ("Learning representations by back-propagating errors") did not invent the underlying technique — it is a special case of reverse-mode automatic differentiation, independently derived by Werbos (1974), Linnainmaa (1970) in the context of the chain rule for computational graphs, and Parker — but it demonstrated, with concrete multi-layer experiments, that the method could learn useful internal (hidden-layer) representations, ending the stagnation that followed Minsky and Papert's 1969 critique of single-layer perceptrons.

**Mechanism.** Consider a network with layers $a^{(l)} = \sigma(z^{(l)})$, $z^{(l)} = W^{(l)} a^{(l-1)} + b^{(l)}$, and loss $L$. Define the error term $\delta^{(l)} = \partial L / \partial z^{(l)}$. The chain rule gives the recurrence
$$
\delta^{(l)} = \left( (W^{(l+1)})^\top \delta^{(l+1)} \right) \odot \sigma'(z^{(l)}), \qquad \delta^{(L)} = \nabla_a L \odot \sigma'(z^{(L)}),
$$
with $\odot$ the Hadamard product, from which the weight gradients follow directly as $\partial L / \partial W^{(l)} = \delta^{(l)} (a^{(l-1)})^\top$. This recurrence is exactly reverse-mode differentiation applied to the computational graph of the network: each layer's local Jacobian is applied once, backward, so the total cost stays linear in the number of edges rather than growing combinatorially as in naive forward-mode differentiation of a deep composition. The 1986 contribution was to pair this with stochastic gradient descent and momentum, and to show empirically — on tasks such as parity and encoder-decoder problems — that the hidden units organized into meaningful feature detectors rather than collapsing to trivial solutions.

**Literature context.** Backpropagation's correctness as a gradient computation is not in question — it is exact differential calculus, not an approximation — but its optimization-theoretic behavior on non-convex loss surfaces remained poorly understood for decades. The vanishing/exploding gradient problem (Hochreiter, 1991) showed that $\delta^{(l)}$ can shrink or grow geometrically in $l$ when $\|\sigma'\|$ and $\|W\|$ are not controlled, motivating later work on initialization schemes (Glorot & Bengio, 2010), normalization layers, and residual connections, all of which can be read as engineering the spectral properties of the backward recurrence above. Open questions that remain active research areas include why gradient descent on the resulting non-convex, high-dimensional loss landscape reliably finds low-loss solutions, and to what extent backpropagation is a plausible model of learning in biological neural circuits, given its requirement of exact, symmetric weight transport between forward and backward passes.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement backpropagation from scratch (no autodiff library) for a small multilayer perceptron, and numerically verify each computed gradient component against a finite-difference estimate $\partial L/\partial w \approx [L(w+\epsilon) - L(w-\epsilon)]/2\epsilon$ across several layers and activation functions. Then instrument the norm $\|\delta^{(l)}\|$ per layer during training on a deep (8–15 layer) network with and without a normalization scheme, to reproduce the vanishing-gradient effect empirically.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should report the gradient-checking tolerances achieved (typically $<10^{-6}$ relative error), present the per-layer gradient-norm curves as evidence for or against vanishing gradients, and discuss how the chosen activation function and initialization scale affected the depth at which gradients became numerically negligible.

### deep_blue_1997 (0.0s)

## Deep Blue 1997

**Definition.** Deep Blue is the special-purpose chess computer, developed by IBM's Chess Team (Feng-hsiung Hsu, Murray Campbell, Joe Hoane, and collaborators), that defeated reigning world champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match on May 11, 1997 (score: 3.5–2.5). The system had no learning component in the sense that would come to dominate AI a decade later: it did not train a value function from data, did not adjust weights via gradient descent, and carried no relationship to [[backprop_1986]] beyond the field's shared ambition. Its intelligence was the product of exhaustive, parallelized tree search over massively engineered special-purpose hardware, guided by a hand-tuned evaluation function.

**Mechanism.** Deep Blue searched the game tree using alpha-beta pruning with iterative deepening, extended by domain-specific move-ordering heuristics, a large opening book, and an endgame tablebase for positions with few pieces remaining. The system reached a search depth of 6–8 plies in normal operation, extending selectively to 20+ plies along forcing lines (checks, captures, threats). What made this tractable was custom silicon: 480 VLSI chess chips, each implementing move generation and position evaluation in hardware, distributed across 30 IBM RS/6000 SP nodes, together evaluating roughly 200 million positions per second. The evaluation function itself — scoring material, king safety, pawn structure, piece mobility — was hand-crafted and hand-tuned by grandmaster consultant Joel Benjamin and the engineering team, encoding chess knowledge explicitly as weighted features rather than learning it from game corpora.

**Literature context.** Deep Blue sits at the endpoint of a research program traceable to Claude Shannon's 1950 paper "Programming a Computer for Playing Chess" and Turing's earlier theoretical treatment, and it validated the "brute-force" search paradigm associated with Ken Thompson's Belle and later Hsu's own doctoral work (ChipTest, Deep Thought). Its victory is frequently misread in popular accounts as an early triumph of "AI" in the sense later associated with learned representations; the more precise historical claim, made by Campbell, Hoane, and Hsu in their 2002 *Artificial Intelligence* retrospective, is that domain-specific search plus custom hardware substituted for learned evaluation, and that this substitution was specific to chess's tractable branching factor (~35) and well-understood evaluation features. What remains open in the literature is a rigorous account of why search-plus-heuristics generalizes poorly to domains like Go, where the branching factor (~250) and the absence of a reliable static evaluation function made brute force insufficient until Monte Carlo tree search combined with learned value/policy networks (AlphaGo, 2016) closed the gap — a comparison that clarifies exactly what Deep Blue's approach could and could not do.

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could reimplement a scaled-down alpha-beta engine (e.g., in Python or Rust with a hand-tuned evaluation function) and empirically measure how playing strength (via Elo estimation against a reference engine such as Stockfish at reduced depth) scales with search depth versus with evaluation-function sophistication, holding hardware budget fixed — directly testing Deep Blue's own design tradeoff.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should report the search algorithm and pruning strategy used, the branching-factor and depth statistics observed, the Elo or win-rate results as a function of the depth/evaluation tradeoff, and an explicit discussion of why this approach does or does not transfer to a second domain (e.g., a simplified Go or Othello variant) tested under the same framework.

### imagenet_2012 (0.0s)

## Imagenet 2012

**Definition.** ImageNet-2012 refers to the ILSVRC-2012 classification challenge and its winning submission, AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton), an eight-layer convolutional neural network trained on 1.2 million labeled images across 1,000 categories. AlexNet reduced top-5 error from 26.2% (best prior, hand-engineered feature pipeline) to 15.3%, a margin so large it ended the dominance of SIFT/HOG-plus-SVM pipelines in computer vision within roughly two years. The result is not a single algorithmic novelty but a demonstration that a specific *conjunction* of ingredients — deep hierarchical feature learning, GPU-parallelized training, a dataset with sufficient scale and label quality, and several regularization/optimization tricks — crosses a threshold that none of the ingredients crosses alone.

**Mechanism.** The network stacks five convolutional layers (with max-pooling and local response normalization) followed by three fully connected layers, trained via stochastic gradient descent with momentum and backpropagation. Three engineering choices were decisive rather than incidental: (1) ReLU activations ($f(x) = \max(0, x)$) replaced saturating sigmoid/tanh units, avoiding vanishing gradients and yielding faster convergence; (2) dropout in the fully connected layers curbed overfitting given ~60 million parameters trained on a dataset that, while large, was still finite relative to model capacity; (3) training was split across two GPUs with parameter communication only at specific layers, a workaround for memory limits that also had the incidental effect of encouraging feature specialization across the two streams. None of these techniques was individually new — ReLUs and dropout both predate 2012 in the literature — but their combination at this scale, on this dataset, had not been demonstrated to work.

**Literature context.** The open question the result raised, and which the field spent the subsequent decade answering, is *why* depth plus scale plus compute produces qualitatively better representations rather than merely quantitatively better ones — the features learned in early convolutional layers turned out to transfer across unrelated vision tasks (Yosinski et al., 2014), suggesting the network was learning something closer to general visual primitives than task-specific discriminants. This motivated the empirical scaling-law literature (Hestness et al., 2017; Kaplan et al., 2020) that treats dataset size, parameter count, and compute budget as jointly predictive of performance — a claim that is still debated in its generality outside vision and language domains, and whose mechanistic explanation (why do overparameterized networks generalize at all?) remains an active area of statistical learning theory.

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable graduate project is a controlled ablation: reimplement a scaled-down AlexNet on a subset of ImageNet (or CIFAR-100 as a lower-compute proxy) and independently toggle ReLU vs. tanh, dropout on/off, and single- vs. multi-GPU-equivalent batch normalization, measuring top-5 error and convergence epochs for each configuration. A second, more open-ended project replicates the Yosinski transferability experiment on a modern architecture (e.g., a small ViT) to test whether the layer-wise generality-to-specificity gradient observed in 2014 still holds under attention-based rather than convolutional inductive biases.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should isolate which ablated component contributed the largest single error-rate change, report training curves (not just final accuracy) to distinguish optimization effects from generalization effects, and explicitly state whether the transfer-learning gradient across layers replicates in the alternative architecture — a negative or partial result here is itself a publishable finding about the specificity of the 2012 mechanism.

### alphago_2016 (0.0s)

## Alphago 2016

**Definition.** AlphaGo is a reinforcement-learning system that combines deep convolutional neural networks with Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) to select moves in the game of Go, a two-player perfect-information game with a state space estimated at roughly $2.1 \times 10^{170}$ legal positions — far beyond the reach of the alpha-beta search and handcrafted evaluation functions that had solved chess. In March 2016, a version of AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, a 9-dan professional, 4 games to 1, a result many researchers had expected to remain a decade away.

**Mechanism.** AlphaGo's architecture separates *policy* from *value*. A policy network $p_\sigma(a \mid s)$, trained first by supervised learning on roughly 30 million human expert moves and then refined by self-play policy-gradient reinforcement learning, outputs a probability distribution over moves $a$ given board state $s$. A separate value network $v_\theta(s)$, trained by regression on the outcomes of self-play games generated by the policy network, estimates the probability of winning from $s$ without playing the game out to completion. Both networks reduce the two intractable dimensions of Go search: the policy network narrows the *breadth* of the tree (which moves to consider), and the value network truncates its *depth* (when to stop simulating and estimate instead). These estimates guide an MCTS procedure that asymptotically converges toward optimal play, in the sense that as simulation count grows the tree-search value estimate converges to the game-theoretic minimax value — the same guarantee underlying MCTS more generally, here made practical by using the networks as a prior rather than search alone. This design is inseparable from the sequence [[imagenet_2012]]: AlphaGo's policy and value networks reuse the deep CNN architectures whose feature-extraction capacity was established on natural images, applied here to a $19 \times 19$ board encoded as a stack of binary feature planes.

**Literature context.** Silver et al. (2016, *Nature*) established that this policy-plus-value-plus-search architecture could reach superhuman strength using human game records as an initial prior. The open question the paper left — how much of that strength depended on human data versus self-play alone — was answered within two years: AlphaGo Zero (2017) and AlphaZero (2018) removed human game data entirely, learning purely from self-play with a single combined network and generalizing the approach to chess and shogi. This raises the still-active research question of what inductive biases (board symmetry, MCTS as a structured prior, the choice of self-play curriculum) are doing the essential work, versus what is incidental to Go specifically.

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable project is to reproduce the core policy-value-MCTS loop on a reduced board (e.g., $9 \times 9$ Go, or a simpler combinatorial game like Hex), measuring Elo-rated playing strength as a function of (a) MCTS simulation budget per move, (b) training self-play games, and (c) presence or absence of the value network (pure policy rollout vs. value-guided search). Open-source frameworks (e.g., KataGo, or minimal reimplementations such as MuGo) make this feasible on a single GPU within a graduate course timeframe.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should report Elo or win-rate curves against a fixed baseline opponent across each ablation, not final win/loss records alone, and should explicitly characterize the diminishing-returns regime of simulation budget — the point past which additional MCTS rollouts no longer improve the win rate — since this scaling behavior, not any single victory, is the quantity of theoretical interest.

### transformer_2017 (20.4s)

## Transformer 2017

The Transformer, introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), replaces recurrence and convolution with a single primitive — scaled dot-product self-attention — as the mechanism for modeling dependencies within a sequence. This is a genuine architectural and complexity result, not merely an engineering convenience: it changes the computational graph's depth from $O(n)$ sequential steps (as in an RNN) to $O(1)$ sequential steps, at the cost of $O(n^2 d)$ work per layer, where $n$ is sequence length and $d$ is representation dimension.

**Mechanism.** Given an input sequence embedded as $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, the model computes three learned linear projections, queries $Q = XW^Q$, keys $K = XW^K$, and values $V = XW^V$, and forms attention weights via

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
$$

where $d_k$ is the key dimension and the scaling factor $\sqrt{d_k}$ prevents the softmax from saturating as $d_k$ grows (the dot products' variance scales with $d_k$ under standard initialization, so unscaled logits push gradients toward zero). Multi-head attention runs $h$ such operations in parallel on projected subspaces and concatenates the results, letting the model attend to different relational structures simultaneously. Because self-attention has no inherent notion of order, positional information is injected explicitly — in the original paper via sinusoidal functions $PE_{(pos,2i)} = \sin(pos/10000^{2i/d})$, $PE_{(pos,2i+1)} = \cos(pos/10000^{2i/d})$, chosen so that relative positions correspond to a fixed linear transformation, though later work (learned embeddings, rotary embeddings, ALiBi) treats this as an open design axis rather than a settled choice. Each encoder/decoder block wraps attention and a position-wise feed-forward network in residual connections and layer normalization, which the literature has since shown are load-bearing for trainability at depth, not stylistic.

**Literature context.** What is well established: the architecture parallelizes across sequence positions during training, achieves superior long-range dependency modeling versus LSTMs on benchmarks reported in the original paper, and — critically for the field's trajectory — scales predictably with data and compute (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022), which is the property that makes it the substrate of every subsequent large language model, including the reasoning systems descended from it. What remains open and actively contested: the $O(n^2)$ attention cost is a genuine scaling bottleneck, motivating a large family of approximations (sparse attention, linear attention, state-space hybrids such as Mamba) whose trade-offs against exact attention on long-context tasks are not fully characterized; the mechanistic-interpretability question of what attention heads actually compute is only partially answered (induction heads, e.g., are documented, but a general theory is absent); and whether attention is doing something computationally distinctive versus other architectures achieving comparable scaling laws is unresolved.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement scaled dot-product attention and a two-layer encoder block from the equations above (no library abstraction), train it on a small sequence task (e.g., copy or sort a token sequence), and empirically measure how validation loss and required training steps change as $n$ grows, holding parameter count fixed — testing directly whether the $O(n^2)$ term becomes the binding constraint before model capacity does.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the exact attention variant and positional scheme used, report wall-clock and FLOP cost alongside accuracy (not accuracy alone), and explicitly distinguish claims supported by the experiment from claims taken from the literature — particularly regarding scaling behavior, where a single small-scale run cannot establish trends that only emerge across orders of magnitude of compute.
