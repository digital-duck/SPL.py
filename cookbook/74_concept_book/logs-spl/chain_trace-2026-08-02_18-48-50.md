### derivative (0.2s)

## Derivative

The derivative of a function measures how fast its output changes relative to its input at any given point. More precisely, the derivative of $f$ at $x = a$ is the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to zero:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

Geometrically, this limit is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$. When the limit exists, $f$ is said to be **differentiable** at $a$. The derivative function $f'(x)$ gives you that slope at every point simultaneously.

**Worked Example.** Find the derivative of $f(x) = x^2$ at $x = 3$.

Apply the definition directly:

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(6 + h) = 6$$

So the slope of $y = x^2$ at $x = 3$ is $6$, and the tangent line there is $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$.

In practice, the power rule $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$ lets you skip the limit algebra: $f'(x) = 2x$, so $f'(3) = 6$ immediately.

**Problem-Solving Application.** A company's daily revenue (in thousands of dollars) is modeled by $R(x) = -2x^2 + 80x$, where $x$ is the number of units sold. To maximize revenue, you need the value of $x$ where the rate of change flips from positive to negative — that is, where $R'(x) = 0$.

Differentiate: $R'(x) = -4x + 80$. Setting this equal to zero gives $x = 20$. Since $R''(x) = -4 < 0$, the function is concave down, confirming this is a maximum. Selling 20 units yields peak daily revenue of $R(20) = -2(400) + 80(20) = \$800{,}000$.

This pattern — differentiate, set equal to zero, check concavity — is the core algorithm for optimization problems across physics, economics, and engineering. The derivative transforms a vague question ("where is the peak?") into a precise algebraic equation you can solve.

### absolute_extrema (0.2s)

## Absolute Extrema

The **absolute maximum** of a function $f$ on a domain $D$ is the largest output value $f(c)$ such that $f(c) \geq f(x)$ for every $x$ in $D$. The **absolute minimum** is the smallest: $f(c) \leq f(x)$ for all $x \in D$. Together these are called **absolute extrema** (or *global* extrema). They answer the question: "Over the entire domain, where is the function biggest, and where is it smallest?"

The **Extreme Value Theorem** provides the foundational guarantee: if $f$ is continuous on a closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on that interval. This theorem tells you the extrema *exist*; the challenge is finding them.

**Finding absolute extrema on $[a, b]$ — the closed-interval method:**

1. Find all critical numbers in $(a, b)$: solve $f'(x) = 0$ and note where $f'(x)$ is undefined.
2. Evaluate $f$ at each critical number and at both endpoints $a$ and $b$.
3. The largest value is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum.

**Worked example.** Find the absolute extrema of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 1$ on $[-1, 3]$.

*Step 1.* $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x - 2)$, so $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$.

*Step 2.* Evaluate at the critical numbers and endpoints:

$$f(-1) = -1 - 3 + 1 = -3$$
$$f(0) = 0 - 0 + 1 = 1$$
$$f(2) = 8 - 12 + 1 = -3$$
$$f(3) = 27 - 27 + 1 = 1$$

*Step 3.* The absolute maximum is $1$, attained at $x = 0$ and $x = 3$. The absolute minimum is $-3$, attained at $x = -1$ and $x = 2$.

**Application.** A manufacturer's weekly profit (in dollars) is modeled by $P(x) = -2x^2 + 80x - 300$, where $x \in [10, 30]$ is units produced. To maximize profit, apply the closed-interval method: $P'(x) = -4x + 80 = 0$ gives $x = 20$. Checking $P(10) = 300$, $P(20) = 500$, $P(30) = 300$ shows the absolute maximum profit is **\$500** at 20 units. Notice the endpoints matter — ignoring them could lead you to miss that producing at the boundary is equally suboptimal in both directions here.

### critical_point (0.2s)

## Critical Point

A **critical point** of a function $f(x)$ is any value $x = c$ in the domain of $f$ where either $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist. The geometric intuition is direct: at a peak or valley, the tangent line is perfectly horizontal — slope zero. Critical points are the only candidates where a continuous function can achieve a local maximum or minimum, which makes finding them the first step in virtually every optimization problem.

**Worked Example**

Find and classify the critical points of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5$.

**Step 1 — Find critical points.** Compute the derivative and set it equal to zero:

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 0 \implies x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0 \implies (x-3)(x+1) = 0$$

Critical points: $x = -1$ and $x = 3$.

**Step 2 — Classify.** Use the second derivative test. Compute $f''(x) = 6x - 6$.

- $f''(-1) = -12 < 0$ → concave down → **local maximum** at $x = -1$, where $f(-1) = 10$.
- $f''(3) = 12 > 0$ → concave up → **local minimum** at $x = 3$, where $f(3) = -22$.

If the second derivative were zero at a critical point, you'd fall back to analyzing the sign change of $f'$ directly (the first derivative test).

**Problem-Solving Application**

Critical points drive real optimization. Suppose a farmer has 200 m of fencing to enclose a rectangular pen against a barn wall — so only three sides need fencing. Letting $x$ be the width, the length is $200 - 2x$, and the area is:

$$A(x) = x(200 - 2x) = 200x - 2x^2, \quad 0 < x < 100$$

Setting $A'(x) = 200 - 4x = 0$ gives the single critical point $x = 50$. Since $A''(50) = -4 < 0$, this is a maximum: a $50\ \text{m} \times 100\ \text{m}$ pen yields the greatest area of $5{,}000\ \text{m}^2$.

The workflow is always the same: express the quantity to optimize as a function of one variable, differentiate, solve $f' = 0$, classify the result. Critical points turn an open-ended question — "what's the best configuration?" — into a tractable algebra problem.

### local_extrema (0.2s)

## Local Extrema

A **local maximum** of a function $f$ is a point $x = c$ where $f(c) \geq f(x)$ for all $x$ near $c$. A **local minimum** is a point where $f(c) \leq f(x)$ for all $x$ nearby. Together these are called **local extrema** — peaks and valleys of the graph when you zoom in close enough. Unlike global extrema, which compare a value against the entire domain, a local extremum only needs to win in its neighborhood.

The first tool for finding them is the **First Derivative Test**. If $f'(c) = 0$ (or $f'(c)$ is undefined), then $c$ is a **critical point** — a candidate for a local extremum. Whether it actually is one depends on how the derivative changes sign around $c$:

- If $f'$ changes from **positive to negative** at $c$, then $f$ has a **local maximum** there.
- If $f'$ changes from **negative to positive** at $c$, then $f$ has a **local minimum** there.
- If $f'$ does not change sign, the critical point is neither (a saddle or inflection point).

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$.

Compute the derivative: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$.

Setting $f'(x) = 0$ gives critical points $x = -1$ and $x = 1$.

- For $x < -1$: $f'(x) > 0$ (increasing)
- For $-1 < x < 1$: $f'(x) < 0$ (decreasing)
- For $x > 1$: $f'(x) > 0$ (increasing)

So $f'$ changes **positive → negative** at $x = -1$: local maximum with $f(-1) = 2$.  
And $f'$ changes **negative → positive** at $x = 1$: local minimum with $f(1) = -2$.

**Applying the Concept.** When you need to optimize something — minimize cost, maximize revenue, find the best angle for a projectile — local extrema are your first targets. The procedure is always the same: (1) differentiate, (2) find critical points, (3) apply the First Derivative Test (or the **Second Derivative Test**: if $f''(c) < 0$ the point is a local max; if $f''(c) > 0$ it is a local min). The second test is faster algebraically but fails when $f''(c) = 0$, in which case you fall back to the first.

Practice: find all local extrema of $f(x) = x^4 - 8x^2 + 3$, then verify with a quick sketch to build intuition for what the algebra is telling you.

### extreme_value_theorem (0.2s)

## Extreme Value Theorem

**Definition.** The Extreme Value Theorem (EVT) guarantees the existence of extreme values on a closed, bounded interval. Formally:

> If $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ attains an absolute maximum value $f(c)$ and an absolute minimum value $f(d)$ for some $c, d \in [a, b]$.

Both conditions are load-bearing. Drop continuity — say $f(x) = 1/x$ on $[-1, 1]$ — and the function blows up with no maximum. Drop the closed endpoints — consider $f(x) = x$ on $(0, 1)$ — and the supremum $1$ is never actually reached. The EVT is an existence result: it tells you extreme values are there, not where they are. Finding them requires a separate procedure.

**Finding absolute extrema.** Because a continuous function on $[a, b]$ cannot "escape" to infinity or sneak past an endpoint, every candidate for an extreme value falls into one of two categories: a **critical point** in the interior (where $f'(x) = 0$ or $f'(x)$ is undefined) or an **endpoint** ($a$ or $b$). The procedure is straightforward:

1. Verify $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$.
2. Find all $x \in (a, b)$ where $f'(x) = 0$ or $f'(x)$ does not exist.
3. Evaluate $f$ at every critical point and at both endpoints.
4. The largest output is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum.

**Worked example.** Find the absolute extrema of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 1$ on $[-1, 3]$.

$f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous everywhere — EVT applies.

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x - 2)$$

Critical points: $x = 0$ and $x = 2$, both in $(-1, 3)$.

| $x$ | $f(x)$ |
|-----|--------|
| $-1$ | $(-1)^3 - 3(1) + 1 = -3$ |
| $0$  | $0 - 0 + 1 = 1$ |
| $2$  | $8 - 12 + 1 = -3$ |
| $3$  | $27 - 27 + 1 = 1$ |

**Absolute maximum:** $f = 1$, attained at $x = 0$ and $x = 3$. **Absolute minimum:** $f = -3$, attained at $x = -1$ and $x = 2$.

**Problem-solving application.** The EVT underpins every optimization problem with a finite, continuous domain — from minimizing material cost over a bounded design parameter to maximizing revenue within a production capacity. Whenever a problem says "on the interval $[a, b]$," invoke the EVT first to confirm a solution must exist, then use the critical-point checklist to find it. Skipping the existence check risks spending effort optimizing a function that has no attainable extreme value at all.

### fermats_theorem (0.2s)

## Fermats Theorem

**Definition**

Fermat's Little Theorem states that if $p$ is a prime number and $a$ is any integer not divisible by $p$, then

$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$$

A compact equivalent form: $a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}$ holds for *every* integer $a$, including multiples of $p$. The theorem is a foundational result in modular arithmetic — it tells you that raising an integer to a prime-minus-one power and dividing by that prime always leaves remainder 1.

**Why it is true** (sketch): The integers $\{a, 2a, 3a, \ldots, (p-1)a\}$ reduced mod $p$ are a permutation of $\{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\}$, because $p$ is prime and $p \nmid a$. Multiplying both sets together gives $(p-1)! \cdot a^{p-1} \equiv (p-1)! \pmod{p}$. Since $(p-1)!$ shares no factor with $p$, we cancel it to get $a^{p-1} \equiv 1$.

**Worked example**

Take $p = 7$, $a = 3$. The theorem predicts $3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$.

$$3^1 = 3,\quad 3^2 = 9 \equiv 2,\quad 3^3 \equiv 6,\quad 3^4 \equiv 4,\quad 3^5 \equiv 5,\quad 3^6 \equiv 15 \equiv 1 \pmod{7} \checkmark$$

**Problem-solving application: fast modular exponentiation and primality testing**

The theorem immediately simplifies exponent reduction. To compute $7^{103} \pmod{11}$, note $p = 11$ so $7^{10} \equiv 1$. Write $103 = 10 \cdot 10 + 3$, giving $7^{103} = (7^{10})^{10} \cdot 7^3 \equiv 1^{10} \cdot 343 \equiv 343 \pmod{11}$. Since $343 = 31 \cdot 11 + 2$, the answer is $\mathbf{2}$.

The theorem is also the basis for RSA encryption. In RSA, a message $m$ is encrypted as $m^e \bmod n$ and decrypted by raising to a carefully chosen inverse exponent $d$ satisfying $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{(p-1)(q-1)}$ — Fermat's theorem guarantees the round-trip returns $m$.

```python
# Quick verification in Python
p = 11
a = 7
print(pow(a, p - 1, p))   # → 1, confirming Fermat's theorem

# Practical use: fast modular inverse when p is prime
def mod_inverse(a, p):
    return pow(a, p - 2, p)   # a^(p-2) ≡ a^(-1) mod p

print(mod_inverse(3, 7))   # → 5, because 3 × 5 = 15 ≡ 1 (mod 7)
```

The key habit when you see large exponents mod a prime is to immediately reduce the exponent modulo $p - 1$ before computing anything — Fermat's theorem makes that legal.

### limits_at_infinity (0.2s)

## Limits At Infinity

**Definition.** A limit at infinity describes how a function behaves as its input grows without bound. We write $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ to mean that $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to the value $L$ as $x$ increases without limit. Similarly, $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$ tracks behavior as $x$ decreases without bound. When such a limit exists and equals $L$, the horizontal line $y = L$ is called a **horizontal asymptote** of the graph.

**Key theorem.** For any rational exponent $n > 0$,

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{x^n} = 0$$

This single fact powers most limit-at-infinity calculations: any term with $x$ in the denominator vanishes as $x \to \infty$.

**Worked example.** Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5x + 1}{2x^2 + 4}$.

Direct substitution gives $\infty/\infty$, an indeterminate form. The standard technique is to **divide every term by the highest power of $x$ in the expression**, here $x^2$:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5x + 1}{2x^2 + 4} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3 - \dfrac{5}{x} + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{2 + \dfrac{4}{x^2}}$$

As $x \to \infty$, every term with $x$ in the denominator goes to zero:

$$= \frac{3 - 0 + 0}{2 + 0} = \frac{3}{2}$$

The function has a horizontal asymptote at $y = \tfrac{3}{2}$.

**Problem-solving pattern.** For rational functions, comparing the degrees of numerator and denominator gives a quick read:

- Degree of numerator $<$ degree of denominator: limit is $0$.
- Degrees equal: limit is the ratio of leading coefficients.
- Degree of numerator $>$ degree of denominator: limit is $\pm\infty$ (no horizontal asymptote).

For non-rational functions — exponentials, logarithms, or combinations — apply the same divide-by-highest-power idea, or reach for L'Hôpital's Rule when the form remains $\infty/\infty$ or $0/0$ after algebraic simplification.

**Practice.** Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sqrt{9x^2 + 1}}{x + 2}$. Hint: factor $x^2$ out of the square root carefully, noting that $\sqrt{x^2} = x$ for $x > 0$. The answer is $3$.

### rolles_theorem (17.7s)

## Rolle's Theorem

**Definition.** Rolle's Theorem is a foundational result in calculus that guarantees the existence of a "flat spot" on a smooth curve whenever the curve starts and ends at the same height.

**Theorem (Rolle, 1691).** Let $f$ be a function satisfying three conditions on a closed interval $[a, b]$:
1. $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$,
2. $f$ is differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$,
3. $f(a) = f(b)$.

Then there exists at least one point $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f'(c) = 0$.

**Why it must be true.** By the Extreme Value Theorem, $f$ attains an absolute maximum $M$ and an absolute minimum $m$ on $[a, b]$. If $M = m$, then $f$ is constant and $f'(c) = 0$ everywhere—trivially satisfied. Otherwise, at least one of $M$ or $m$ is achieved at an interior point $c \in (a, b)$. At an interior extremum of a differentiable function, the tangent line must be horizontal: if $f'(c) \neq 0$, the function would be rising or falling there, contradicting it being a local max or min. Therefore $f'(c) = 0$.

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 4x$ on $[-2, 2]$.

Check the three conditions:
- $f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous and differentiable everywhere. ✓
- $f(-2) = -8 + 8 = 0$ and $f(2) = 8 - 8 = 0$, so $f(a) = f(b)$. ✓

By Rolle's Theorem, at least one $c \in (-2, 2)$ satisfies $f'(c) = 0$.

Compute: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 4$. Setting this to zero:
$$3x^2 - 4 = 0 \implies x = \pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \approx \pm 1.155.$$

Both values lie inside $(-2, 2)$, confirming the theorem—and revealing a local maximum near $x \approx -1.155$ and a local minimum near $x \approx 1.155$.

**Problem-solving application.** Rolle's Theorem can be used to prove that an equation has exactly one real root. Suppose you have already found one root $r$ of $f(x) = 0$ and you want to rule out a second root $s$. Assume for contradiction that $f(s) = 0$ as well with $s \neq r$. Then $f(r) = f(s) = 0$, and by Rolle's Theorem some $c$ between them satisfies $f'(c) = 0$. If you can show $f'(x) \neq 0$ on all of $(r, s)$ (for example, by proving $f'$ has constant sign), the contradiction establishes uniqueness. This technique—often called the *Rolle uniqueness argument*—appears frequently in analysis, numerical methods, and differential equations.

### mean_value_theorem (20.8s)

## Mean Value Theorem

**Definition.** The Mean Value Theorem (MVT) is one of the central results of differential calculus. It formalizes the intuition that if you drive 120 miles in 2 hours, your speedometer must have read exactly 60 mph at some moment — even if you sped up and slowed down the whole way.

**Theorem (Mean Value Theorem).** Let $f$ be continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$. Then there exists at least one point $c \in (a, b)$ such that

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.$$

The right-hand side is the slope of the **secant line** connecting $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$. The theorem guarantees that somewhere between $a$ and $b$, the tangent line to $f$ is parallel to that secant line.

The proof rests on Rolle's Theorem (the special case where $f(a) = f(b)$, so the secant slope is zero), applied to a cleverly shifted auxiliary function.

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - x$ on $[0, 2]$. The secant slope is

$$\frac{f(2) - f(0)}{2 - 0} = \frac{(8 - 2) - 0}{2} = 3.$$

Set $f'(c) = 3c^2 - 1 = 3$, which gives $c^2 = \tfrac{4}{3}$, so $c = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.155$. This value lies in $(0, 2)$, confirming the theorem.

**Problem-solving applications.** The MVT is most useful as an estimation and bounding tool.

*Bounding change.* If $|f'(x)| \leq M$ for all $x \in [a, b]$, then $|f(b) - f(a)| \leq M(b - a)$. This is the standard way to control how much a function can change over an interval — essential in numerical analysis and error estimation.

*Proving inequalities.* To show $\ln(1 + x) \leq x$ for $x \geq 0$, apply MVT to $f(t) = \ln(1 + t)$ on $[0, x]$: there exists $c \in (0, x)$ with $\ln(1 + x) = \frac{x}{1 + c}$. Since $1 + c > 1$, the right side is strictly less than $x$.

*Speed trap reasoning.* If a car's position function satisfies $s(0) = 0$ and $s(3) = 225$ miles, MVT guarantees the car exceeded 74 mph at some instant — legally actionable even without continuous tracking.

The MVT's power is in these one-line arguments: continuity plus differentiability turns average behavior into a concrete guarantee about instantaneous behavior.

### concavity (17.2s)

## Concavity

**Definition.** A function $f$ is *concave up* on an interval if its graph curves like a bowl opening upward — tangent lines lie *below* the curve. It is *concave down* if the graph curves like an arch — tangent lines lie *above* the curve. The second derivative gives a clean algebraic test: if $f''(x) > 0$ on an interval, $f$ is concave up there; if $f''(x) < 0$, it is concave down. A point where the concavity changes sign is called an *inflection point*.

**Why it matters beyond the definition.** Concavity tells you how a rate of change is itself changing. A function can be increasing but slowing down (concave down) or increasing and speeding up (concave up) — two situations that look identical in a table of values but have very different implications in practice, from stock prices to drug absorption curves.

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 1$.

Compute the second derivative:
$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9, \qquad f''(x) = 6x - 12.$$

Set $f''(x) = 0$: $\;6x - 12 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 2$.

Check signs: for $x < 2$, $f''(x) < 0$ (concave down); for $x > 2$, $f''(x) > 0$ (concave up). Since the concavity changes at $x = 2$, that is an inflection point. The point is $f(2) = 8 - 24 + 18 + 1 = 3$, so $(2, 3)$ is the inflection point.

**Problem-solving application.** Concavity sharpens the second-derivative test for local extrema. Suppose you find a critical point $x = c$ where $f'(c) = 0$. If $f''(c) > 0$, the function is concave up at $c$, so it has a *local minimum* there. If $f''(c) < 0$, it has a *local maximum*. For the example above, $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = 1$ and $x = 3$. Since $f''(1) = -6 < 0$, there is a local max at $x = 1$; since $f''(3) = 6 > 0$, there is a local min at $x = 3$. This is more efficient than checking sign changes in $f'$ manually, especially when $f'$ factors awkwardly. The one failure case: if $f''(c) = 0$, the test is inconclusive and you must fall back to the first-derivative sign test.

### end_behavior (20.2s)

## End Behavior

**End behavior** describes what a function's output does as the input grows without bound in either direction — that is, what happens to $f(x)$ as $x \to +\infty$ and as $x \to -\infty$. Rather than fixating on the complicated middle of a graph, end behavior lets you immediately characterize both "tails."

For polynomial functions, end behavior is governed entirely by the **leading term** — the term with the highest power of $x$. Every other term becomes negligible when $x$ is very large. Specifically, two features of the leading term $ax^n$ determine the tails:

- **Degree $n$ (even vs. odd):** An even degree means both tails go in the *same* direction; odd degree means they go in *opposite* directions.
- **Leading coefficient $a$ (positive vs. negative):** A positive $a$ pulls the right tail upward; a negative $a$ pulls it downward.

**Worked Example**

Determine the end behavior of $f(x) = -2x^3 + 5x^2 - x + 7$.

The leading term is $-2x^3$. The degree is 3 (odd) and the leading coefficient is $-2$ (negative).

- As $x \to +\infty$: $-2x^3 \to -\infty$, so $f(x) \to -\infty$.
- As $x \to -\infty$: $-2x^3 \to +\infty$, so $f(x) \to +\infty$.

The graph rises on the left and falls on the right — a hallmark of odd-degree polynomials with a negative leading coefficient.

**Problem-Solving Application**

End behavior is a powerful first filter when sketching or analyzing graphs. Suppose you need to decide whether $g(x) = 3x^4 - 8x^3 + x - 2$ can have a horizontal asymptote or level off to a finite value. Because the leading term is $3x^4$ — even degree, positive coefficient — both tails head to $+\infty$. This immediately tells you $g$ has no horizontal asymptote and grows without bound, even before you find a single zero or turning point.

It also rules out certain scenarios: if a problem asks whether this function could satisfy $g(x) < 0$ for all large $x$, you can say no instantly, because the right tail goes to $+\infty$.

For rational functions, end behavior is found by comparing the degrees of numerator and denominator, which directly yields the horizontal asymptote (or the oblique asymptote when the numerator's degree exceeds the denominator's by exactly one). The logic is the same: isolate the dominant terms and let the rest vanish.

### mvt_corollaries (22.0s)

## MVT Corollaries

The Mean Value Theorem guarantees that a differentiable function has at least one point where its instantaneous rate of change equals its average rate of change over an interval. By itself, that is a useful existence result — but the theorem's real power comes from the corollaries it implies, which connect the sign and magnitude of a derivative to the global behavior of a function.

**The three essential corollaries.** Assume $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$.

1. **Zero derivative implies constant.** If $f'(x) = 0$ for every $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is constant on $[a, b]$.
2. **Equal derivatives differ by a constant.** If $f'(x) = g'(x)$ for every $x \in (a, b)$, then $f(x) = g(x) + C$ for some constant $C$.
3. **Sign of derivative determines monotonicity.** If $f'(x) > 0$ for every $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is strictly increasing on $[a, b]$; if $f'(x) < 0$, then $f$ is strictly decreasing.

Each corollary follows by the same short argument: pick any two points $x_1 < x_2$ in the interval, apply the MVT to get $f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1)$ for some $c$ between them, then read off what the hypothesis on $f'(c)$ forces about the sign of $f(x_2) - f(x_1)$.

**Worked example.** Suppose you know $f'(x) = 3x^2$ everywhere and $f(0) = 5$. By Corollary 2, any two antiderivatives of $3x^2$ differ by a constant. Since $g(x) = x^3$ satisfies $g'(x) = 3x^2$, we conclude $f(x) = x^3 + C$. Plugging in the initial condition: $5 = 0 + C$, so $f(x) = x^3 + 5$. The corollary is what licenses you to write "$+ C$" in every antiderivative problem — without it, you could not rule out wildly different functions sharing the same derivative.

**Problem-solving application.** Show that $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ has exactly one zero on $[2, 3]$. Compute $f(2) = 8 - 6 = 2 > 0$ and $f(3) = 27 - 9 = 18 > 0$ — the Intermediate Value Theorem alone does not help here. Instead, check $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x^2 - 1) > 0$ for all $x \in (2, 3)$. By Corollary 3, $f$ is strictly increasing on this interval, so it can cross zero at most once; since $f$ is positive at both endpoints, it crosses zero zero times on $[2, 3]$, confirming no root lies there. Monotonicity, not just sign, decides the answer.

### first_derivative_test (18.7s)

## First Derivative Test

A **critical point** of a function $f$ is any $x = c$ in its domain where either $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist. Critical points are *candidates* for local maxima and minima, but not every critical point is one. The First Derivative Test gives you a systematic way to decide which.

**The test.** Suppose $c$ is a critical point of $f$.

- If $f'$ changes from **positive to negative** at $c$ (the function rises to the left of $c$, then falls to the right), then $f$ has a **local maximum** at $c$.
- If $f'$ changes from **negative to positive** at $c$ (the function falls, then rises), then $f$ has a **local minimum** at $c$.
- If $f'$ does **not change sign** at $c$, then $f$ has **neither** a local maximum nor a local minimum there — the critical point is a "flat" inflection point.

The logic is geometric: a function can only switch from increasing to decreasing (or vice versa) by passing through a moment where its slope is zero or undefined. The sign change of $f'$ is exactly the fingerprint of that switch.

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$.

Find the derivative: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$.

Set $f'(x) = 0$: critical points at $x = -1$ and $x = 1$.

Build a sign chart by testing one value in each interval:

| Interval | Test value | $f'$ | $f$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $x < -1$ | $x = -2$ | $3(9) - 3 > 0$ | increasing |
| $-1 < x < 1$ | $x = 0$ | $-3 < 0$ | decreasing |
| $x > 1$ | $x = 2$ | $9 > 0$ | increasing |

At $x = -1$: $f'$ goes $+ \to -$, so **local maximum** at $f(-1) = 2$.
At $x = 1$: $f'$ goes $- \to +$, so **local minimum** at $f(1) = -2$.

**Applying it.** When solving optimization problems, the workflow is always the same: differentiate, find where the derivative is zero or undefined, then run the sign chart. The sign chart turns an algebraic calculation into a visual story about the function's behavior. Practice building the chart carefully — choosing test points just inside each interval, not at the boundaries — and you will rarely misclassify a critical point.

### horizontal_asymptote (15.4s)

## Horizontal Asymptote

**Definition.** A horizontal asymptote is a horizontal line $y = L$ that a function $f(x)$ approaches as $x$ grows without bound in the positive or negative direction. Formally, we say $y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote if

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$$

Unlike vertical asymptotes, which the function never crosses, a function *can* cross its horizontal asymptote — the asymptote only describes long-run behavior, not local behavior.

For rational functions $f(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$, three cases determine the horizontal asymptote based on the degrees of the numerator ($n$) and denominator ($m$):

- If $n < m$: the asymptote is $y = 0$
- If $n = m$: the asymptote is $y = \frac{\text{leading coefficient of } p}{\text{leading coefficient of } q}$
- If $n > m$: no horizontal asymptote exists (the function grows without bound)

**Worked example.** Find the horizontal asymptote of $f(x) = \dfrac{3x^2 - 5}{2x^2 + x + 1}$.

The numerator and denominator both have degree 2, so we're in the equal-degree case. Divide every term by $x^2$:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3 - \frac{5}{x^2}}{2 + \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}} = \frac{3 - 0}{2 + 0 + 0} = \frac{3}{2}$$

The horizontal asymptote is $y = \dfrac{3}{2}$.

**Problem-solving application.** Horizontal asymptotes appear naturally when modeling real-world saturation — population growth limited by resources, drug concentration approaching a steady state, or learning curves flattening over time.

Suppose a company's market share is modeled by $M(t) = \dfrac{80t}{t + 10}$, where $t$ is months since launch. To find the long-run market share:

$$\lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{80t}{t + 10} = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{80}{1 + \frac{10}{t}} = \frac{80}{1} = 80$$

The horizontal asymptote $y = 80$ tells us the market share approaches 80% but never exceeds it — a hard ceiling baked into the model's structure. Recognizing this allows analysts to ask whether 80% is a realistic ceiling or whether the model itself needs revision.

When evaluating asymptotes on exams, always divide by the highest power in the denominator — it mechanically kills lower-order terms and exposes the limiting ratio directly.

### oblique_asymptote (16.6s)

## Oblique Asymptote

When the degree of the numerator of a rational function is **exactly one greater** than the degree of the denominator, the function does not level off toward a horizontal line as $x \to \pm\infty$. Instead, it approaches a slanted line of the form $y = mx + b$. That line is called an **oblique asymptote** (sometimes a *slant asymptote*). Geometrically, the gap between the curve and the line shrinks to zero as $|x|$ grows, even though the curve never actually touches the line.

To find the oblique asymptote, perform **polynomial long division** on the rational function. Write $f(x) = \dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ and divide $p(x)$ by $q(x)$:

$$f(x) = (\text{quotient}) + \frac{\text{remainder}}{q(x)}$$

The quotient is a degree-1 polynomial, and it becomes the asymptote because the remainder term $\dfrac{r(x)}{q(x)} \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$.

**Worked example.** Find the oblique asymptote of $f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 - 3x + 5}{x - 1}$.

Divide $2x^2 - 3x + 5$ by $x - 1$:

$$2x^2 - 3x + 5 \div (x-1) = 2x - 1 + \frac{4}{x-1}$$

So $f(x) = 2x - 1 + \dfrac{4}{x-1}$. As $x \to \pm\infty$, the fraction $\dfrac{4}{x-1} \to 0$, and the curve hugs the line $y = 2x - 1$. That line is the oblique asymptote.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose you are analyzing cost efficiency: a company's average cost per unit is $A(x) = \dfrac{3x^2 + 10x + 4}{x + 2}$ dollars, where $x$ is production volume. Long division gives:

$$A(x) = 3x + 4 + \frac{-4}{x+2}$$

The oblique asymptote $y = 3x + 4$ tells you that at very high production volumes, average cost grows approximately linearly at a rate of \$3 per additional unit — the fixed overhead spread ($-4/(x+2)$) becomes negligible. This insight lets managers project long-run marginal cost without computing $A(x)$ for every $x$.

**Key check:** oblique asymptotes exist only when $\deg(p) = \deg(q) + 1$. If the degrees are equal, you get a horizontal asymptote; if the numerator's degree exceeds the denominator's by two or more, the end behavior is parabolic or higher, not a straight line.

### second_derivative_test (15.9s)

## Second Derivative Test

**Definition.** Once you know a function has a critical point (where $f'(x) = 0$ or $f'(x)$ is undefined), you still need to determine whether that point is a local minimum, a local maximum, or neither. The Second Derivative Test uses the curvature of the graph to answer that question without plotting points around the critical point.

**The test.** Let $c$ be a critical point of $f$ where $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c)$ exists. Then:

- If $f''(c) > 0$, the graph is **concave up** at $c$, so $f$ has a **local minimum** there.
- If $f''(c) < 0$, the graph is **concave down** at $c$, so $f$ has a **local maximum** there.
- If $f''(c) = 0$, the test is **inconclusive** — you must fall back to the First Derivative Test.

The intuition: a positive second derivative means $f'$ is increasing near $c$. Since $f'(c) = 0$ and the derivative is rising, the function was falling before $c$ and rising after — the signature of a valley. Negative $f''$ means the opposite: a hill.

**Worked example.** Find and classify the critical points of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$.

1. **Find critical points.** $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1) = 0$, so $x = 1$ and $x = -1$.

2. **Compute $f''$.** $f''(x) = 6x$.

3. **Apply the test.**
   - At $x = 1$: $f''(1) = 6 > 0$ → **local minimum**. Value: $f(1) = -2$.
   - At $x = -1$: $f''(-1) = -6 < 0$ → **local maximum**. Value: $f(-1) = 2$.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose a manufacturer's profit is modeled by $P(q) = -2q^3 + 9q^2 - 12q + 5$, where $q$ is thousands of units produced. To find the production level that locally maximizes profit:

Set $P'(q) = -6q^2 + 18q - 12 = -6(q-1)(q-2) = 0$, giving $q = 1$ and $q = 2$.

Compute $P''(q) = -12q + 18$.

- At $q = 1$: $P''(1) = 6 > 0$ → local **minimum** (avoid this).
- At $q = 2$: $P''(2) = -6 < 0$ → local **maximum** (target this).

The second derivative test identified $q = 2$ thousand units as the profit-maximizing output without tabulating surrounding values — a clean, algebraic shortcut that scales to far more complex models.

### curve_sketching (27.2s)

## Curve Sketching

Curve sketching is the practice of producing an accurate qualitative graph of a function by systematically extracting information from the function itself and its derivatives — without plotting dozens of points. The goal is to reveal the function's essential shape: where it rises or falls, where it bends, and where it has peaks, valleys, or asymptotes.

The standard checklist runs in roughly this order: (1) **domain** — where is $f$ defined? (2) **intercepts** — solve $f(x) = 0$ and evaluate $f(0)$; (3) **symmetry** — odd ($f(-x) = -f(x)$) or even ($f(-x) = f(x)$)?; (4) **asymptotes** — check limits as $x \to \pm\infty$ and at any discontinuities; (5) **first derivative** — where $f'(x) > 0$ the graph rises, where $f'(x) < 0$ it falls, and sign changes in $f'$ mark local extrema; (6) **second derivative** — where $f''(x) > 0$ the graph is concave up, where $f''(x) < 0$ it is concave down, and zeros of $f''$ are candidate inflection points.

**Worked example.** Sketch $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$.

*Domain:* all reals. *Symmetry:* $f(-x) = -f(x)$, so the graph is odd — symmetric about the origin. *Intercepts:* $f(0)=0$; $x^3 - 3x = x(x^2-3) = 0$ gives $x = 0,\pm\sqrt{3}$.

*First derivative:* $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$.  
Sign chart: $f' > 0$ on $(-\infty,-1)$, $f' < 0$ on $(-1,1)$, $f' > 0$ on $(1,\infty)$.  
Local max at $x=-1$: $f(-1) = 2$. Local min at $x=1$: $f(1) = -2$.

*Second derivative:* $f''(x) = 6x$.  
Concave down for $x < 0$, concave up for $x > 0$. Inflection point at $(0,0)$ — confirmed because $f''$ changes sign there.

Putting this together: the graph rises to a peak at $(-1,2)$, falls through the origin (an inflection point), reaches a trough at $(1,-2)$, then rises again, crossing the $x$-axis at $\pm\sqrt{3}$.

**Applying the method.** A common problem asks you to find all values of $x$ where $f$ has a local minimum without a calculator. The strategy: compute $f'$, find its zeros, then use either the first-derivative sign test (does $f'$ change from negative to positive?) or the second-derivative test ($f''(x) > 0$?). For $f(x) = x^4 - 8x^2$, you get $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 16x = 4x(x-2)(x+2)$, with sign changes from $-$ to $+$ at $x = -2$ and $x = 2$, so those are the two local minima. The sketch then confirms the W-shaped profile you would expect from a quartic with a negative middle section.
