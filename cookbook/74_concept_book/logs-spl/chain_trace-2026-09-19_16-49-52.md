## Graph

**Definition.** A graph is a pair $G = (V, E)$ where $V$ is a finite (or countable) set of vertices and $E$ is a set of edges, each edge joining exactly two vertices. In the *simple, undirected* case, $E \subseteq \binom{V}{2}$, so an edge is an unordered pair $\{u, v\}$ with $u \neq v$; multigraphs permit repeated edges, and directed graphs (digraphs) replace $E \subseteq \binom{V}{2}$ with $E \subseteq V \times V$. Write $n = |V|$, $m = |E|$. The *degree* $\deg(v)$ of a vertex is the number of edges incident to it; the *degree sequence* is $(\deg(v_1), \dots, \deg(v_n))$.

**Theorem (Handshaking Lemma).** For any graph $G = (V, E)$,
$$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2m.$$
*Proof.* Each edge $\{u, v\}$ contributes exactly $1$ to $\deg(u)$ and $1$ to $\deg(v)$, hence contributes exactly $2$ to the sum $\sum_v \deg(v)$, regardless of which vertices it joins. Summing over all $m$ edges gives the result. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary.** The number of vertices of odd degree is even — an immediate parity consequence, foundational to the classical solution of the Königsberg bridge problem (Euler, 1736), often cited as the founding act of graph theory.

**Literature context.** The abstraction $G = (V,E)$ underlies an immense apparatus: adjacency and incidence matrix representations (linking graph theory to spectral and linear-algebraic methods), the theory of graph isomorphism (whose exact complexity remains open — known to be in $\mathsf{NP}$, quasi-polynomial-time solvable by Babai's 2015 algorithm, but not known to be in $\mathsf{P}$ or $\mathsf{NP}$-complete), and structural questions such as planarity (Kuratowski's theorem), colorability (the Four Color Theorem, Appel–Haken 1976), and connectivity (Menger's theorem). Random graph models, notably the Erdős–Rényi $G(n,p)$ model, connect graphs to probability and threshold phenomena, while extremal graph theory (Turán-type problems) studies how large $m$ can be under structural constraints on $G$. Representation choice — adjacency matrix ($O(n^2)$ space, $O(1)$ edge queries) versus adjacency list ($O(n+m)$ space, efficient for sparse graphs) — is itself an active engineering concern in large-scale graph processing systems.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement both adjacency-matrix and adjacency-list representations of a graph, and empirically measure the crossover density $p^* = m^*/\binom{n}{2}$ at which the matrix representation becomes more time-efficient than the list representation for breadth-first traversal, as a function of $n$. Then generate Erdős–Rényi random graphs $G(n,p)$ for a range of $p$ near the connectivity threshold $p = \ln n / n$ and empirically verify the sharp threshold phenomenon for connectivity predicted by Erdős–Rényi (1960).

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the graph model and representation precisely (including whether self-loops or multi-edges are permitted), report asymptotic complexity claims alongside measured wall-clock data, present the empirical threshold curve with confidence bands over repeated trials, and discuss any deviation from the theoretical $\ln n/n$ threshold with a hypothesis (finite-size effects, generator bias, etc.).
