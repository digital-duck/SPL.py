### cell_membrane (0.0s)

## Cell Membrane

Every cell needs a boundary that separates its internal chemistry from the outside world while still allowing controlled exchange with it. The cell membrane performs this job using a **phospholipid bilayer**: two stacked layers of phospholipid molecules, each about 2.5 nm thick, for a total membrane thickness of roughly 5 nm. Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic (water-attracting) phosphate head and two hydrophobic (water-repelling) fatty acid tails. In water, the molecules spontaneously arrange into two sheets with tails pointing inward, away from water, and heads pointing outward, toward the watery cytoplasm on one side and the extracellular fluid on the other. This self-assembly happens without any energy input from the cell — it is driven by the same principle that keeps oil droplets clustered in water.

The bilayer is **selectively permeable**: small nonpolar molecules like O₂ and CO₂ slip through the hydrophobic core easily, but ions and large polar molecules like glucose cannot cross unassisted. This is where membrane proteins come in. Rather than sitting in fixed positions, proteins are embedded throughout the bilayer and can drift laterally — a behavior captured by the **fluid mosaic model**, which pictures proteins floating in the lipid layer like icebergs in the sea. Some proteins form channels or pumps that move specific substances across the membrane; others act as receptors that bind signaling molecules from outside the cell.

**Worked example**: Suppose a drug must reach a target inside a cell. If the drug is polar and charged, it cannot diffuse through the hydrophobic tail region of the bilayer — the membrane blocks it, just as it blocks free ions. Pharmaceutical chemists exploit this by designing drugs with a balance of polar and nonpolar character, or by relying on transport proteins that already recognize a similar natural molecule, to shuttle the drug across.

**Problem-solving application**: If you observe a substance passively crossing a membrane without any protein channel, you can predict it is small and nonpolar (like O₂, CO₂, or ethanol). If crossing requires a specific membrane protein and stops when that protein is genetically knocked out, you can conclude the substance is polar or charged (like glucose or Na⁺), and its transport depends on the protein embedded in the bilayer — a direct application of structure predicting function.

### diffusion (0.0s)

## Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of dissolved solute particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, driven by random molecular motion rather than any directed force. Because individual molecules move randomly, they are simply more likely to wander from a crowded region into an empty one than the reverse — so at the population level, this randomness produces the predictable, directional flow we call diffusion. The process continues until concentration is uniform throughout the system, a state called dynamic equilibrium: molecules still move, but with no *net* directional flow.

**Worked example.** Picture a sealed tank of water in which a spoonful of dye is dropped in one corner. Initially, the dye concentration is high near the drop point and zero elsewhere. Over time, dye molecules spread outward, and the color gradually becomes uniform across the tank. If you tracked concentration $C$ at a fixed point 10 cm from the drop site over several hours, it would rise steadily, then level off once equilibrium is reached — it never reverses and re-concentrates, because there are vastly more ways for molecules to be spread out than clustered together.

**Quantitative treatment.** Because diffusion is a physical transport process governed by a genuine mathematical law, it merits formal treatment: Fick's First Law states that the diffusive flux $J$ (amount of solute crossing a unit area per unit time) is proportional to the concentration gradient:

$$J = -D\frac{dC}{dx}$$

Here $C$ is concentration, $x$ is position, and $D$ is the diffusion coefficient (units of $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$), which depends on the solute, solvent, and temperature. The negative sign captures the essential physics: flux flows *down* the gradient, from high to low concentration.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose glucose diffuses across a thin membrane with $D = 6.7 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}$, membrane thickness $\Delta x = 0.01\ \text{cm}$, and concentrations of $0.5\ \text{mol/L}$ and $0.1\ \text{mol/L}$ on either side. Approximating the gradient as $\Delta C/\Delta x$:

$$J = -D\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = -(6.7\times10^{-6})\left(\frac{0.1-0.5}{0.01}\right) = 2.68\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}\,\text{cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$$

This flux tells a physiologist how quickly glucose crosses a cell membrane passively — a calculation underlying models of nutrient absorption, drug delivery, and cellular respiration.

### electrolyte (0.0s)

## Electrolyte

An electrolyte is a mineral that dissolves in water and separates into charged particles called ions. In the human body, the four electrolytes most responsible for nervous-system function are sodium ($\text{Na}^+$), potassium ($\text{K}^+$), chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$), and calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$). Dissolved in the fluid inside and outside a neuron, these ions are free to move, and because each carries an electric charge, their movement constitutes an electric current — the physical basis of every signal the nervous system produces.

The one new idea to add here is the concentration gradient: a neuron's cell membrane keeps the concentration of each ion different inside versus outside the cell. Sodium and calcium are far more concentrated outside; potassium is far more concentrated inside. This imbalance is maintained by the cell and represents stored potential energy, much like water held behind a dam — energy that is released once a pathway opens for the ions to flow back down their gradient, from where they are abundant to where they are scarce.

That pathway is the membrane's ion channel, a protein that opens or closes in response to a trigger. Consider a concrete case: a neuron at rest has an internal voltage of about $-70\ \text{mV}$ relative to the outside. When a stimulus opens sodium channels, $\text{Na}^+$ ions flood inward down their gradient, and the interior voltage rises rapidly toward $+30\ \text{mV}$ — the depolarization phase of the action potential. Almost immediately after, potassium channels open, $\text{K}^+$ flows outward down its own gradient, and the voltage falls back toward its resting level. This is not a chemical reaction consuming the ions; it's ion traffic redistributing charge across the membrane.

Problem-solving applications of this concept extend well beyond neuroscience: interpreting an ECG or EEG requires recognizing that the recorded voltages are the aggregate electrical signature of ion movement across many cell membranes at once. Clinically, an electrolyte imbalance — too little potassium, for example — directly impairs the ability of neurons and cardiac cells to generate normal electrical signals, which is why blood electrolyte panels are a standard diagnostic tool.

### electrostatic_force (0.0s)

## Electrostatic Force

Electrostatic force is the interaction between charged particles: opposite charges attract, like charges repel. This force is what pulls ions across cell membranes, drives current through wires, and holds atoms together in ionic bonds. Because it is a fundamental force with a precise mathematical description, understanding it requires the actual law that governs it — not just the qualitative rule of "opposites attract."

Coulomb's law quantifies the force between two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by a distance $r$:

$$
F = k_e \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}
$$

where $k_e \approx 8.99 \times 10^9 \ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$ is Coulomb's constant. The force acts along the line connecting the two charges. If $q_1$ and $q_2$ have the same sign, $F$ is positive and the force is repulsive; if they have opposite signs, $F$ is negative and the force is attractive. Notice the inverse-square dependence on $r$: doubling the distance between two charges reduces the force to one-quarter of its original strength.

**Worked example.** Suppose a sodium ion ($\text{Na}^+$, charge $q_1 = +1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) sits $2.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$ from a chloride ion ($\text{Cl}^-$, charge $q_2 = -1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) inside a cell membrane channel. The magnitude of the force between them is:

$$
F = (8.99 \times 10^9) \cdot \frac{(1.6 \times 10^{-19})(1.6 \times 10^{-19})}{(2.0 \times 10^{-9})^2} \approx 5.75 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}
$$

Since the charges are opposite, this force is attractive, pulling the ions toward each other.

**Problem-solving application.** In biology and chemistry, electrostatic force explains why ions move down electrochemical gradients: a positively charged ion is pushed away from regions of net positive charge and pulled toward regions of net negative charge, contributing (alongside diffusion) to the net flow of ions across a membrane. When a problem involves several surrounding charges rather than just one pair, apply Coulomb's law to each pair separately, then combine those individual pulls and pushes into a single overall force — accounting for both their strength and the direction each one acts in — to find the net effect on the ion in question.

### electrical_potential (0.0s)

## Electrical Potential

Every membrane in a living cell is a tiny battery. On one side sits a solution rich in some ion — say, potassium — and on the other, a solution poor in it. Left alone, those ions would diffuse down their concentration gradient, spreading out until evenly mixed. But ions carry charge, and a membrane restricts their movement to specific channels. As positive potassium ions leak out through open channels, they leave behind unbalanced negative charge inside the cell, and the resulting electrostatic pull drags the escaping ions back. The system settles into a standoff: chemical diffusion pushing ions one way, electrostatic attraction pulling them the other. The "pressure" stored in that standoff — the work required to move a unit of charge from one side of the membrane to the other — is the electrical potential, measured in volts (joules per coulomb).

This potential is not abstract; it is directly measurable and directly consequential. A resting neuron holds its interior at about $-70\ \text{mV}$ relative to the outside, meaning roughly 70 millivolts of pent-up driving force sit ready to move charge the instant a channel opens. That voltage is set by the balance of ion gradients, described quantitatively by the Nernst equation:

$$E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{outside}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{inside}}}\right)$$

Here $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is temperature in kelvin, $z$ is the ion's charge, $F$ is Faraday's constant, and the bracketed terms are concentrations on each side. This equation is unavoidable, not decorative: it is the only way to predict the equilibrium voltage for a given ion gradient, since it captures the exact point where the electrical force balances the diffusional one.

Problem-solving application: suppose extracellular potassium concentration is 5 mM and intracellular is 140 mM. At body temperature ($T \approx 310\ \text{K}$), with $z = +1$, plugging into the Nernst equation yields $E_K \approx -91\ \text{mV}$ — close to a real neuron's resting potential, confirming potassium is the dominant driver at rest. The same equation, applied to any other permeant ion, gives that ion's own equilibrium voltage from its own inside/outside concentration ratio — a single calculation you repeat, plugging in different concentrations and charges, to characterize any ion gradient across any membrane. Computing $E_{\text{ion}}$ this way is the standard first step in modeling any excitable-cell circuit.

### ion_channel (31.4s)

## Ion Channel

An ion channel is a transmembrane protein embedded in the [[cell_membrane]] that forms a narrow, water-filled pore connecting the intracellular and extracellular spaces. Unlike active transporters that consume ATP, ion channels move [[electrolyte]] species passively — no energy input is required. The channel's selectivity filter, a ring of precisely spaced amino acid residues, admits ions based on size and charge, so a given channel typically conducts one ion species (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, or Cl⁻) far more readily than others.

The new primitive this section introduces is the **electrochemical driving force**: the net push on an ion through an open channel, produced by two effects acting together — the ion's tendency to move down its concentration gradient, and the pull or push it feels from the membrane's electrical field. These combine into a single balance point, the Nernst equation, which gives the membrane voltage at which the two effects exactly cancel for one ion species:

$$
E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}}}\right)
$$

Here $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is absolute temperature, $z$ is the ion's charge, $F$ is Faraday's constant, and the bracketed terms are extracellular and intracellular concentrations. $E_{\text{ion}}$ is the membrane potential at which net flow through the channel stops.

**Worked example.** For potassium in a typical mammalian neuron, $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{out}} \approx 5\,\text{mM}$ and $[\text{K}^+]_{\text{in}} \approx 140\,\text{mM}$, with $z=+1$. At body temperature ($T = 310\,\text{K}$), $E_K \approx -89\,\text{mV}$. Since the resting membrane potential (around $-70\,\text{mV}$) is less negative than $E_K$, the electrochemical driving force still pushes K⁺ out of the cell through open potassium channels — the concentration gradient "wins" slightly over the inward electrical pull.

**Problem-solving application.** Given any two ion concentrations and knowledge of which channels are open, you can predict flow direction without measuring current directly: compare the actual membrane potential $V_m$ to $E_{\text{ion}}$. If $V_m > E_{\text{ion}}$, the ion flows outward; if $V_m < E_{\text{ion}}$, it flows inward. This single comparison — reused throughout neurophysiology — lets you reason quantitatively about depolarization, hyperpolarization, and how selectively opening or blocking a channel type will shift $V_m$ toward that ion's own equilibrium potential.

### neuron (0.0s)

## Neuron

A neuron is a eukaryotic cell specialized for rapid electrical and chemical signaling, forming the fundamental unit of the nervous system. Its architecture divides labor across three regions: dendrites, branching extensions that receive incoming signals from other neurons; the soma (cell body), which houses the nucleus and organelles and integrates those incoming signals; and the axon, a long projection that carries an outgoing signal away from the soma toward target cells. Like other eukaryotic cells, the neuron relies on standard organelles — mitochondria for ATP production, ribosomes and endoplasmic reticulum for protein synthesis — but it deploys them toward a distinctive purpose: sustaining a cell whose membrane can rapidly change its electrical state and whose shape can span, in some human motor neurons, over a meter.

Consider a sensory neuron in your fingertip that detects a hot stove. Heat-sensitive receptors on the dendrites convert (transduce) thermal energy into small electrical changes. If enough dendrites are stimulated at once, the soma integrates these inputs and triggers an action potential — a brief, self-propagating reversal of the membrane's electrical charge — which races down the axon. At the axon's end, this electrical signal triggers the release of chemical messengers (neurotransmitters) into a synapse, the junction with the next neuron, propagating the signal onward until it reaches the spinal cord and brain, producing the reflex to pull your hand back.

This structure directly solves a problem that plain diffusion cannot: getting a signal across long distances quickly and reliably. A molecule diffusing passively through cytoplasm would take minutes to travel even a few millimeters — far too slow for reflexes or thought. By concentrating charged-ion channels along the axon membrane, the neuron converts distance into an actively regenerated electrical pulse that travels at up to 120 meters per second in myelinated axons, rather than relying on molecules physically drifting from one end of the cell to the other. When diagnosing nervous-system dysfunction — for instance, why a reflex arc is delayed — the practical strategy is to localize which region is compromised: impaired dendritic reception, disrupted soma integration (as in some neurodegenerative diseases), or slowed axonal conduction (as in demyelinating conditions like multiple sclerosis), since each maps to a distinct structural stage of the signal's path.

### neurotransmitter (0.0s)

## Neurotransmitter

A neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger released by a presynaptic neuron that diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on a postsynaptic neuron, muscle cell, or gland, converting an electrical signal into a chemical one and back again. When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open, calcium influx triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane, and neurotransmitter molecules are released by exocytosis. Once bound to receptors on the far side of the cleft, the neurotransmitter either excites the postsynaptic cell (making it more likely to fire, as with glutamate) or inhibits it (making it less likely to fire, as with GABA). The signal is then terminated by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion away from the synapse.

**Worked example.** Consider a motor neuron communicating with a muscle fiber via acetylcholine (ACh) at the neuromuscular junction. An action potential arrives, calcium enters the terminal, and vesicles release ACh into the cleft. ACh binds receptors on the muscle membrane, opening ion channels that depolarize the muscle cell and trigger contraction. The enzyme acetylcholinesterase then rapidly breaks down ACh, clearing the synapse so the muscle can either reset or receive the next signal distinctly. This clearance step is what allows rapid, repeated firing — without it, ACh would linger and the receptors would stay activated, producing sustained rather than controlled contraction.

**Problem-solving application.** Now suppose a substance blocks acetylcholinesterase itself, rather than acting anywhere else in the cycle — this is exactly how nerve agents and some pesticides work. Reasoning through the mechanism: if the enzyme that normally breaks down ACh is disabled, ACh released into the cleft is no longer cleared. It keeps binding and rebinding receptors on the muscle membrane, so the postsynaptic cell stays depolarized instead of resetting between signals. The result is sustained, uncontrolled muscle activation rather than the brief, distinct contractions a normal cycle produces — which is why acetylcholinesterase inhibition is so dangerous at the neuromuscular junction. Notice that the amount of ACh released per action potential and the receptors it binds haven't changed at all; only the clearance step has been disrupted, and that alone is enough to change the outcome. This is the general strategy for predicting the effect of any drug or toxin acting on a chemical synapse: identify which single step of the release–binding–clearance cycle is being targeted, then infer whether disrupting that one step increases or decreases net signal transmission, holding everything else in the cycle fixed.

### chemical_synapse (0.0s)

## Chemical Synapse

A chemical synapse is the junction where a presynaptic neuron communicates with a postsynaptic neuron by releasing a chemical messenger—a neurotransmitter—into the narrow gap between them, the synaptic cleft (roughly 20 nanometers wide). Unlike an electrical synapse, where current flows directly through gap junctions, a chemical synapse converts an electrical signal into a chemical one and back again, introducing a brief delay but allowing far greater flexibility: signals can be amplified, filtered, or reversed in sign (excitatory or inhibitory) depending on which neurotransmitter and receptor are involved.

**How transmission works.** When an action potential arrives at the presynaptic terminal, it opens voltage-gated calcium channels. The resulting influx of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ triggers synaptic vesicles—membrane-bound sacs filled with neurotransmitter—to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release their contents into the cleft via exocytosis. The neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the cleft and bind to receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, opening ion channels there. The result is a postsynaptic potential: a small depolarization (excitatory) or hyperpolarization (inhibitory) in the receiving neuron. (Some receptors act more slowly through internal signaling cascades rather than opening a channel directly, but the outcome is the same kind of postsynaptic potential.)

**Worked example.** Consider glutamate, the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the vertebrate brain. At an excitatory synapse, an action potential triggers glutamate release; it binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane that open channels permeable to $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$. Sodium influx depolarizes the postsynaptic cell, producing an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP). If enough EPSPs arrive close together, their combined effect can push the postsynaptic neuron's membrane potential past threshold, triggering its own action potential and propagating the signal onward.

**Problem-solving application.** Chemical synapses are also the primary target of most psychoactive drugs and neurological therapies, because each step in the process—release, diffusion, binding, or clearance—is a distinct point of intervention. For example, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) block the transporter that normally clears serotonin from the cleft, prolonging its action on postsynaptic receptors and intensifying signaling at that synapse. Understanding synaptic transmission as a multistep pathway, rather than a single event, lets you predict a drug's effect from which step it targets: interfering with clearance prolongs a signal, while interfering with an earlier step (release or binding) would instead weaken or block it.

### equilibrium_potential (13.0s)

## Equilibrium Potential

Every ion channel is a doorway, and every open doorway lets ions respond to two forces at once: the concentration gradient that pushes them from high to low concentration, and the electric field that pushes charged particles toward regions of opposite charge. The equilibrium potential is the specific membrane voltage at which these two forces on a given ion species exactly cancel, so the net flow of that ion through open channels drops to zero — even though individual ions still cross in both directions.

This is a genuinely quantitative concept, not a descriptive one, because "exactly balances" has a precise numerical answer given by the Nernst equation:

$$
E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF} \ln\!\left(\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}}}\right)
$$

Here $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is absolute temperature, $F$ is Faraday's constant, $z$ is the ion's charge (e.g., $+1$ for $\text{K}^+$, $+2$ for $\text{Ca}^{2+}$), and the concentrations are measured outside versus inside the cell. At physiological temperature, this simplifies for monovalent ions to a convenient working form: $E_{\text{ion}} \approx 61.5 \log_{10}([\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}/[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}})$ millivolts.

**Worked example.** A typical neuron has extracellular $\text{K}^+$ at 5 mM and intracellular $\text{K}^+$ at 140 mM. Applying the simplified equation:

$$
E_K = 61.5 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{5}{140}\right) \approx 61.5 \times (-1.45) \approx -89 \text{ mV}
$$

This tells us that at a membrane potential of about $-89$ mV, the outward diffusive pressure on $\text{K}^+$ is exactly canceled by the inward electrical pull generated by the negative interior. Notice that the sign and magnitude depend entirely on the concentration ratio and the ion's charge: swap in $\text{Na}^+$ concentrations (145 mM outside, 12 mM inside), and $E_{Na} \approx +61$ mV — a very different equilibrium point.

**Problem-solving application.** The Nernst equation lets you predict the *direction* of ion flow at any given membrane potential $V_m$, not just the equilibrium point: if $V_m$ is more negative than $E_{\text{ion}}$, that ion will flow inward when its channels open; if less negative, it flows outward. This single calculation underlies predicting resting potential, action potential thresholds, and the effect of any channel-blocking drug or channelopathy on cell excitability.

### epsp (30.0s)

## Epsp

An excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) is a temporary, localized depolarization of the postsynaptic neuron's membrane, produced when a neurotransmitter released across a [[chemical_synapse]] opens ion channels that let positive charge flow into the cell. This shift moves the membrane potential closer to the threshold needed to fire an action potential — hence "excitatory." An EPSP is not itself an action potential: it is graded, decays over a few milliseconds, and spreads passively (decrementally) as it travels from the synapse toward the axon hillock, the site where the neuron actually decides whether to fire.

**Worked example.** At a typical excitatory synapse, the postsynaptic membrane sits at a resting potential of about $-70\text{ mV}$. When the released neurotransmitter binds its receptor, channels open and allow positively charged ions to rush into the cell, driven by both the electrical and concentration gradients. This local influx briefly raises the membrane potential — say, to $-65\text{ mV}$ at the synapse. That 5 mV shift is the EPSP. If it reaches the axon hillock still large enough to cross the firing threshold (typically around $-55\text{ mV}$), it triggers an all-or-none action potential; if not, it simply fades before getting there.

**Problem-solving application: summation.** A single EPSP is rarely enough to reach threshold, since most decay before traveling far. Neurons solve this through summation: multiple EPSPs add together before they decay, whether they arrive close together in time from the same synapse or arrive at roughly the same moment from different synapses across the neuron. Either way, the depolarizations combine as they converge on the axon hillock. Because EPSPs are graded and additive (within limits), a neuron effectively performs analog integration of its inputs, tracking the net local depolarization produced by all its active excitatory synapses. Only when this combined effect at the axon hillock exceeds threshold does the neuron fire; opposing signals that push the membrane away from threshold subtract from the total rather than add to it. This summation mechanism is why understanding EPSPs matters for problem-solving in neuroscience: it explains how a neuron converts many weak, individually subthreshold signals into a reliable, threshold-based decision — a computational principle that recurs from simple reflex circuits to models of learning and memory.

### ipsp (27.7s)

## Ipsp

An **inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)** is a transient hyperpolarization (or, in some cases, a stabilizing effect that resists change) of the postsynaptic membrane produced when a chemical synapse releases neurotransmitter that opens ion channels moving the membrane potential away from threshold. Where an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) nudges a neuron toward firing, an IPSP does the opposite: it makes the neuron *less* likely to generate an action potential during that window.

The mechanism follows directly from the chemical synapse machinery. Neurotransmitters such as GABA or glycine bind postsynaptic receptors that open chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$) or potassium ($\text{K}^+$) channels. Because the equilibrium potential for these ions ($E_{Cl^-} \approx -70\ \text{mV}$, $E_{K^+} \approx -90\ \text{mV}$) sits at or below the neuron's resting potential ($\approx -65\ \text{mV}$), letting these ions flow drives the membrane potential further from the $\approx -55\ \text{mV}$ threshold needed to fire — or holds it near rest even if other inputs push toward threshold. This second effect happens because opening $\text{Cl}^-$ channels also increases the membrane's overall conductance, which dampens the effect of simultaneous excitatory currents even when the potential itself barely moves.

**Worked example.** Suppose a neuron at resting potential $-65\ \text{mV}$ receives an EPSP that alone would depolarize it to $-50\ \text{mV}$ (above the $-55\ \text{mV}$ threshold, triggering a spike). If a GABA-releasing interneuron fires simultaneously and produces an IPSP of $-8\ \text{mV}$, the summed potential is roughly $-50 + (-8) = -58\ \text{mV}$ — below threshold, so no action potential occurs. This shows that postsynaptic potentials are not evaluated in isolation: they add together, across the dendrites where they arrive and across the timing of when they arrive, before the neuron "decides" whether to fire.

**Problem-solving application.** IPSPs are the basis of neural circuit computation: they sharpen the timing of a neuron's response, keep excitatory activity from spiraling out of control, and help sensory circuits (such as those in the retina) sharpen contrast by suppressing neighboring, weakly activated cells. When modeling a neuron's firing decision — whether in a physiology problem set or a computational neuroscience simulation — the practical task is to algebraically sum concurrent EPSPs and IPSPs (accounting for their decay over time and distance from the soma) and compare the net potential to threshold, exactly as in the worked example above.

### resting_potential (13.6s)

## Resting Potential

A neuron at rest is not electrically idle — it actively maintains a voltage difference across its membrane, typically about $-65\ \text{mV}$ (inside relative to outside). This resting potential arises from two components working together: ion channels that let specific ions cross the membrane, and the equilibrium potential each ion would settle at if it alone controlled the voltage.

**Why it isn't just one ion's equilibrium potential.** If the membrane were permeable only to $\text{K}^+$, the resting potential would equal the potassium equilibrium potential, $E_K \approx -90\ \text{mV}$, calculated from the Nernst equation using the steep concentration gradient (high $\text{K}^+$ inside, low outside) maintained by the sodium-potassium pump. But real membranes leak a small amount of $\text{Na}^+$ as well, and $\text{Na}^+$'s equilibrium potential is strongly positive ($E_{Na} \approx +60\ \text{mV}$). The resting potential is a weighted compromise between these two pulls, set by how many channels of each type are open — the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation formalizes this as a permeability-weighted average of the ion equilibrium potentials. Because far more potassium channels are open at rest than sodium channels, the resting potential sits close to $E_K$ but is pulled slightly toward $E_{Na}$, landing near $-65\ \text{mV}$.

**Worked example.** Suppose a membrane's resting permeability ratio is $P_K : P_{Na} = 25:1$, with $E_K = -90\ \text{mV}$ and $E_{Na} = +60\ \text{mV}$. A permeability-weighted estimate gives

$$
V_m \approx \frac{P_K E_K + P_{Na} E_{Na}}{P_K + P_{Na}} = \frac{25(-90) + 1(60)}{26} \approx -84.6\ \text{mV}
$$

Increasing $P_{Na}$ (more open sodium channels) pulls $V_m$ upward, toward zero — which is exactly the mechanism that later triggers an action potential.

**Problem-solving application.** This model lets you predict how channel changes shift resting potential without memorizing outcomes: blocking $\text{K}^+$ channels (raising $P_{Na}$ relative to $P_K$) depolarizes the cell, while a drug that opens more $\text{K}^+$ channels hyperpolarizes it, moving $V_m$ closer to $E_K$. Because the sodium-potassium pump continuously restores the ion gradients that the equilibrium potentials depend on, resting potential is a steady-state balance, not a passive default — disrupting the pump (e.g., with metabolic poisons) causes the gradients, and thus the resting potential itself, to collapse toward zero over time.

### threshold (10.8s)

## Threshold

Threshold is the critical level of membrane depolarization a neuron must reach for an action potential to fire. It is not a fixed property of the membrane alone but the outcome of a running tally: excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) push the membrane potential up from rest, inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (IPSPs) pull it back down, and if the net effect at the axon hillock crosses the threshold value — typically around $-55\text{ mV}$, starting from a resting potential near $-70\text{ mV}$ — voltage-gated sodium channels open and the neuron fires. Below threshold, no matter how close, nothing happens; the neuron either fires a full action potential or it doesn't. This all-or-none behavior is what makes threshold a genuine decision boundary rather than a graded response.

**Worked example.** Suppose a neuron rests at $-70\text{ mV}$ and its threshold is $-55\text{ mV}$, so it needs $15\text{ mV}$ of net depolarization to fire. Three synapses activate together: two excitatory inputs each contribute $+6\text{ mV}$, and one inhibitory input contributes $-4\text{ mV}$. If these summate linearly at the axon hillock, the net change is

$$
\Delta V = (+6) + (+6) + (-4) = +8\text{ mV}
$$

bringing the membrane to $-62\text{ mV}$ — still $7\text{ mV}$ short of threshold, so no action potential fires. This illustrates spatial summation: inputs arriving from different synapses combine, and inhibition can veto excitation even when multiple excitatory signals are active.

**Problem-solving application.** Threshold problems typically ask you to determine whether a given combination of inputs is sufficient to trigger firing, or to find the minimum number/strength of inputs needed. The general approach: compute net depolarization as (sum of EPSPs) + (sum of IPSPs, as negative values), add it to resting potential, and compare to threshold. For example, if each EPSP contributes $+5\text{ mV}$ and there are no inhibitory inputs, the minimum number of simultaneous EPSPs needed to cross a $15\text{ mV}$ gap is $\lceil 15/5 \rceil = 3$. Real neurons complicate this with temporal summation (successive EPSPs arriving close in time before the first decays) and nonlinear summation near threshold, but the additive rule above is the standard first-approximation model used in coursework and captures the core logic: excitation and inhibition are added algebraically, and firing is a threshold-crossing event, not a proportional response.

### voltage_gated_channel (12.9s)

## Voltage Gated Channel

A voltage-gated channel is an ion channel whose gate is controlled not by a chemical messenger but by the neuron's own membrane potential — the electrical voltage difference across the membrane. Embedded in the channel protein is a voltage sensor, typically a set of charged amino acid residues on a transmembrane helix. As the membrane depolarizes (becomes less negative inside), the electric field across the membrane changes, exerting a force on these charges that physically pulls the sensor domain, twisting the protein and swinging the pore open. Below the channel's threshold voltage, the gate stays shut; once the membrane reaches that threshold, the channel opens, letting its specific ion (commonly $\text{Na}^+$ or $\text{K}^+$) flow down its electrochemical gradient.

This threshold behavior is what makes voltage-gated channels the engine of the action potential. Picture a neuron at rest around $-70\text{ mV}$. A stimulus depolarizes the membrane slightly. If it reaches roughly $-55\text{ mV}$, voltage-gated $\text{Na}^+$ channels snap open. Sodium rushes in, driving the membrane potential rapidly upward — which opens still more $\text{Na}^+$ channels, a positive feedback loop that produces the sharp upstroke of the spike. Milliseconds later, those same channels inactivate (a separate part of the protein blocks the pore even though the voltage sensor is still "on"), while voltage-gated $\text{K}^+$ channels — slower to respond — finally open and let potassium exit, repolarizing the cell back toward rest.

The problem-solving payoff of understanding this mechanism is being able to reason about *why* a signal is all-or-none rather than graded. Because opening depends on crossing a threshold voltage, a stimulus that depolarizes the membrane to $-60\text{ mV}$ produces no channel opening and no propagated signal, while one reaching $-54\text{ mV}$ triggers a full-blown spike identical in size to any other spike — the channel doesn't "partially open" in proportion to the stimulus. This threshold logic also explains refractory periods: while $\text{Na}^+$ channels sit in their inactivated state, no additional stimulus, however strong, can trigger a second spike, which caps the maximum firing rate of a neuron and is the reason action potentials travel as discrete, non-decaying pulses rather than fading analog signals along the axon.

### action_potential (13.3s)

## Action Potential

An action potential is an all-or-none electrical signal that propagates along a neuron's axon once the membrane potential crosses a **threshold** value, typically around $-55\text{ mV}$. Unlike a graded signal that shrinks as it travels, the action potential regenerates itself at full amplitude along the entire length of the axon, allowing it to carry information reliably over distances that can exceed a meter in the human body. The event depends entirely on **voltage-gated channels**, membrane proteins that open or close in response to the membrane's own voltage rather than to a chemical messenger.

The sequence unfolds in four phases. First, some depolarizing input pushes the membrane potential toward threshold. If threshold is reached, voltage-gated sodium channels snap open, and $\text{Na}^+$ rushes in down its electrochemical gradient, driving the membrane rapidly toward positive values (depolarization, the rising phase, peaking near $+30\text{ mV}$). Second, the sodium channels inactivate within about a millisecond while slower voltage-gated potassium channels open, letting $\text{K}^+$ flow out and repolarizing the membrane (the falling phase). Third, potassium channels close more slowly than they opened, producing a brief afterhyperpolarization below resting potential. Finally, the sodium-potassium pump and leak channels restore resting conditions, and the sodium channels reset to their closed-but-available state.

The regenerative property comes from a simple feedback loop: depolarization opens more sodium channels, which causes more depolarization. Because this cycle is self-reinforcing once threshold is crossed, the signal cannot be partial — it fires at a fixed magnitude or not at all, and it renews itself in each adjacent patch of membrane as it moves down the axon, which is why the signal does not decay with distance the way a passive electrical current would.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose a neuron receives two simultaneous inputs that individually depolarize the membrane to $-62\text{ mV}$ and $-58\text{ mV}$, with resting potential at $-70\text{ mV}$ and threshold at $-55\text{ mV}$. Neither input alone reaches threshold, but if their effects sum spatially at the axon hillock (spatial summation), the combined depolarization can exceed $-55\text{ mV}$ and trigger a full action potential. This illustrates why threshold acts as a decision boundary: a neuron functions as an integrator of many small inputs, firing only when their combined effect on voltage-gated channels is sufficient to launch the regenerative sodium influx — a computational principle that underlies how networks of neurons filter and combine information.

### leak_k_channel (32.2s)

## Leak K Channel

While voltage-gated channels dominate the drama of an action potential, the resting neuron depends on a quieter, always-open channel: the leak K$^+$ channel. Unlike gated channels, whose conductance switches on or off in response to voltage or ligand binding, the leak channel has no gate at all — it is permeable to K$^+$ ions at every moment, regardless of membrane voltage. This constant, low-level conductance is what stabilizes the resting membrane potential and continuously pulls it back toward the K$^+$ equilibrium potential, $E_K$ (established by the Nernst equation, roughly $-90\,\text{mV}$ in a typical neuron, where intracellular K$^+$ is much higher than extracellular), whenever a disturbance pushes it away.

The one new idea here is that the leak channel's current obeys a simple linear (Ohmic) relationship: $I_K = g_{leak}(V_m - E_K)$, where $g_{leak}$ is a small but nonzero conductance present at all times, and $V_m$ is the current membrane potential. This equation says the leak current is proportional to how far the membrane has strayed from $E_K$ — no threshold, no switch, just a steady pull.

Consider what this current does functionally: if some perturbation depolarizes the membrane above $-90\,\text{mV}$, the term $(V_m - E_K)$ becomes positive, driving an outward K$^+$ current that removes positive charge from the cell and pushes $V_m$ back down. If the membrane is hyperpolarized below $E_K$, the term becomes negative, the current reverses direction, and $V_m$ is pushed back up. This is negative feedback in its simplest electrical form.

Problem-solving application: suppose $g_{leak} = 0.05\,\text{mS/cm}^2$ and $E_K = -90\,\text{mV}$. If a synaptic input transiently depolarizes the membrane to $-70\,\text{mV}$, the leak current is $I_K = 0.05(-70-(-90)) = 1.0\,\mu\text{A/cm}^2$ outward — enough to begin restoring the resting potential even before any gated channel activates. Practicing this calculation across different displacement voltages builds intuition for how conductance magnitude sets the speed of the membrane's return to rest, a relationship you will later combine with other simultaneously active conductances to compute the true resting potential.

### myelin (0.0s)

## Myelin

Myelin is a fatty, insulating substance that wraps around the axons of neurons, dramatically increasing the speed and efficiency of electrical signal transmission. In the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord), myelin is produced by oligodendrocytes; in the peripheral nervous system, Schwann cells perform this role. Rather than coating the axon in one continuous sheath, myelin forms segments separated by small, exposed gaps called nodes of Ranvier.

This gapped structure is the key to myelin's function. In an unmyelinated axon, an electrical signal (action potential) must regenerate continuously along every point of the membrane, a slow process. In a myelinated axon, the insulated segments prevent current from leaking out, so the electrical signal can effectively "jump" from one node of Ranvier to the next — a phenomenon called saltatory conduction (from the Latin *saltare*, "to leap"). Because the signal only needs to regenerate at the nodes rather than continuously, conduction velocity increases severalfold, and the axon uses far less metabolic energy to do so, since ion pumps only need to restore ion balance at the sparse nodes rather than along the entire membrane.

Consider a concrete comparison: an unmyelinated axon in the human PNS conducts signals at roughly 0.5 to 2 meters per second. A myelinated axon of similar diameter can conduct at 100 meters per second or more — a difference that matters enormously in practice. The reflex arc that pulls your hand away from a hot stove depends on myelinated sensory and motor neurons transmitting signals fast enough to prevent injury; without myelin, the delay could mean a serious burn before the reflex engages.

Myelin's importance is also visible when it fails. In multiple sclerosis, the immune system attacks and degrades myelin in the CNS, disrupting saltatory conduction and producing symptoms like muscle weakness, numbness, and impaired coordination, depending on which pathways are affected. This clinical example reinforces the applied lesson: myelin is not just structural padding but a functional component of the nervous system's signaling architecture, and its integrity directly determines how quickly and reliably an organism can sense and respond to its environment.

### saltatory_conduction (13.2s)

## Saltatory Conduction

In an unmyelinated axon, an action potential propagates as a continuous wave: every patch of membrane must depolarize in sequence, and conduction velocity scales only with the square root of axon diameter. Vertebrates solve this scaling problem with myelin — a fatty insulating sheath produced by Schwann cells (peripheral nervous system) or oligodendrocytes (central nervous system) that wraps segments of axon, interrupted at regular intervals by short bare stretches called the **nodes of Ranvier**. Saltatory conduction is the resulting mode of propagation, in which the action potential appears to "jump" (Latin *saltare*, to leap) from node to node rather than traveling continuously.

The mechanism combines two distinct processes. Along the myelinated internode, the sheath increases membrane resistance and decreases membrane capacitance, so depolarizing current entering at one node spreads passively — and rapidly — along the axon core to the next node, much like current flowing through an insulated wire. This passive spread is fast but decremental: the signal weakens with distance. Voltage-gated $\text{Na}^+$ channels are absent under the myelin but are densely clustered at each node, so when the spreading current arrives, it is strong enough to trigger a fresh, full-amplitude action potential there. The signal is regenerated node by node instead of continuously, which is both faster and more metabolically efficient, since active $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ pumping is required only at the nodes rather than across the entire membrane.

Consider the clinical application: in multiple sclerosis and Guillain–Barré syndrome, the immune system attacks myelin, exposing larger stretches of bare axon between functional nodes. Passive current decays before reaching the next sodium channel cluster, so the action potential fails to regenerate — conduction slows or blocks entirely, producing numbness, weakness, or vision loss depending on which axons are affected. This directly illustrates the length constant $\lambda$, the distance over which a passive signal decays to $1/e$ of its original amplitude, given by

$$\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{r_m}{r_i}}$$

where $r_m$ is membrane resistance and $r_i$ is internal (axial) resistance. Myelin increases $r_m$, increasing $\lambda$ and allowing wider node spacing without conduction failure. Demyelination lowers $r_m$, shrinks $\lambda$, and can push internode distance beyond the safety margin needed for regeneration — the biophysical basis for the disease's symptoms.
