### physiology (0.0s)

## Physiology

You already know that when you're healthy, your body isn't just sitting still — it's actively working to stay balanced. Your heart rate, body temperature, blood sugar, and dozens of other variables hover around specific values, not because they're locked in place, but because your body constantly adjusts them. This active balancing act is **physiology**: the study of how living systems function normally, moment to moment.

The key idea is the **set point** — a target value the body tries to maintain, like $37°C$ for core temperature or roughly $90$–$100$ mg/dL for fasting blood glucose. Around each set point is a **normal range**, since real systems fluctuate slightly rather than sitting at one exact number. What keeps a variable inside its range is a **feedback loop**: a sensor detects a change, a control center compares it to the set point, and an effector responds to push the variable back.

Most physiological feedback is *negative feedback* — the response opposes the original change, which is what makes stability possible. If you get too hot, sweat glands activate to cool you down; if blood sugar rises after eating, insulin lowers it back toward the set point. This loop structure — sense, compare, respond — repeats across nearly every organ system, from breathing rate to hydration.

Understanding this "healthy floor" matters because it's the baseline against which illness is measured: symptoms are often just a set point pushed out of range, or a feedback loop struggling to respond.

**Practice problem:** After a hard workout, your body temperature rises to $38.5°C$. Identify the sensor, control center, and effector involved in bringing it back down, and explain whether this is negative or positive feedback.

### anatomy (0.0s)

## Anatomy

You already know anatomy is "the map of the body" — where the heart sits, how bones connect, what a nerve looks like under a scope. That intuition is correct, but anatomy is more than a list of parts. It is an organized hierarchy, and understanding that hierarchy is what lets you reason about the body instead of just memorizing it.

The levels run from small to large: cells organize into tissues (groups of similar cells doing one job, like muscle tissue or epithelial tissue), tissues combine into organs (the heart, the liver), and organs work together in organ systems (the circulatory system, the digestive system). Each level depends on the one below it — a heart can only pump because its muscle tissue can contract, and muscle tissue can only contract because of proteins inside individual cells. This is why anatomy and physiology are taught together: anatomy tells you *where* a structure is and *what shape* it has, while physiology explains *why* that shape lets it do its job.

Anatomists also use standard reference terms so any two people describing the body mean the same thing: *anterior/posterior* (front/back), *superior/inferior* (above/below), *medial/lateral* (toward/away from the midline). This shared vocabulary is why a doctor's note, an X-ray, and a surgical plan can all describe the same location precisely.

### receptor (0.0s)

## Receptor

Enzymes catalyze reactions, and transporters move molecules across membranes—but receptors do something different: they *listen*. A receptor is a protein built to bind one specific signaling molecule, called a ligand, and then trigger a response inside the cell. The ligand could be a hormone like insulin, a neurotransmitter like dopamine, or a drug designed in a lab.

Structurally, a receptor has at least two working parts: a binding site shaped to fit its ligand (often with a "lock and key" or "induced fit" specificity), and a mechanism to pass the signal onward—transduction. Some receptors sit on the cell surface, with the binding site facing outward and a tail reaching into the cytoplasm; when the ligand binds, the receptor changes shape, and that shape change triggers a cascade inside the cell. Others sit inside the cell and bind ligands that can cross the membrane directly, like steroid hormones.

This is where pharmacology and physiology meet. Physiology asks what a signal does to the body; pharmacology asks how a drug can imitate, block, or amplify that signal. Both answers run through the same molecular gatekeeper.

**Simple rule:** binding affinity plus receptor shape determines selectivity—how "choosy" a receptor is. A drug that fits a receptor's binding site can act as an agonist (turns the response on) or an antagonist (blocks the site without activating it).

**Practice problem:** A new drug binds tightly to the same receptor as adrenaline but produces no downstream effect when it binds—the cell doesn't respond. Is this drug acting as an agonist or an antagonist? Explain your reasoning in one or two sentences.

### sign (0.0s)

## Sign

You already know the basic idea: a sign is something a clinician can *observe*, while a symptom is something a patient *feels*. Fever, a fast pulse, or a heart murmur heard through a stethoscope — these are signs. Now let's look at why that distinction matters and how signs actually get produced.

Every sign comes from a chain: an underlying physiological change produces a physical, measurable effect. A fever is a sign because the hypothalamus resets the body's temperature setpoint higher, and a thermometer captures the result — a number, not a feeling. An elevated heart rate is a sign because faster electrical firing in the heart's sinoatrial node speeds up contractions, and a clinician counts beats per minute. A heart murmur is a sign because turbulent blood flow through a valve creates a sound wave the ear (or a stethoscope) can pick up. In each case, structure (anatomy) is doing something (physiology), and that "something" leaves a trace an outside observer can detect and often quantify.

This gives us a simple rule: **a sign requires an anatomical structure whose physiological activity produces an externally observable, verifiable effect.** No structure, no activity, no observation — no sign. Symptoms skip the "externally observable" part entirely, since only the patient has access to them.

**Practice problem:** A doctor examines a patient and notes: (a) the patient says their stomach hurts, (b) the patient's temperature reads 101°F, (c) the doctor hears crackling sounds in the lungs through a stethoscope. Which of these are signs, and which is a symptom? For each sign, briefly identify the anatomy and physiology involved.

### symptom (0.0s)

## Symptom

You already know that pain or a cough are symptoms — signals your body sends when something's off. But look closer, and "symptom" has a precise meaning in medicine: it is what the *patient reports*. It's subjective. Only you can tell your doctor that you feel dizzy, exhausted, or short of breath. A doctor can *measure* your heart rate or take your temperature — those are called signs — but a symptom lives entirely in your own experience.

This distinction matters because of where symptoms come from structurally. Every physiological process — breathing, digestion, blood pressure — runs toward a target, a homeostatic set point. A symptom is the felt experience of departure from that set point. Fatigue might signal low oxygen delivery to tissues; dyspnea (shortness of breath) signals your respiratory system straining to meet oxygen demand; pain often signals tissue damage or inflammation disrupting normal function.

Here's a pattern worth noticing: this is exactly what an engineer means by *error* — the gap between a system's actual state and its set point. A thermostat "feels" a room is too cold the way your body "feels" it needs more oxygen. Both are raw signals, not diagnoses. A symptom tells you *that* something deviated, not *why*.

$$
\text{symptom} \approx |\text{actual state} - \text{set point}|
$$

**Practice problem:** A patient reports feeling "constantly tired" and "out of breath climbing stairs." List these as symptoms, then propose one physiological set point each might reflect a departure from (hint: think about what oxygen and energy delivery depend on).

### diagnosis (0.0s)

## Diagnosis

You already know the intuitive version: a patient has symptoms, a doctor thinks about what disease could explain them. But real diagnosis is more precise than pattern-matching — it's a step-by-step process of narrowing down possibilities using probability.

Every disease has a **prior probability**: how common it is in the population being examined. A doctor doesn't start from zero — they start from prevalence. If a disease affects 1 in 1,000 people, that's the starting belief before any test is run.

Then comes evidence: symptoms (what the patient reports), signs (what the doctor observes), and test results. Each test has two key properties. **Sensitivity** is the chance a test correctly identifies someone who *has* the disease (true positive rate). **Specificity** is the chance it correctly clears someone who *doesn't* have it (true negative rate). No test is perfect — every one carries some chance of a false positive or false negative.

This is where Bayes' theorem enters: it's the formal rule for updating a probability once new evidence arrives.

$$
P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive test}) = \frac{P(\text{positive test} \mid \text{disease}) \cdot P(\text{disease})}{P(\text{positive test})}
$$

The surprising pattern: a positive result on a rare disease, even with a very accurate test, often still means the person probably *doesn't* have it — because so many more healthy people were tested than sick ones. This is why doctors combine multiple tests and signs rather than trusting one result alone.

**Practice problem:** A disease affects 1% of a population. A test for it is 90% sensitive and 90% specific. If a random person tests positive, is it more likely they have the disease or not? (Hint: imagine testing 1,000 people and count outcomes in each group.)

### pharmacodynamics (0.0s)

## Pharmacodynamics

You already know pharmacokinetics tracks a drug's journey through the body — absorption, distribution, elimination. Pharmacodynamics asks the opposite question: once the drug is *there*, what does it do? The answer almost always runs through receptors — proteins on or in cells that a drug molecule binds to, triggering a downstream effect.

Binding follows a simple logic: drug and receptor come together and fall apart constantly, and the balance between those rates is described by the dissociation constant $K_d$ — the concentration at which half the receptors are occupied at equilibrium. A low $K_d$ means tight binding (high affinity); a high $K_d$ means weak binding. But occupying a receptor isn't the same as producing an effect — that connection is captured by the Hill equation:

$$E = \dfrac{E_{max} \cdot C^n}{EC_{50}^n + C^n}$$

Here $E$ is the observed effect, $C$ is drug concentration, $E_{max}$ is the maximum possible effect the tissue can produce, and $EC_{50}$ is the concentration giving half-maximal effect. The exponent $n$ (the Hill coefficient) controls how sharply the curve rises — near $n=1$ the response climbs gradually; larger $n$ makes it switch on more steeply, often reflecting multiple binding sites cooperating.

The pattern to remember: effect saturates. No matter how much drug you add, response can't exceed $E_{max}$, because only so many receptors exist. This is why doubling a dose rarely doubles the effect once you're near the top of the curve.

**Practice problem:** A drug has $EC_{50} = 4\ \text{mg/L}$ and $n=1$. If $E_{max} = 100$, what percent of maximum effect occurs at $C = 4\ \text{mg/L}$? At $C = 12\ \text{mg/L}$?

### pharmacokinetics (0.0s)

## Pharmacokinetics

You already know the outline: a drug enters the body, spreads through tissues, gets broken down, and eventually leaves — Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion (ADME). Pharmacokinetics turns that story into a quantitative model that predicts drug concentration in the blood over time.

The simplest version treats the body as a single "compartment" — imagine blood and tissues as one well-mixed tank. After a drug enters, its concentration $C(t)$ rises, peaks, then falls as the body clears it. Two numbers describe this curve: $C_{max}$, the peak concentration, and $T_{max}$, the time at which it occurs. Once absorption is finished, elimination usually follows first-order kinetics — a constant fraction of the drug is removed per unit time, not a constant amount. That gives exponential decay:

$$C(t) = C_0 \, e^{-kt}$$

Here $k$ is the elimination rate constant. From it we get the drug's half-life:

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k}$$

This is the time for the concentration to drop by half — a fixed number no matter how much drug you start with, which is why doctors can predict dosing schedules from a single measured half-life.

Finally, the total drug exposure over time — important for judging effectiveness and toxicity — is the area under the concentration curve, $AUC = \int_0^\infty C(t)\, dt$. For simple exponential decay, $AUC = C_0/k$.

**Practice problem:** A drug has $C_0 = 80$ mg/L and a half-life of 4 hours. What is $k$, and what will the concentration be after 12 hours?

### treatment (9.9s)

## Treatment

You already know the idea: when the body drifts from its healthy baseline, treatment nudges it back. Now let's look at how doctors actually design that nudge — because "give medicine" is really three separate decisions layered together.

First, a treatment needs a **diagnosis** — you can't correct a deviation you haven't identified. Second, once you know the target, you choose **dose, timing, and route**. Pharmacokinetics tells you what the body does to the drug: how fast it's absorbed, distributed, broken down, and excreted. Pharmacodynamics tells you what the drug does to the body — how strongly it binds its target and produces an effect at a given concentration.

This is where the **therapeutic window** becomes crucial. Every drug has a minimum effective concentration (below it, nothing happens) and a maximum safe concentration (above it, toxicity begins). The window is the range between them:

$$C_{min,effective} \le C_{drug} \le C_{max,safe}$$

A drug with a wide window (like many antibiotics) is forgiving of dosing errors. A drug with a narrow window (like warfarin or digoxin) demands careful monitoring, because a slightly high dose causes harm while a slightly low one fails to treat. Timing and route matter too: an IV drug acts fast but is harder to reverse, while an oral drug is absorbed slowly, giving the body more buffer time.

**Practice problem:** A drug is effective above 10 mg/L and toxic above 40 mg/L. A patient's blood level is measured at 8 mg/L two hours after a dose, and it's known to roughly double every two hours from absorption before declining. Is a second dose right now more likely to land inside the therapeutic window, or risk toxicity? Explain your reasoning.
