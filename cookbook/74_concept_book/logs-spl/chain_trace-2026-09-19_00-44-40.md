## Charge

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter, carried by subatomic particles, that determines how strongly and in what direction they interact electromagnetically. Charge comes in two types, arbitrarily labeled positive and negative: the proton carries a positive charge, the electron an equal but negative charge, and the neutron none at all. Particles with like charges repel one another; particles with opposite charges attract. Charge is quantized — it always appears in whole-number multiples of the elementary charge $e \approx 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$ — and it is conserved: the total charge in an isolated system never changes, even as particles are created, destroyed, or rearranged in a reaction.

The force between two charges is described quantitatively by Coulomb's Law:

$$F = k_e \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

where $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the magnitudes of the two charges (in coulombs), $r$ is the distance between them, and $k_e \approx 8.99 \times 10^9\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$ is Coulomb's constant. A positive result for $F$ (same-sign charges) indicates repulsion; a negative result (opposite-sign charges) indicates attraction. Note the structural resemblance to Newton's law of gravitation — both are inverse-square laws — but charge, unlike mass, can be negative, which is precisely why electric forces can both attract and repel while gravity only attracts.

**Worked example:** Two point charges, $q_1 = +3\ \mu\text{C}$ and $q_2 = -5\ \mu\text{C}$, sit $0.2\text{ m}$ apart. Find the force between them.

$$F = (8.99 \times 10^9) \frac{(3\times10^{-6})(5\times10^{-6})}{(0.2)^2} \approx 3.37\text{ N}$$

Since the charges have opposite signs, this $3.37\text{ N}$ force is attractive, pulling the two charges toward each other along the line connecting them.

**Problem-solving application:** When charges are conserved in a nuclear or chemical reaction, you can use charge bookkeeping to identify an unknown product — summing the charges of reactants and setting that equal to the sum of charges of products, then solving for the missing term. This same conservation logic, combined with Coulomb's Law, underlies circuit analysis, electrostatic shielding, and the design of particle detectors.
