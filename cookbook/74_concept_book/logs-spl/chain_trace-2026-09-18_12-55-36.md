### prefix (6.3s)

## Prefix

Imagine your friend is running, and you shout one small word before it: "re-run," "un-run"... okay, those sound silly, but try "return." "Turn" is the action. "Re-" jumps in front and says: do it again, back the other way. Same runner, same turning — but now the whole meaning points somewhere new.

That's what a prefix does. It's a steerer. It doesn't change what kind of word you have — "turn" was an action, "return" is still an action — it just grabs the wheel and aims the meaning: inward, backward, across, before, or together.

Think of a word as a little car. The stem is the engine. The prefix is a sign stuck on front telling the car which way to go.

$$
\underbrace{un}_{\text{opposite}} + \underbrace{happy}_{\text{stem}} = unhappy
$$

$$
\underbrace{pre}_{\text{before}} + \underbrace{view}_{\text{stem}} = preview
$$

**Now you try:** Take the word "view." Add "re-" to the front. What new direction does the word point now — and what do you think "review" means?

### stem (14.5s)

## Stem

Imagine every word is a tree. Some parts change with the seasons — a prefix here, a new ending there. But the trunk stays the same. That trunk is the stem, and it's the piece that carries the word's real meaning.

Take the word "hopeful." The ending "-ful" just means "full of." But if you take that away, you're left with "hope" — and *that's* the part telling you what the word is about. Without a stem, a word is just decoration with nothing underneath.

Every single English word has at least one stem, whether it traces back to old Latin, ancient Greek, or the Germanic languages English grew from long ago. The stem is the anchor everyone can point to and say: "this is the meaning."

$$
\underbrace{hope}_{\text{stem: meaning}} + \underbrace{ful}_{\text{ending}} = hopeful
$$

**Now you try:** Look at "careless" and "teacher." Cover up the endings "-less" and "-er." What stem is left standing, and what is it about?

### in- (8.4s)

## In-

Imagine you're standing outside a house, and someone says "come in." That little word "in-" is like a hand pulling you through the doorway — it always points *inward*, *into* something.

Here's the fun part: "in-" is a shape-shifter. It doesn't always look the same! It changes its costume depending on what letter comes right after it, almost like it's blending in with its neighbor. Before "m," "p," or "b," it becomes "im-" (think *immerse* — sliding *into* water). Before "l," it becomes "il-" (*illuminate* — light going *into* darkness). Before "r," it becomes "ir-" (*irrigate* — water flowing *into* fields).

So the meaning never changes — always "into" or "in" — only the costume does, to make the word smoother to say.

$$
\underbrace{in\text{-}}_{\text{into}} \xrightarrow{\text{before } m/p/b} im\text{-} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{before } l} il\text{-} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{before } r} ir\text{-}
$$

**Now you try:** Can you guess why we say "*impossible*" instead of "*inpossible*"? Which letter made "in-" change its costume?

### port (6.8s)

## Port

Imagine you're moving to a new house. You pack everything into boxes and carry them to a truck. That truck can carry anything — books, toys, furniture. In Latin, the word for "carry" was *portare*, and it left behind a busy little root: **port**.

Once you know "port" means "carry," a whole family of words snaps into focus. A *portable* radio is one you can carry. When you *transport* something, you carry it *across* (trans-) to a new place. An *import* is something carried *in* (im-), and an *export* is carried *out*. Even a ship's *port* is a place where goods get carried on and off boats! And today, when you *port* a file to a new computer, you're still just carrying something from one place to another — Latin just didn't know about computers yet.

$$
\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = \text{transport (carry across)}
$$

**Now you try:** Can you guess what *export* means, using "carry" and "out"?

### con- (7.0s)

## Con-

Picture two kids each holding one handle of a heavy basket. Alone, neither can lift it — but together, they carry it easily. That "togetherness" is exactly what the prefix *con-* brings to a word.

*Con-* means "together" or "with." It doesn't do a job by itself — it changes how another word's job gets done, by making sure it happens jointly, not alone.

Think of *con-* as a little steering wheel. Whatever action the root word names, *con-* points it toward "doing this with someone."

$$
\underbrace{con}_{\text{together}} + \underbrace{struct}_{\text{build}} = \text{construct (build jointly, piece by piece)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{con}_{\text{together}} + \underbrace{nect}_{\text{bind, tie}} = \text{connect (tie together)}
$$

Now you try: think of the word "concert." A single musician plays alone — but a *concert* means many musicians playing *together*. Can you spot the "con-" doing its job of gathering people into one shared action?

### dict (6.4s)

## Dict

Picture a wise old counting keeper in a village long ago. Every time someone said a word, this keeper wrote it down and explained what it meant. People started calling this book of words a "dict"-something — because in Latin, *dicere* meant "to say." Isn't it wonderful that a word for a book full of words comes from the simple act of speaking?

That's the secret hiding inside so many English words: **dict** always points back to saying, telling, or ruling with words. A *dictionary* is a book of things said and defined. A *verdict* is a truth that gets said out loud after careful thought. Even a *dictator* is someone who tells others what to do — using only their voice!

$$
\underbrace{dict}_{\text{to say}} + \underbrace{ionary}_{\text{collection}} = dictionary
$$

**Now you try:** Think of the word *predict*. It has *dict* hiding inside it, plus "pre-" meaning "before." Can you guess what "predict" means, using what you know about saying?

### ject (10.8s)

## Ject

Imagine a word as a tiny machine, and inside it lives a secret gear that always means "throw." That gear is *ject* — from an old Latin word, *iacere*, meaning to throw. Whenever you spot *ject* hiding inside a bigger word, you can be almost sure something is being thrown — maybe not with a ball, but with an idea, a person, or even light.

Think of *ject* like the stem of a plant. A stem alone doesn't do much, but attach a little piece to it, and it grows into something new: throw something *back*, and you *reject* it. Throw something *in*, and you *inject* it. Throw something *out*, and you *eject* it. Throw something *across*, and you *project* it, like a beam of light thrown across a room.

$$
\underbrace{ject}_{\text{throw}} + \underbrace{pro}_{\text{forward}} = project
$$

**Now you try:** Can you guess what "throwing back" an idea might mean in a conversation?

### pre- (7.2s)

## Pre-

Picture this: you're about to sing "happy," but someone stops you and says, "wait — sing about the time BEFORE you were happy." How do you do that with one word? English has a trick: you don't need a whole new word. You just tuck a tiny piece onto the front of the one you already have.

That tiny piece is *pre-*. It means "before." It's like a little time-machine dial you attach to a word, and it spins that word's meaning backward, into the earlier moment.

Think of "heat" and "preheat." Heating is the cooking step. Preheating is the step your oven does *before* the cooking even starts — warming up in advance, getting ready. Same root idea, "heat," but *pre-* drags it into the past, ahead of everything else.

$$
\underbrace{pre}_{before} + \underbrace{heat}_{to warm} = preheat
$$

Now you try: take the word "view" (to look at something) and add *pre-* to the front. What do you get, and when does it happen — before or after the main event?

### re- (7.0s)

## Re-

Have you ever built a tower of blocks, knocked it down, and built it again? That's what "re-" does to words — it says "let's do this one more time!"

Take the word "do." Now put "re-" in front: "re-do." That means doing it again! "Re-" is like a little helper that hops onto the front of a word and whispers, "again, again!" Sometimes it also means going back — like "re-turn," going back to where you started.

$$\underbrace{re}_{again/back} + \underbrace{turn}_{go} = return$$

Think of "re-" as a boomerang word-piece. A boomerang flies out and comes back. "Re-" does the same thing to meaning — it sends the word out to happen once more, or brings it back home.

**Now you try:** Take the word "play." Add "re-" to the front. What new word do you get, and what does it mean? Try it again with "read" and "build" too!

### spect (5.7s)

## Spect

Imagine you're a detective. What's the first thing you do? You *look*. You watch, you check, you observe every clue. That's exactly what the word root **spect** means — it comes from an old Latin word, *specere*, which simply meant "to look."

Once you know this, a whole family of English words suddenly makes sense! When you **inspect** something, you look closely *into* it. When you **expect** a friend to visit, you're looking *out for* them. A **spectator** at a game is someone who looks and watches. Even a pair of **spectacles** (old-fashioned word for glasses) helps you look better!

So "spect" is like a secret looking-glass hidden inside many words — once you spot it, you know the word has something to do with watching, checking, or seeing.

**Now you try:** Can you guess what "spectacular" might mean, using what you know about looking?

### struct (6.3s)

## Struct

Imagine you're building a treehouse. You don't start with the roof — you start with the frame, the beams that hold everything else up. That's exactly what the Latin word *struere* means: "to build."

**Struct** is the building root. It shows up whenever English talks about things put together, piece by piece — buildings, but also plans, sentences, even societies. A "structure" is anything built from parts. To "construct" something is to build it up. To "destroy" it — with *de*, meaning down — is to knock the building down.

Once you notice *struct*, you start seeing hidden buildings everywhere: in "instruct" (to build knowledge into someone's mind), in "infrastructure" (the framework built underneath a city), even in "obstruct" (to build something in someone's way).

$$
\underbrace{struct}_{\text{build}} + \underbrace{ure}_{\text{result of}} = structure
$$

**Now you try:** Take *struct* and add *con-* (together). What do you get, and what do you think it means?

### trans- (8.0s)

## Trans-

Picture a train crossing a bridge from one country to another. The moment it rolls over that bridge, it doesn't just move — it *becomes* something new: a traveler in a foreign land. That's the feeling behind "trans-." It's not just "moving," it's "moving across a line that changes what you are on the other side."

Think of *transport* (carrying something across a distance), *transform* (changing across into a new shape), or *translate* (carrying meaning across from one language into another). Every time, something crosses a boundary — a place, a shape, a language — and comes out different on the far side.

$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = transport$$

**Now you try**: Take the word *transplant*. A gardener digs up a seedling from one pot and moves it "across" into another pot. What is crossing, and what boundary does it cross? Try building your own word with *trans-* and a root you already know, like *form* or *fer*.

### construct (5.4s)

## Construct

Imagine you and your friends want to build a fort out of blankets, chairs, and pillows. No single piece is a fort by itself — a chair is just a chair. But when you gather the pieces and put them together the right way, something new appears: a fort!

That's what the word "construct" means. It means to build something by bringing pieces together on purpose.

Look closely at the word itself. It has a hidden clue inside: "con-" means "together," and "struct" means "to build." So the word is literally telling you what it means!

$$
\underbrace{con}_{together} + \underbrace{struct}_{build} = construct
$$

When you *construct* something — a sentence, a story, a tower of blocks, or an argument in a debate — you are choosing pieces and joining them in an order that makes sense.

**Now you try:** Think of something you built recently — a sandwich, a sandcastle, a Lego robot. What were the small pieces? How did putting them together *on purpose* create something bigger than any single piece?

### import (8.5s)

## Import

Picture a word that travels. "Import" means to bring something in from outside — a country imports mangoes, a computer imports a file, your brain imports a new idea from a book. But look closer at the word itself: it's built from *in-* (meaning "into") and *port* (meaning "carry" or "gate," like the port where ships dock). So import literally means "carry into port."

Here's the twist: you already met this *in-* before, in the word "inspect." But notice import doesn't spell it "in-port" — it spells it "im-port." Why the change? Because *in-* is a shape-shifter. When it sits next to certain sounds, like the "p" in port, it slides into "im-" to make pronunciation smoother. Same brick, different disguise.

$$
\underbrace{im}_{\text{into (shape-shifted)}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = import
$$

**Now you try:** Can you guess why "in-" becomes "im-" in "impossible" too? Say both words out loud and listen for the clue.

### inspect (6.4s)

## Inspect

Have you ever peeked *inside* a closed box before opening it all the way? That careful, close-up look is what "inspect" means.

$$\underbrace{in\text{-}}_{\text{into, within}} + \underbrace{spect}_{\text{look}} = \text{inspect}$$

"Spect" means to look — you'll spot it again in *spectacle* and *spectator*. Add "in-," and the looking turns inward, aiming your eyes deep into something rather than just glancing past it. A teacher who inspects your homework isn't skimming it — she's looking *into* every line. A doctor who inspects a wound leans close, checking every detail.

So "inspect" is a looking word wearing a direction tag: look *in*.

**Now you try:** Next time you get a new toy, don't just look at it — inspect it. Turn it over, peek into its gears or seams, and see what "looking in" reveals that a quick glance would have missed.

### predict (7.4s)

## Predict

Imagine your friend has thrown a ball straight up. Before it comes down, you shout, "It's going to land in your hands!" You haven't seen the future — but you've watched enough balls fall to *say beforehand* what will happen.

That's exactly what "predict" means. Look at its two pieces:

$$\underbrace{pre}_{\text{before}} + \underbrace{dict}_{\text{to say}} = predict$$

The root *dict* is the same "saying" root found in words like "dictate" and "diction" — it's about speaking or declaring something. Add *pre-*, and instead of saying what already happened, you're steering your words forward in time, saying what *will* happen before it does.

Weather forecasters predict rain. Scientists predict eclipses. You predict who'll win a game before it starts.

**Now you try:** Look outside right now. Predict one thing that will happen in the next hour — will a cloud drift by? Will someone walk past your window? Say it out loud before it happens, just like the ball-catcher did.

### reject (8.1s)

## Reject

Picture this: you send a paper airplane flying across the room. Someone catches it, crumples it up, and throws it right back to you. That's what "reject" means — something gets sent back instead of kept.

Think about handing a friend a jar of pickles you made. If they taste one and hand the jar back to you, that's rejecting — sending it back the way it came, away from them and toward you again.

The word is built from two pieces. "Re-" means back, and "-ject" comes from an old root meaning to throw. Put together, "reject" literally means to throw something back.

$$\underbrace{re}_{back} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = reject$$

So whenever you hear "reject," picture a throw in reverse — something offered, then sent flying back.

Now you try: Think of a word using "-ject" you might already know, like "project" (throw forward) or "inject" (throw in). What direction does each one throw?

### transport (7.9s)

## Transport

Picture a bucket brigade passing water over a fence into a neighbor's yard. Someone carries the bucket, and someone aims where it goes over the fence. That's transport: carrying something *across* a boundary.

The word tells you this directly. "Port" is an old root meaning "carry" — you still hear it in "porter," someone who carries your bags. "Trans-" means "across," like a transatlantic flight crossing an ocean. Put them together, and you get "carry across."

$$
\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = transport
$$

Think of the crossing point — a fence, a river, a border — as the boundary. The "steerer" is whoever decides *where* the carrying happens: a truck driver steering goods across a state line, a ferry captain steering passengers across a strait. The carrying root, "port," stays the same no matter what crosses: people, packages, or ideas.

**Now you try:** Can you think of another "port" word — like "export" or "import"? What gets carried, and across what boundary?

### prefix_root_composition (8.0s)

## Prefix Root Composition

Picture this: you meet a knight named "spect" — his whole job is *looking*. Send him *in* and he becomes a spy who peeks inside a box: inspect. Send him *across* and he rides a message to a faraway castle: transport (well, "port" is the carrier root, but watch the same trick). Send "dict," the sayer, out *before* something happens, and he predicts the future. Send "struct," the builder, *together* with others, and they construct a tower. Send "ject," the thrower, *back* against something, and he rejects it. Send "port" *into* a new land, and goods import.

The prefix is a tiny arrow. It doesn't change what the root *means* — it changes where the root is *pointed*.

$$\underbrace{in}_{into} + \underbrace{spect}_{look} = inspect$$
$$\underbrace{trans}_{across} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = transport$$
$$\underbrace{pre}_{before} + \underbrace{dict}_{say} = predict$$
$$\underbrace{con}_{together} + \underbrace{struct}_{build} = construct$$
$$\underbrace{re}_{back} + \underbrace{ject}_{throw} = reject$$
$$\underbrace{im}_{into} + \underbrace{port}_{carry} = import$$

Now you try: "spect" also means look. What word do you get if you aim it *back* — re + spect?

### cross_language_transfer (8.0s)

## Cross Language Transfer

Imagine you learn the word "transport" in English — cars, trains, moving things from place to place. Then in Spanish class you meet "transportar," and something clicks: same idea, same shape, almost the same sound! You didn't have to learn it twice.

That click happens because languages that are cousins often build words from the same bricks. English, Spanish, French, and Portuguese all inherited pieces from Latin, so a brick like "trans-" (across) plus "port" (carry) shows up again and again, just dressed differently.

$$\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{port}_{\text{carry}} = \text{transport (English)}$$
$$\underbrace{trans}_{\text{across}} + \underbrace{portar}_{\text{carry}} = \text{transportar (Spanish)}$$

Once you notice this pattern in one word, you can spot it in others — "predict" and "predicción" share the same trick. Learning to look for shared bricks is a skill, not a fact, and it works in any family of related languages.

**Now you try:** Think of the word "television." What smaller pieces might it be built from? Do you think French or Spanish has a similar-sounding word?
