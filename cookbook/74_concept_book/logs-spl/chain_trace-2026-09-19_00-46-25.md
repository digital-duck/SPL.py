## Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of dissolved solute particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, driven by random molecular motion rather than any directed force. Because individual molecules move randomly, they are simply more likely to wander from a crowded region into an empty one than the reverse — so at the population level, this randomness produces the predictable, directional flow we call diffusion. The process continues until concentration is uniform throughout the system, a state called dynamic equilibrium: molecules still move, but with no *net* directional flow.

**Worked example.** Picture a sealed tank of water in which a spoonful of dye is dropped in one corner. Initially, the dye concentration is high near the drop point and zero elsewhere. Over time, dye molecules spread outward, and the color gradually becomes uniform across the tank. If you tracked concentration $C$ at a fixed point 10 cm from the drop site over several hours, it would rise steadily, then level off once equilibrium is reached — it never reverses and re-concentrates, because there are vastly more ways for molecules to be spread out than clustered together.

**Quantitative treatment.** Because diffusion is a physical transport process governed by a genuine mathematical law, it merits formal treatment: Fick's First Law states that the diffusive flux $J$ (amount of solute crossing a unit area per unit time) is proportional to the concentration gradient:

$$J = -D\frac{dC}{dx}$$

Here $C$ is concentration, $x$ is position, and $D$ is the diffusion coefficient (units of $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$), which depends on the solute, solvent, and temperature. The negative sign captures the essential physics: flux flows *down* the gradient, from high to low concentration.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose glucose diffuses across a thin membrane with $D = 6.7 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}$, membrane thickness $\Delta x = 0.01\ \text{cm}$, and concentrations of $0.5\ \text{mol/L}$ and $0.1\ \text{mol/L}$ on either side. Approximating the gradient as $\Delta C/\Delta x$:

$$J = -D\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = -(6.7\times10^{-6})\left(\frac{0.1-0.5}{0.01}\right) = 2.68\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}\,\text{cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$$

This flux tells a physiologist how quickly glucose crosses a cell membrane passively — a calculation underlying models of nutrient absorption, drug delivery, and cellular respiration.
