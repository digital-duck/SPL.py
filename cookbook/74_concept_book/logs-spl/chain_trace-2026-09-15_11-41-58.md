### 宀 (7.1s)

## 宀

Imagine you are tucking a tiny dollhouse under a blanket, just the roofline peeking out — pointy in the middle, sloping down on both sides. That shape, all by itself, is a Chinese character: 宀. It doesn't mean "house" exactly. It's more like a promise: *whatever stands under me is safe, covered, settled in.*

宀 almost never appears alone in real writing. It's a helper — a radical — that sits on top of other pieces like a roof sits on top of walls. When you spot 宀 at the top of a character, your brain can guess: "something about home, shelter, or being tucked away safely" before you even know the whole word!

Think of it like a costume all "indoor" characters wear. A pig under the roof? That becomes 家, home. A woman under the roof? That becomes 安, peace — because a peaceful place starts with feeling safe indoors.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{\text{something}}_{\text{underneath}} = \text{a sheltered idea}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw a little roof shape. What would you tuck underneath it to make your own "safe place" word?

### 女 (6.2s)

## 女

Long ago, someone wanted to draw a person kneeling gently, hands folded, sitting back on their heels — a calm, graceful pose. That simple sketch became the character 女, meaning "woman."

Why does this feel right? Because early picture-writers didn't draw faces — they drew *posture*, the shape a body makes. Kneeling was a common, respectful way to sit, and the character froze that shape in ink.

But 女 is more than one word. It became a building block — a piece other characters borrow whenever they want to point toward mothers, sisters, daughters, or family bonds. Think of it as a root that many family words grow from.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{kneeling woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{好 (good, meaning "woman with child")}
$$

That's the trick: combine 女 with another small picture, and you get a brand-new idea.

**Now you try:** Look at 妈 (mother) or 姐 (older sister). Can you spot 女 hiding inside each one?

### 子 (6.3s)

## 子

Picture a baby wrapped snugly in a blanket, tiny arms tucked in, sound asleep. That's exactly what the character 子 draws — a swaddled infant. Look at the shape: a round head on top, and below it, two little strokes like wrapped arms and a bundled body. No legs kicking around — just a cozy, bound-up baby.

Once you see "baby" in 子, something fun happens: this character loves to hang out at the end of other words, turning them into things. Add it to 房 (house) and you get 房子, "house-thing" — a house! Add it to 桌 (table-ish idea) and you get 桌子, a table. It's like a friendly little tag saying "this is a noun, a real object you can point to."

So 子 wears two hats: alone, it's simply "child." Attached to another character, it becomes a quiet helper turning ideas into named things.

Now you try: take 椅 (chair) and add 子. What word do you get, and what does it name?

$$\underbrace{椅}_{\text{chair}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{noun suffix}} = \text{椅子 (chair)}$$

### 字 (8.0s)

## 字

Imagine a little kid, safe under a roof, being raised at home. That's the picture inside this very character — 字, the word that means "character" itself!

Look closely: on top is 宀 (a roof), and underneath is 子 (a child). A child under a roof — someone being cared for, growing up, learning. That's exactly what happens to a Chinese character too! Most characters aren't born whole. They start as simple pieces, and then get "raised" into something new, just like a child raised at home grows into who they become.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \underbrace{字}_{\text{character}}$$

That's why the whole system of studying how characters connect and grow from each other is called **ZiNets** (字 + Networks) — named after this very character! The writing system literally named itself using its own idea of "growing up."

**Now you try:** Can you think of a character you know that seems "built" from two smaller pictures? Draw it and guess what story it tells!

### 安 (5.7s)

## 安

Imagine coming home after a long day outside — the wind is cold, your feet are tired, and then you step through the door into a warm, quiet room. Everything inside you relaxes. That feeling of "ahhh, I'm safe now" is exactly what this character captures.

Look at its shape: a roof sits over a woman sitting calmly inside. Long ago, a home with someone settled safely inside it was the picture of peace — no danger outside could reach her.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{女}_{woman} = \underbrace{安}_{peace,\ safe,\ settled}
$$

That's why this character shows up in words like 平安 (safe and sound) and 安全 (safety) — both are about being protected, calm, and free from harm.

**Now you try:** Picture your own safest, coziest spot — maybe your bed, a grandparent's kitchen, or a reading nook. Draw a roof over a stick figure sitting there. That's the feeling this character was born to show.

### cultural_etymology (6.7s)

## Cultural Etymology

Imagine you found an old photograph buried in your backyard — a picture of your great-great-great-grandparents standing in their house, thousands of years ago. That's what happens every time you look closely at a Chinese character. It isn't just a symbol for a sound or an idea — it's a tiny snapshot, frozen in time.

Take 安 (ān), the character for "peace" or "safety." Long ago, someone drew a roof over a woman, meaning a woman safely inside her home. That image never left — it's still sitting right there inside the character today, like a fossil preserved in rock.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = 安 \;(\text{peace, safety})$$

Even more amazing: 字 (zì), the word for "character" itself, is built the same way — a roof over a child, showing a baby being raised under a roof, nurtured into something new. The system describes its own birth.

**Now you try:** Find 安 in a text. Can you picture the roof and the person underneath, the way the first scribe imagined it?
