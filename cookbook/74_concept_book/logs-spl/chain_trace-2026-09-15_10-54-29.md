### 宀 (7.4s)

## 宀

Imagine you are drawing a tiny house from above — not the walls, just the roof, sloping down on both sides to keep the rain out. That slanted line with two little strokes hanging off its edges is 宀. It never stands alone; it always sits on top of something else, like a hat waiting for a head.

Why does this feel right? Because a roof's whole job is to cover something. So whenever you see 宀 perched at the top of a character, your brain should whisper: "something underneath is being sheltered, kept safe, settled in one place."

Think of it as a promise: whatever character sits beneath this roof gets a home, a family, or a safe space. The roof itself carries no sound and no meaning alone — it's a signal, not a word.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof/shelter}} + \underbrace{\text{something below}}_{\text{person, object, idea}} = \text{a housed, settled thing}$$

Now you try: look for 宀 sitting on top of other characters. Ask yourself — what is being sheltered underneath this little roof?

### 女 (7.4s)

## 女

Imagine an ancient artist trying to draw a person kneeling gracefully, hands folded, sitting back on her heels — a pose of quiet dignity used in old ceremonies. That curved, folded shape became the character 女, meaning "woman."

Why does this feel right? Because early Chinese writing loved to sketch what it saw. A standing person became a different character, but someone kneeling, arms crossed in front, turned into this soft, bending shape — 女.

Here's the wonderful part: this simple kneeling figure didn't stay alone. It became a building block — a "semantic anchor" — inside many other characters about family and relationships. Whenever 女 appears inside a bigger character, it's a clue that the meaning has something to do with women, mothers, or family ties.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman, kneeling}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{好 (good, pleasing)}
$$

Now you try: Next time you see a character with 女 tucked inside it, pause and guess — could it be about a mother, a sister, or family? Draw your own kneeling 女 and see how it feels to write.

### 子 (6.1s)

## 子

Picture a tiny bundle wrapped snugly in a blanket, with just a round head and two little arms peeking out. That's exactly what the character 子 looked like thousands of years ago — a drawing of a swaddled baby! Over time the picture got simpler and turned into the neat little character you see today, but if you squint, you can still imagine that wrapped-up shape.

Because 子 started as "baby" or "child," it grew to mean something even bigger: any young person, a son or daughter, or even something small and seed-like (like 种子, a "seed"). But 子 has a second job too — it loves to hop onto the end of other words to turn them into nouns, almost like adding a friendly little stamp that says "this is a thing!" For example:

$$
\underbrace{桌}_{table} + \underbrace{子}_{noun\ suffix} = 桌子\ (table)
$$

Now you try: think of 椅 (chair-root) — what happens when you add 子 to the end?

### 字 (7.4s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny child, tucked safely under the roof of a house, being raised and taught. That is the story hiding inside the character 字 (zì), the very word that means "character" in Chinese!

Why does this feel right? Because that is exactly what a character is — something small that grows up under care, learns its shape, and becomes part of a family. The roof (宀) protects and gathers; the child (子) is the new thing being formed. Put a child under a roof, and you get a character being "raised" into existence.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字 \text{ (character, to raise)}
$$

This is so central to the Chinese writing system that scholars even named a whole way of studying characters "ZiNets" — networks of 字, each one grown from earlier pieces, just like children grow up in families.

**Now you try:** Can you think of something else that gets "raised" under protection before it's ready — a seedling in a garden, a puppy in a home? Draw a roof over it!

### 安 (6.3s)

## 安

Imagine a little girl comes home after a long day. Outside, the wind is loud and the world feels big and unpredictable. But once she steps under her roof, sits down, and is simply *there* — safe, still, no longer needing to run or hide — everything softens. That feeling has a name.

The character 安 paints exactly this picture: a roof (宀) with a woman (女) resting quietly beneath it. Not doing anything dramatic — just being sheltered, settled, at ease. That stillness under shelter *is* peace.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = \underbrace{安}_{\text{peace, at ease}}
$$

You see this same feeling stretched into bigger words: 平安 (píngān) means everything is calm and safe, like after a storm passes. 安全 (ānquán) means safety itself — nothing can hurt you.

**Now you try:** Draw a roof, then imagine someone you love resting calmly underneath it. What does that safe, quiet feeling remind you of in your own home?

### cultural_etymology (11.3s)

## Cultural Etymology

Imagine digging up a very old photograph — not of a person, but of an idea. That is what happens every time you look closely at a Chinese character. It was drawn thousands of years ago to show a picture, and somehow, that picture is still hiding inside the character you see today.

Take the character 安, meaning *peace* or *safety*. Long ago, someone drew a woman sitting calmly under a roof. Why did that feel peaceful? Because home, with someone safe inside it, has meant "all is well" for as long as people have had homes.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = 安 \text{ (peace)}$$

That same roof shows up again and again, quietly holding other ideas together. Under it, a child (子) becomes 字, the word for "character" itself — because characters, like children, are born from simpler parts and grow into something whole.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字 \text{ (character)}$$

**Now you try:** Pick a character you know. Ask: what tiny picture might be hiding inside it — and why did that picture feel like the right one?
