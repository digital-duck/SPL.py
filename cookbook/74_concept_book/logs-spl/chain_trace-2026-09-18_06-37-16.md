### 一 (0.0s)

## 一

You already know 一 (yī) as "one" — the simplest possible stroke, a single horizontal line. But 一 is also doing quiet structural work across the writing system, and once you see it, you'll spot it everywhere.

First, as radical 1 (Kangxi radical), 一 is the ancestor of a whole family of characters. It's not just "the character that means one" — it's a *classifying unit* that dictionaries use to group characters by shared form or meaning. Characters like 二 (two) and 三 (three) build directly on it by stacking strokes, showing you a rare case where a number's *shape* literally encodes its *value*: one line, two lines, three lines.

Second, 一 shows up as an intensifier or totality marker, not just a count. In 一生 (yīshēng, "one's whole life") or 一切 (yīqiè, "everything"), 一 pushes toward *entireness* — "all of it," "the whole thing" — rather than the number 1 you'd use in arithmetic. This is a pattern worth noticing: a character's core meaning (unity, oneness) can stretch into related meanings (wholeness, totality) depending on context.

Third, in spoken math or when reading digits aloud one at a time (like a phone number or ID), 一 is sometimes pronounced *yāo* instead of *yī* — this avoids confusion with 七 (qī, "seven"), which sounds similar in noisy conditions.

3. 一年 (yī nián)

### 丨 (0.0s)

## 丨

You've likely seen the vertical bar in expressions like $a \mid b$, read "$a$ divides $b$." At first glance it's just shorthand — but it's actually defining a precise mathematical relationship, not just a notation trick.

**Formal definition.** For integers $a$ and $b$, we say $a \mid b$ if there exists an integer $k$ such that

$$b = ka$$

So $3 \mid 12$ because $12 = 4 \cdot 3$. But $3 \nmid 10$, since no integer $k$ satisfies $10 = 3k$.

**Structure worth noticing.** This relation isn't random — it behaves in predictable ways:

- **Reflexive:** $a \mid a$ for every integer $a$ (take $k = 1$).
- **Transitive:** if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$. (Proof sketch: $b = ka$ and $c = mb$, so $c = mka$ — and $mk$ is still an integer.)
- **Every integer divides 0**, since $0 = 0 \cdot a$.
- **Only $\pm 1$ divide every integer.**

Because of transitivity and reflexivity, divisibility acts like an ordering on the positive integers — not a full ranking like $\leq$, but a structure mathematicians call a *partial order*: some numbers are comparable ($2 \mid 8$), others aren't ($5$ and $8$ share no divisibility relation at all).

This same bar reappears elsewhere in math — in conditional probability $P(A \mid B)$, or set-builder notation $\{x \mid x > 0\}$ — always signaling "under this condition." Divisibility is the cleanest place to see its logical bite.

**Practice problem:** Using the transitivity rule above, explain why $4 \mid 60$ given that $4 \mid 20$ and $20 \mid 60$. Then find a counterexample showing $a \mid c$ does *not* imply $c \mid a$.

### 十 (0.0s)

## 十

You already know 十 means ten. But look at what it's built from: 一 (one, the horizontal stroke) and 丨 (a vertical stroke). Put them together and you get a cross — and that cross is doing real work as a number symbol.

Here's the structure: in early counting systems, marks for 1 through 9 were often just tally strokes — one, two, three lines stacking up. But ten is where a system needs a new idea, because you can't just draw ten strokes and expect anyone to read it at a glance. So 十 uses a different strategy: instead of *repeating* a unit, it makes a new symbol that stands for a *group*. This is the same jump every number system has to make. In Roman numerals, `IIIIIIIIII` (ten strokes) is abandoned in favor of `X`. In our Arabic system, we don't write ten ones either — we switch columns and write `10`. The cross shape 十 is Chinese writing's version of that same leap: one clean mark for "a full group of ten."

The pattern that matters here is *place value logic*, even in a non-positional script. 十 doesn't get combined with 一 through 九 by repetition — it gets combined by *position and multiplication*. 二十 isn't "twenty strokes," it's $2 \times 10$. 三十 is $3 \times 10$. This is the seed of how Chinese builds all its larger numbers, and it mirrors how you already write numbers in base ten: the digit "2" in "20" means $2 \times 10^1$.

### 又 (0.0s)

## 又

You've probably picked up 又 (yòu) as a word that pops up when something happens *again*, or when two things are true *at the same time*. That intuition is a great start — now let's look at how it actually works as a structural piece of the sentence.

Think of 又 as a repetition or addition marker that attaches to a verb or adjective, not to a noun. It signals: "this action/state, one more time" or "this trait, alongside another trait." A useful pattern to notice is the paired construction **又...又...**, which links two qualities or actions happening together, similar to how in English we might say "both... and..." — for example, describing weather that is both hot and humid, you'd frame it as 又热又闷 ("both hot and stuffy").

There's a subtlety worth flagging: 又 describes something that *already happened* or is a *known repeating pattern*, which is different from 再 (zài), which is used for something that *will* repeat in the future. This distinction is a small but important rule: 又 = past/realized repetition, 再 = future/intended repetition. Getting this pairing right is a mark of moving from beginner to intermediate fluency, because English "again" doesn't make that time distinction at all — you have to track it yourself.

**Practice problem:** Using 又...又..., write one short sentence in Chinese (with pinyin) describing a fruit or food that has two qualities at once — for example, something that is both sweet and sour. Then write a second sentence using 又 alone to say "it rained again yesterday."

### 土 (0.0s)

## 土

You already know 土 (tǔ) as earth or dust — think of a dirt path, or clay you'd shape into a bowl. But look at the character's structure and something interesting appears: 土 is built from 一 (a horizontal stroke, "one") stacked over 十 (a cross shape, "ten"). This isn't a coincidence of strokes — ancient scribes were drawing a simplified picture of a mound of earth with a plant or marker rising from it, and over centuries the pictograph flattened into these two familiar components.

Here's the structural pattern worth noticing: 土 is one of the oldest **radicals** (部首, bùshǒu) in the writing system — a building-block component that organizes meaning across hundreds of other characters. Any character containing 土 usually relates to earth, ground, soil, or things made from it: 地 (dì, land/ground), 城 (chéng, city wall — originally an earthen wall), 塊 (kuài, a lump or clump), 埋 (mái, to bury). When you spot 土 inside an unfamiliar character, you can guess its meaning lies somewhere in the domain of "earth-related" even before you know the pronunciation.

This is also why 土 extends metaphorically to *local*, *indigenous*, and even *unsophisticated* (as in 土氣, tǔqì, "uncultured/rustic") — things tied to the native soil, as opposed to refined or foreign. And in ancient music theory, 土 names one of the **eight instrument categories** (八音, bāyīn), grouping instruments made from clay, like the ocarina-like 塤 (xūn).

### 寸 (0.0s)

## 寸

You've likely met 寸 (cùn) as "inch" — but it's really a **unit definition**, and units are one of the first formal structures you meet in measurement theory. A unit is not a fixed physical distance forever; it's a *reference length* that a whole system of measurement is built on top of.

Historically, 1 寸 was defined as roughly the width of a thumb — hence the character's shape, built from 一 (a mark) placed near 又 (a hand), pointing at a hand's own measure. That's the intuition. The structure is that every larger unit in the traditional Chinese system is a fixed multiple of 寸:

$$
1 \text{ 尺 (chǐ)} = 10 \text{ 寸}, \qquad 1 \text{ 寸} = 10 \text{ 分 (fēn)}
$$

Notice the pattern: this is a **base-10 positional system for length**, exactly like the metric system's meter → decimeter → centimeter chain. If $L$ is a length in 寸, you can write it in mixed units the same way you'd expand a decimal number:

$$
L = 10a + b, \quad a = \lfloor L/10 \rfloor \text{ (chǐ)}, \quad b = L \bmod 10 \text{ (fen equivalents)}
$$

This is the same "unit conversion by powers of a base" idea you'll formalize later with dimensional analysis and unit conversion factors in physics — a ratio like $\frac{1\text{ 尺}}{10\text{ 寸}} = 1$ lets you multiply through any measurement without changing its actual size.

**Practice problem:** A board is measured as 3 尺 and 7 寸 long. Express its total length in 寸 only, then convert that number to 分.

### 寺 (0.0s)

## 寺

You already know 寺 (sì) as "temple" — the place with incense, chanting, maybe a giant Buddha statue. But look at its two building blocks: 土 (soil, earth) sitting on top of 寸 (a small unit of measurement, roughly an inch, also used for "hand" or "controlled action"). That combination is a clue about a much older meaning.

Long before 寺 meant "temple," it meant a government office — specifically, an administrative building where officials measured, recorded, and managed things: land, taxes, law. Think of 土 as "the land/territory being governed" and 寸 as "precise, regulated control" — together, "a place where the land is administered by exact rule." Only later, when Buddhism arrived in China around the 1st century CE, foreign monks were first housed in a government reception office called the 鴻臚寺 (Honglu Si). When they built permanent religious quarters, the name 寺 stuck — and its meaning shifted from "government office" to "temple," dragging Buddhist and eventually Islamic (mosque) usage along with it.

This is a common pattern in Chinese character history: a character's *radical components* often preserve an older meaning that the character's *current usage* has drifted away from. The rule to remember: $\text{character meaning today} \neq \text{sum of its parts' original meanings}$ — but the parts are still fossils of an earlier semantic layer.
