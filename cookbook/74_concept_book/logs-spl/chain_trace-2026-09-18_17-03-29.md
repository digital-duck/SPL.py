## Observation

Observation is the deliberate act of noticing, measuring, and recording phenomena in the natural world. It is the foundational empirical act of science: before a hypothesis can be formed, tested, or discarded, something must first be seen, counted, timed, or otherwise registered as data. What separates scientific observation from casual noticing is systematicity — the observer specifies in advance what will be measured, how, and under what conditions, so that the record can be checked, repeated, and compared against other records.

Consider a simple worked example. A biology student wants to know whether a species of moth is more active at dusk than at midday. She sets up a fixed observation protocol: count moths landing on a lit trap every 15 minutes, for two hours at dusk and two hours at midday, on five separate days. The resulting counts are raw observational data — no interpretation yet, just numbers tied to a time and a condition. Only after this data exists can she ask whether the pattern is real or due to chance, which is a separate act of inference (statistics, later in this course) built on top of observation.

The problem-solving skill worth practicing here is designing an observation protocol that avoids two common failure modes: selection bias, where the method of observing systematically favors certain outcomes over others (e.g., only checking the trap when moths happen to be visible), and confounding, where an uncontrolled variable changes alongside the one being studied (e.g., dusk observations happening on cooler, calmer evenings than midday ones, so "time of day" and "temperature" are tangled together). A well-designed observation protocol fixes as many conditions as possible — same trap, same light, same recorder, same weather range — so that when a pattern shows up in the data, it can plausibly be attributed to the variable of interest rather than to how the data was collected.

Try this: suppose you are asked to observe whether traffic noise affects how long birds sing near a city park. Before recording a single measurement, write down exactly what you will count, how often, at which locations, and what you will hold constant. That written protocol — not the data itself — is the real test of whether your observation will support a meaningful conclusion later.

## Scientific Model

A scientific model is a simplified representation of a system, process, or object that is too large, too small, too complex, too fast, or too dangerous to observe or manipulate directly. Models range from physical replicas (a ball-and-stick molecule) to mathematical equations (Newton's law of gravitation) to computational simulations (a climate model). What makes a model *scientific*, rather than merely imaginative, is that it is built from and constrained by observation: its structure and parameters are fit to experimental data, and its predictions must be testable against further data. Critically, every model has a domain of validity — a range of conditions under which it reliably matches reality — outside of which it breaks down.

**Worked example.** The ideal gas law, $PV = nRT$, models the relationship between pressure, volume, and temperature of a gas by treating gas particles as point masses with no intermolecular forces. This model was built from observations (Boyle's and Charles's experiments) and works remarkably well for gases at low pressure and high temperature, where particles are far apart and interactions are negligible. But at high pressure or low temperature, real gas molecules occupy volume and attract one another, so the model's predictions diverge from measurement — for example, it fails to predict that gases condense into liquids. This is not a "flaw" to be embarrassed about; it is the expected behavior of a model whose simplifying assumptions no longer hold.

**Problem-solving application.** When you encounter a model, the productive question is never just "what does it say?" but "under what conditions was it validated, and am I inside that range?" Suppose you're asked to estimate the pressure of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ gas compressed to a small volume at low temperature — conditions near condensation. Applying $PV = nRT$ naively will overestimate pressure, because it ignores intermolecular attraction and molecular volume. The correct problem-solving move is to recognize the model's limits and switch to a refined one, such as the van der Waals equation, $\left(P + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT$, which adds correction terms for those very effects. This illustrates the general skill of working with scientific models: identify the assumptions baked into a model, check whether your scenario satisfies them, and choose or adapt a model whose domain of validity actually covers your problem — rather than trusting any single equation as universally true.
