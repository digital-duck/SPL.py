## Social Network

A **social network** is a model of relationships among a set of people, organizations, or other entities. It has two ingredients: **nodes** (the actors) and **edges** (the ties between them, such as friendship, following, collaboration, or messaging). Edges may be *undirected* (mutual friendship) or *directed* (A follows B), and may carry weights (how often two people interact). The key idea is that structure matters: who you are connected to, and how those connections are arranged, often explains behavior better than your individual attributes alone.

In code, a social network is simply a graph. The most common representation is an adjacency list, which stores each node's neighbors.

### Worked example

Consider five students with these friendships: Ana–Ben, Ana–Cy, Ben–Cy, Cy–Dee, Dee–Eli.

```python
friends = {
    "Ana": {"Ben", "Cy"},
    "Ben": {"Ana", "Cy"},
    "Cy":  {"Ana", "Ben", "Dee"},
    "Dee": {"Cy", "Eli"},
    "Eli": {"Dee"},
}
degree = {p: len(f) for p, f in friends.items()}
```

The **degree** of a node is its number of ties: Cy has degree 3, Ana and Ben have 2, Dee has 2, and Eli has 1. Cy is the most connected person. Notice also that Ana, Ben, and Cy are all mutually connected. This triangle is a tight cluster, which is typical of real social networks.

A useful measure of clustering is the **clustering coefficient** of a node: the fraction of pairs of its neighbors who are themselves connected,

$$C_i = \frac{\text{number of edges among neighbors of } i}{\binom{k_i}{2}},$$

where $k_i$ is the degree of node $i$. For Ana, $k=2$, and her two neighbors (Ben, Cy) are connected, so $C_{\text{Ana}} = 1/1 = 1$. For Cy, $k=3$ gives $\binom{3}{2}=3$ possible pairs; only Ana–Ben is connected, so $C_{\text{Cy}} = 1/3$.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem:** Eli wants to be introduced to Ana. What is the shortest chain of introductions, and who is the critical person?

Use breadth-first search, which explores the network one "hop" at a time:

```python
from collections import deque

def shortest_path(g, start, goal):
    queue, seen = deque([[start]]), {start}
    while queue:
        path = queue.popleft()
        if path[-1] == goal:
            return path
        for nb in g[path[-1]] - seen:
            seen.add(nb)
            queue.append(path + [nb])

print(shortest_path(friends, "Eli", "Ana"))
# ['Eli', 'Dee', 'Cy', 'Ana']
```

The path has length 3, so Eli is three hops from Ana. Every route between the left cluster and Eli passes through Cy and Dee. Removing either disconnects the network, so they are **bridges** or *cut vertices*. This is why analysts look for such nodes when studying how information, rumors, or diseases spread: a few well-placed individuals can control the flow between groups.

Large real networks show the "small-world" property: despite millions of nodes, typical shortest paths are only about six hops long, because a few long-range ties connect otherwise distant clusters.

## Affiliation Network

### Definition

An **affiliation network** (also called a two-mode or bipartite network) records which *actors* belong to which *groups or events*. There are two kinds of nodes, such as people and clubs, authors and papers, or actors and movies. Edges connect only nodes of different kinds. A person links to a club, but a person never links directly to another person.

The data are stored in an **incidence matrix** $B$, with one row per actor and one column per group:

$$B_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if actor } i \text{ belongs to group } j \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

Affiliation data lets us infer relationships that were never observed directly. Two actors who share a group are indirectly connected. Two groups that share members are also indirectly connected.

### Worked example

Four students join three clubs:

| | Chess | Robotics | Band |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ana | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| Ben | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| Cai | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| Dee | 0 | 1 | 1 |

To find how many clubs each pair of students shares, project onto the actors by computing $A = BB^{T}$. Entry $A_{ik}$ counts the groups that actors $i$ and $k$ have in common, and the diagonal $A_{ii}$ gives each actor's number of memberships.

- Ana and Ben share Chess, so $A_{\text{Ana,Ben}} = 1$.
- Ana and Cai share Robotics, so $A_{\text{Ana,Cai}} = 1$.
- Cai and Dee share Robotics and Band, so $A_{\text{Cai,Dee}} = 2$.
- Ben shares nothing with Cai or Dee, so those entries are $0$.

Projecting onto the groups uses $G = B^{T}B$. Here $G_{\text{Robotics,Band}} = 2$ because Cai and Dee belong to both clubs.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** The school wants to spread a flyer through the students. Whom should it target, and which clubs act as bridges?

**Step 1: Find the most connected actors.** Count each student's distinct contacts, meaning the nonzero off-diagonal entries in their row of $A$. Ana has two contacts (Ben and Cai), Cai has two (Ana and Dee), Ben has one, and Dee has one. Ana and Cai are the best seeds.

**Step 2: Find the bridging group.** Robotics contains Ana, Cai, and Dee, so it has the most members. It is also the only club that connects Ana to the Cai–Dee pair. If Robotics were removed, Ana and Ben would be split off from Cai and Dee.

**Step 3: Interpret the projection with care.** The projection loses information. In $A$, Cai–Dee has weight 2, which could mean two separate interactions or one shared membership counted twice. A club with 50 members also creates 1,225 student pairs, but those ties are probably weaker than the pair from a 3-person club. Analysts often normalize the weights, for example by dividing by group size, to correct for this.

**Takeaway.** Keep the bipartite structure when group size matters. Project it only when you need a one-mode network.
