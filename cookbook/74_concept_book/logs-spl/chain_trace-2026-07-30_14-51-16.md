### neuron (0.0s)

## Neuron

**Definition.** A *neuron* (or *artificial neuron*) is the fundamental computational unit of a neural network. Formally, given an input vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, a weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n)^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, a scalar bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$, and a fixed nonlinear function $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, the neuron computes a scalar output

$$y = \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b\right) = \sigma\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b\right).$$

The inner product $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$ accumulates a weighted sum of inputs shifted by the bias; applying $\sigma$ to this quantity introduces nonlinearity, enabling the neuron to represent something beyond a simple linear threshold.

**Worked Example.** Consider a neuron with $n = 2$ inputs, weights $\mathbf{w} = (0.5,\,-1.0)^{\top}$, bias $b = 0.3$, and $\sigma(z) = \max(0, z)$. For input $\mathbf{x} = (2.0,\,1.0)^{\top}$:

$$z = (0.5)(2.0) + (-1.0)(1.0) + 0.3 = 1.0 - 1.0 + 0.3 = 0.3,$$
$$y = \max(0,\,0.3) = 0.3.$$

Now change $x_2$ to $3.0$: the weighted sum becomes $z = 1.0 - 3.0 + 0.3 = -1.7$, so $y = \max(0,-1.7) = 0$. The neuron produces no output — it is *silent* — demonstrating that the choice of $\sigma$ directly governs which inputs elicit a response.

**Key Theorem (Universal Approximation, informal).** A single hidden layer of neurons with a non-polynomial choice of $\sigma$ can approximate any continuous function $f : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ to arbitrary precision, provided the layer is sufficiently wide. This result, due to Cybenko (1989) and Hornik (1991), justifies stacking neuron layers to represent complex mappings without specifying a functional form in advance.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

x1, x2, w1, w2, b = sp.symbols('x1 x2 w1 w2 b', real=True)

z = w1*x1 + w2*x2 + b
sigma = sp.Piecewise((0, z < 0), (z, True))

vals = {w1: sp.Rational(1,2), w2: -1, b: sp.Rational(3,10),
        x1: 2, x2: 1}
print("z =", z.subs(vals))           # 3/10
print("sigma(z) =", sigma.subs(vals)) # 3/10

# Partial derivative of the output w.r.t. x1
print("dy/dx1 =", sp.diff(sigma, x1).subs(vals))
```

### bias (0.0s)

## Bias

**Definition.** Let $\hat{\theta}$ be an estimator of an unknown parameter $\theta$. The *bias* of $\hat{\theta}$ is defined as

$$\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta,$$

where the expectation is taken over all possible samples of the data. An estimator is called *unbiased* if $\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = 0$, meaning its expected value equals the true parameter it seeks to estimate. Bias is a systematic error: it persists no matter how large the sample grows, distinguishing it from random sampling variability, which vanishes as $n \to \infty$.

**Worked Example.** Suppose $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ are independent draws from a population with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Consider the sample variance computed with denominator $n$:

$$S_n^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^2, \qquad \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i.$$

A direct calculation shows $\mathbb{E}[S_n^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$, so

$$\operatorname{Bias}(S_n^2) = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = -\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.$$

The estimator systematically *underestimates* the true variance. Replacing the denominator with $n-1$ yields the corrected sample variance $s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2$, which satisfies $\mathbb{E}[s^2] = \sigma^2$ and is therefore unbiased.

**Key Theorem (Bias–Variance Decomposition).** For any estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta$, the mean squared error (MSE) decomposes as

$$\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2\right] = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \left[\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\theta})\right]^2.$$

This identity reveals the fundamental trade-off in statistical estimation: reducing bias often inflates variance, and vice versa. An unbiased estimator is not automatically preferable; if a small, controlled bias substantially reduces variance, the biased estimator may achieve lower MSE in practice.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

n, sigma2 = sp.symbols('n sigma^2', positive=True)

# Bias of the naive sample variance (denominator n)
E_Sn2 = (n - 1) / n * sigma2
bias   = sp.simplify(E_Sn2 - sigma2)
print("Bias(S_n^2) =", bias)          # -sigma^2/n

# Confirm corrected estimator is unbiased
E_s2 = sp.simplify((n / (n - 1)) * E_Sn2)
print("E[s^2]      =", E_s2)          # sigma^2
```

### weight (0.0s)

## Weight

**Definition.** In Newtonian mechanics, the *weight* of an object is the gravitational force exerted on it by a massive body (typically Earth). For an object of mass $m$ located near Earth's surface, weight is defined as

$$W = mg,$$

where $g \approx 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ is the standard acceleration due to gravity. Weight is a vector quantity directed toward the center of the attracting body; its SI unit is the **newton** (N $= \mathrm{kg\cdot m\cdot s^{-2}}$). Mass, by contrast, is an intrinsic scalar property of matter measured in kilograms. The distinction is fundamental: a 70 kg astronaut retains that mass on the Moon but experiences a weight of only $70 \times 1.62 \approx 113\ \mathrm{N}$, compared with $70 \times 9.81 \approx 687\ \mathrm{N}$ on Earth.

**Worked Example.** A textbook of mass $m = 1.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ rests on a desk. Determine its weight and the normal force the desk exerts on it.

Apply Newton's second law in the vertical direction. Since the book is in static equilibrium, the net force is zero:

$$\sum F_y = N - W = 0 \implies N = W = mg = 1.5 \times 9.81 = 14.715\ \mathrm{N}.$$

The desk therefore pushes upward on the book with a normal force of approximately $14.7\ \mathrm{N}$.

**Key Theorem (Universal Gravitation and Surface Approximation).** Newton's law of universal gravitation states that the magnitude of the gravitational force between two point masses $M$ and $m$ separated by distance $r$ is

$$F = \frac{GMm}{r^2},$$

where $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2\cdot kg^{-2}}$ is the gravitational constant. Setting $M = M_\oplus$ and $r = R_\oplus$ (Earth's mean radius), one recovers the surface approximation $W = mg$ with $g = GM_\oplus / R_\oplus^2$. This confirms that $g$ is not a universal constant but depends on the planet and altitude.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
print(f"Computed g = {g_val:.4f} m/s^2")

```

### activation_function (0.0s)

## Activation Function

**Definition.** An *activation function* is a mapping $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ applied element-wise to the pre-activation (weighted sum) of a neuron, introducing the nonlinearity required for a neural network to approximate functions beyond the affine class. Formally, given input vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, weight vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$, the neuron output is

$$z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b, \qquad a = \sigma(z).$$

Without a nonlinear $\sigma$, stacking multiple layers collapses to a single affine transformation; the universal approximation theorem requires at least one non-polynomial activation.

**Worked Example.** Consider the *Rectified Linear Unit* (ReLU), defined by $\sigma(z) = \max(0, z)$. Let $\mathbf{w} = (1, -2)^\top$, $b = 0.5$, and $\mathbf{x} = (3, 1)^\top$. Then

$$z = (1)(3) + (-2)(1) + 0.5 = 1.5, \qquad a = \max(0,\, 1.5) = 1.5.$$

Had the input been $\mathbf{x} = (0, 1)^\top$, we would obtain $z = -1.5$ and $a = 0$: the neuron is *silent*, creating the sparse activations that make deep ReLU networks computationally efficient.

**Key Theorem (Universal Approximation, Cybenko 1989).** Let $\sigma$ be any continuous, bounded, and non-constant function. For every continuous $f : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a single hidden-layer network $F$ with activation $\sigma$ such that

$$\sup_{\mathbf{x} \in [0,1]^n} |F(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x})| < \varepsilon.$$

*Proof sketch.* The set of functions representable by such networks forms a dense subset of $C([0,1]^n)$ under the sup-norm; the argument uses the Stone–Weierstrass theorem and the fact that shifted and scaled copies of a non-constant $\sigma$ can approximate any indicator function on a hyperplane.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Max, diff, plot

z = symbols('z', real=True)

relu = Max(0, z)

d_relu = diff(relu, z)

print("ReLU derivative:", d_relu)

plot(relu, (z, -3, 3), title="ReLU activation")
```

Running this cell confirms that $\frac{d}{dz}\text{ReLU}(z)$ is the Heaviside step function: it equals $1$ for $z > 0$ and $0$ for $z < 0$, and is undefined at $z = 0$. This piecewise-constant derivative is what allows gradient-based training to proceed efficiently in deep networks, since the gradient neither shrinks nor explodes as it passes through an active ReLU unit.

### layers (0.0s)

## Layers

**Definition.** A *layer* in a neural network is a function $f^{(\ell)} : \mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell-1}} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}$ that transforms an input vector into an output vector. It does so by composing a linear map with an element-wise nonlinearity. Concretely, given a weight matrix $W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell \times n_{\ell-1}}$, a bias vector $\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}$, and an activation function $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, layer $\ell$ computes

$$\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = \sigma\!\left(W^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\right),$$

where $\sigma$ is applied coordinate-wise and $\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}$ is the network input. A network of $L$ layers composes these maps: $F = f^{(L)} \circ f^{(L-1)} \circ \cdots \circ f^{(1)}$.

**Worked Example.** Consider a two-layer network with $n_0 = 2$, $n_1 = 3$, $n_2 = 1$ and ReLU activation $\sigma(z) = \max(0, z)$. Set input $\mathbf{x} = (1, -1)^\top$ and

$$W^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \mathbf{0}.$$

Computing $W^{(1)}\mathbf{x}$ gives $(1,\,-1,\,0)^\top$; applying ReLU coordinate-wise yields $\mathbf{h}^{(1)} = (1,\,0,\,0)^\top$. With $W^{(2)} = (2,\ 3,\ -1)$ and $b^{(2)} = 0$, the second layer produces $\mathbf{h}^{(2)} = 2{\cdot}1 + 3{\cdot}0 + (-1){\cdot}0 = 2$.

**Key Theorem (Universal Approximation).** Let $\sigma$ be any continuous, non-polynomial activation function. For every continuous function $g : [0,1]^d \to \mathbb{R}$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a single-hidden-layer network $F$ with sufficiently many units such that $\sup_{\mathbf{x}} |F(\mathbf{x}) - g(\mathbf{x})| < \varepsilon$. This theorem guarantees representational power in principle but gives no bound on the required width; deep architectures can achieve the same approximation with exponentially fewer parameters.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

x1, x2 = sp.symbols('x1 x2')
W1 = sp.Matrix([[1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]])
b1 = sp.zeros(3, 1)
x  = sp.Matrix([x1, x2])

relu = lambda v: sp.Matrix([sp.Max(0, vi) for vi in v])
h1 = relu(W1 * x + b1)

W2 = sp.Matrix([[2, 3, -1]])
output = W2 * h1
sp.pprint(sp.simplify(output[0, 0]))
```

Substituting concrete values reproduces the worked example and enables symbolic exploration of how the piecewise-linear output boundary shifts under different weight choices.

### neural_network (0.0s)

## Neural Network

**Definition.** A *neural network* is a parameterized function $f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ composed of alternating linear maps and nonlinear activations. A feedforward network with $L$ layers is defined by the recurrence

$$
\mathbf{a}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{a}^{(\ell)} = \sigma\!\left(W^{(\ell)}\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\right), \quad \ell = 1, \dots, L,
$$

where $W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}}$ is the weight matrix, $\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$ is the bias vector, and $\sigma$ is a scalar activation applied elementwise. The output is $f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a}^{(L)}$, and the full parameter vector is $\theta = \{W^{(\ell)}, \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}$.

**Worked Example.** Consider a two-layer network with architecture $2 \to 3 \to 1$ and ReLU activation $\sigma(z) = \max(0, z)$. Let

$$
W^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \mathbf{0}, \quad W^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad b^{(2)} = 0.
$$

For input $\mathbf{x} = (2, -1)^\top$, the hidden pre-activation is $W^{(1)}\mathbf{x} = (2, -3, -1)^\top$. After ReLU: $\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = (2, 0, 0)^\top$. The output is $f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = W^{(2)}\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = 2$.

**Key Theorem (Universal Approximation).** Let $\sigma$ be any continuous, non-polynomial activation function. Then for every continuous function $g : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a single-hidden-layer network $f_\theta$ such that

$$
\sup_{\mathbf{x} \in [0,1]^n} |f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) - g(\mathbf{x})| < \varepsilon.
$$

The proof sketch proceeds by showing that finite linear combinations of shifted copies of $\sigma$ are dense in $C([0,1]^n)$ under the supremum norm; because $\sigma$ is non-polynomial, no finite combination can vanish identically, which supplies the necessary density. Crucially, the theorem guarantees existence but provides no bound on the required width or a practical learning algorithm.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

x1, x2 = sp.symbols('x1 x2')
x = sp.Matrix([x1, x2])

W1 = sp.Matrix([[1, 0], [-1, 1], [0, 1]])
b1 = sp.zeros(3, 1)
W2 = sp.Matrix([[1, 1, -1]])

relu = lambda v: v.applyfunc(lambda z: sp.Max(0, z))

a1 = relu(W1 * x + b1)
output = W2 * a1
sp.pprint(sp.simplify(output[0, 0]))
```

Running this cell with $\mathbf{x} = (2, -1)^\top$ recovers the scalar output $2$, confirming the worked example symbolically.

### perceptron (0.0s)

## Perceptron

**Definition.** A *perceptron* is a binary linear classifier defined by a weight vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, a bias term $b \in \mathbb{R}$, and a step activation function. Given an input vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the perceptron computes the output

$$\hat{y} = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & z \geq 0 \\ 0 & z < 0 \end{cases}$$

The scalar $z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$ is called the *net input* or *pre-activation*. Geometrically, the decision boundary is the hyperplane $\{\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = 0\}$, which partitions $\mathbb{R}^n$ into two half-spaces corresponding to the two classes.

**Worked Example.** Consider the logical AND function on two binary inputs. Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)^\top$ with labels $y = x_1 \land x_2$. Choose $\mathbf{w} = (1, 1)^\top$ and $b = -1.5$. Evaluating on all four inputs:

| $x_1$ | $x_2$ | $z = x_1 + x_2 - 1.5$ | $\hat{y}$ | $y$ |
|--------|--------|------------------------|-----------|-----|
| 0 | 0 | $-1.5$ | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 0 | $-0.5$ | 0 | 0 |
| 0 | 1 | $-0.5$ | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | $0.5$ | 1 | 1 |

All four outputs match the target, confirming that AND is linearly separable and this perceptron implements it exactly.

**Perceptron Convergence Theorem.** If a training set $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{m}$ with $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ is *linearly separable* — that is, there exists $\mathbf{w}^*$ and $b^*$ such that all examples are correctly classified with margin $\gamma > 0$ — then the perceptron learning rule

$$\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + (y_i - \hat{y}_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i, \quad b \leftarrow b + (y_i - \hat{y}_i)$$

converges in at most $\lfloor R^2 / \gamma^2 \rfloor$ updates, where $R = \max_i \|\mathbf{x}_i\|$. Crucially, convergence is not guaranteed when the data are not linearly separable; the weights cycle indefinitely.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np

# Weights and bias for AND gate
w = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
b = -1.5

X = np.array([[0,0],[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]])
for x in X:
    z = w @ x + b
    y_hat = 1 if z >= 0 else 0
    print(f"x={x}, z={z:.1f}, y_hat={y_hat}")
```

Running this cell reproduces the table above and invites the student to modify $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ to implement OR and NAND, reinforcing the geometric intuition of rotating and translating the decision boundary.

### depth (0.0s)

## Depth

**Definition.** Let $T = (V, E)$ be a rooted tree with root $r \in V$. The *depth* of a node $v \in V$, denoted $\text{depth}(v)$, is the number of edges on the unique path from $r$ to $v$. Equivalently, if $P(r, v) = (r = v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_k = v)$ is the path from the root to $v$, then

$$\text{depth}(v) = k = |P(r, v)| - 1.$$

By convention, the root satisfies $\text{depth}(r) = 0$. The *depth of the tree* $T$, sometimes called its *height*, is

$$\text{depth}(T) = \max_{v \in V} \text{depth}(v).$$

**Worked Example.** Consider the rooted tree below, with root $r = A$:

$$A \to \{B, C\}, \quad B \to \{D, E\}, \quad C \to \{F\}.$$

The path from $A$ to $D$ is $A \to B \to D$, which contains two edges, so $\text{depth}(D) = 2$. Similarly, $\text{depth}(B) = \text{depth}(C) = 1$, $\text{depth}(E) = \text{depth}(F) = 2$, and $\text{depth}(A) = 0$. The depth of the tree is therefore $\max\{0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2\} = 2$.

**Key Theorem (Depth and Level Sets).** For a rooted tree $T$ with $n$ nodes, let $L_k = \{v \in V : \text{depth}(v) = k\}$ denote the *level set* at depth $k$. Then the level sets partition $V$:

$$V = \bigsqcup_{k=0}^{\text{depth}(T)} L_k, \qquad |L_0| = 1.$$

For a *complete $m$-ary tree* — in which every internal node has exactly $m$ children — the level sets satisfy $|L_k| = m^k$, and the total node count is $n = \sum_{k=0}^{h} m^k = \frac{m^{h+1} - 1}{m - 1}$, where $h = \text{depth}(T)$. This geometric growth is the foundation for the $O(\log n)$ search complexity of balanced trees.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, summation, factor

m, k, h = symbols('m k h', positive=True, integer=True)

# Total nodes in a complete m-ary tree of depth h
total = summation(m**k, (k, 0, h))
print("Total nodes:", factor(total))

# Verify for binary tree (m=2), height 3
print("Binary tree h=3:", total.subs([(m, 2), (h, 3)]))
```

Running this cell confirms $n = 15$ for a complete binary tree of depth $3$, consistent with the formula $\frac{2^4 - 1}{2 - 1} = 15$.

## Multilayer Perceptron

**Definition.** A *multilayer perceptron* (MLP) is a feedforward artificial neural network composed of an input layer, one or more *hidden* layers, and an output layer, where every neuron in a given layer is connected to every neuron in the adjacent layer (a *fully connected* or *dense* architecture). Formally, let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_0}$ denote the input. The output of hidden layer $\ell$ is the vector

$$\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = \tanh\!\left(W^{(\ell)}\,\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\right), \quad \ell = 1, \dots, L,$$

where $W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell \times n_{\ell-1}}$ is the weight matrix, $\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}$ is the bias vector, $\tanh$ is applied element-wise as the *activation function*, and $\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}$. The final prediction is $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{h}^{(L)}$, possibly passed through a softmax for classification tasks.

**Worked Example.** Consider a two-layer MLP with $n_0 = 2$, $n_1 = 3$, $n_2 = 1$, and $\sigma = \tanh$. Given

$$W^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & 1\\1 & 1\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \mathbf{0},\quad W^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1 & 1\end{pmatrix},\quad b^{(2)} = 0,$$

and input $\mathbf{x} = (1, -1)^\top$, the hidden activations are $\mathbf{h}^{(1)} = \tanh\!\bigl((1,\,-1,\,0)^\top\bigr) \approx (0.762,\,-0.762,\,0)^\top$, and the output is $\hat{y} = (0.762) + (0.762) + 0 = 1.524$.

**Universal Approximation Theorem.** For any continuous function $f : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a single-hidden-layer MLP with a sigmoidal activation (such as $\tanh$) and finitely many neurons that approximates $f$ to within $\varepsilon$ in the $L^\infty$ norm. This result, due to Cybenko (1989) and Hornik (1991), guarantees expressive power but makes no statement about learnability or the number of neurons required.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
import sympy as sp

x1, x2 = sp.symbols('x1 x2')
W1 = sp.Matrix([[1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]])
b1 = sp.zeros(3, 1)
x = sp.Matrix([x1, x2])

h1 = W1 * x + b1
h1_act = h1.applyfunc(sp.tanh)

W2 = sp.Matrix([[1, -1, 1]])
b2 = sp.zeros(1, 1)
y_hat = W2 * h1_act + b2

print("Output:", y_hat.subs({x1: 1, x2: -1}).evalf())
```

### artificial_intelligence (0.0s)

## Artificial Intelligence

**Definition.** *Artificial intelligence* (AI) is the formal study of computational agents that perceive an environment through sensors, maintain an internal state representation, and select actions according to a policy that maximises a performance measure over time. Formally, an agent is a function $\pi : \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{A}$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space and $\mathcal{A}$ is the action space. The agent's goal is to find an optimal policy

$$\pi^* = \arg\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t \, r_t \;\middle|\; \pi\right],$$

where $r_t$ is the reward received at time step $t$ and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is a discount factor that weights immediate rewards more heavily than distant ones.

**Worked Example.** Consider a chess-playing agent. The state $s \in \mathcal{S}$ is the full board configuration (approximately $10^{44}$ legal positions), and $\mathcal{A}(s)$ is the set of legal moves from $s$. A minimax agent assigns a heuristic value $v(s)$ to each leaf node of a search tree of depth $d$, then propagates values upward by alternating

$$v_{\text{max}}(s) = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} v(s'), \qquad v_{\text{min}}(s) = \min_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} v(s'),$$

where $s'$ is the successor state after action $a$. Alpha–beta pruning removes subtrees that cannot affect the root value, reducing worst-case node evaluations from $O(b^d)$ to $O(b^{d/2})$, where $b$ is the branching factor.

**Key Theorem (No Free Lunch, Wolpert & Macready 1997).** For any two algorithms $A_1$ and $A_2$, summed over all possible discrete objective functions $f$,

$$\sum_f P(\vec{y}_m \mid f, m, A_1) = \sum_f P(\vec{y}_m \mid f, m, A_2),$$

where $\vec{y}_m$ is the sequence of cost values observed over $m$ evaluations. Consequently, no single AI algorithm outperforms every other across all problem classes; domain knowledge is always necessary to select an appropriate method.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, summation, oo, Rational

gamma, r, t = symbols('gamma r t', positive=True)
# Expected discounted return for constant reward r
G = summation(gamma**t * r, (t, 0, oo))
print("Discounted return (|γ|<1):", G)   # r / (1 - gamma)
```

Running this cell confirms the closed form $G = \dfrac{r}{1 - \gamma}$, the theoretical maximum return an agent can accumulate from a stationary reward stream, providing a benchmark against which learned policies are evaluated.

## Deep Learning

**Definition.** A *deep neural network* is a parametric function $f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{n_0} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_L}$ obtained by composing $L$ affine transformations interleaved with nonlinear activation functions:

$$f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma_L(W_L \cdots \sigma_1(W_1 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) \cdots + \mathbf{b}_L),$$

where $W_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell \times n_{\ell-1}}$ is the weight matrix of layer $\ell$, $\mathbf{b}_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}$ is the bias vector, and $\sigma_\ell$ is an element-wise activation function. A common choice is the rectified linear unit, $\sigma(z) = \max(0, z)$. Training minimises an empirical loss $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \ell(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i)$ over a labelled dataset $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ by iteratively updating $\theta$ in the direction of the negative gradient of $\mathcal{L}$.

**Worked Example.** Consider a two-layer network ($L = 2$) for binary classification with $n_0 = 2$, $n_1 = 3$, $n_2 = 1$, and rectified linear activations. Given input $\mathbf{x} = (1, -1)^\top$, weight matrix $W_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & 1\\1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{b}_1 = \mathbf{0}$, the hidden representation is

$$\mathbf{h} = \sigma_1(W_1\mathbf{x}) = \max\!\left(0,\,(1,\,-1,\,0)^\top\right) = (1,\,0,\,0)^\top.$$

With $W_2 = (2,\,-1,\,3)$ and $b_2 = 0$, the output is $f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = 2$, predicting the positive class after thresholding at zero.

**Key Theorem (Universal Approximation).** Let $\sigma$ be any continuous, non-polynomial activation function. For every continuous function $g : [0,1]^d \to \mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a single-hidden-layer network $f_\theta$ such that $\sup_{\mathbf{x}} |f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) - g(\mathbf{x})| < \varepsilon$. The theorem guarantees *existence* of an approximator but gives no bound on the required width; in practice, depth dramatically reduces the width needed for structured functions.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

x1, x2, w11, w12 = sp.symbols('x1 x2 w11 w12', real=True)

# Single neuron with rectified linear activation
z = w11*x1 + w12*x2
relu = sp.Piecewise((z, z > 0), (sp.Integer(0), True))

# Evaluate at the worked-example point
val = relu.subs({x1: 1, x2: -1, w11: 1, w12: 0})
print("Neuron output:", val)

# Evaluate at a point where the pre-activation is negative
val2 = relu.subs({x1: -2, x2: 1, w11: 1, w12: 0})
print("Neuron output (negative pre-activation):", val2)
```

Running this cell confirms the piecewise behaviour of the activation: the neuron transmits its weighted input unchanged when $z > 0$, and returns zero otherwise. This asymmetry is what breaks the symmetry of purely linear composition and endows deep networks with their representational power.

## Natural Language Processing

**Definition.** Natural language processing (NLP) is the subfield of computer science and linguistics concerned with enabling computational systems to analyze, understand, and generate human language. Formally, let $\Sigma^*$ denote the set of all finite strings over a vocabulary $\Sigma$. An NLP system defines a mapping $f: \Sigma^* \to \mathcal{Y}$, where the output space $\mathcal{Y}$ depends on the task — sentiment labels, parse trees, translated sentences, or probability distributions over continuations. The central challenge arises because natural language is ambiguous, context-dependent, and governed by implicit statistical regularities rather than strict formal grammars.

**Worked Example.** Consider sentiment classification. Given the sentence $s = \texttt{"The proof was not trivial, yet elegant"}$, a bag-of-words model represents $s$ as a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\Sigma|}$ where $x_i$ counts occurrences of the $i$-th word. A logistic classifier then outputs

$$\hat{y} = \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b\right), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}},$$

predicting positive sentiment when $\hat{y} > 0.5$. The word "not" and "elegant" receive learned weights $w_{\text{not}} < 0$ and $w_{\text{elegant}} > 0$; their net contribution determines the output. This simple model ignores word order, which motivates richer representations.

**Key Theorem (Zipf's Law).** For a large natural language corpus, if words are ranked by frequency $r = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, then the frequency $f(r)$ of the $r$-th most common word satisfies

$$f(r) \propto \frac{1}{r^{\alpha}}, \qquad \alpha \approx 1.$$

This power-law distribution implies that a small core vocabulary accounts for the majority of tokens, while a long tail of rare words dominates the lexicon by count. Consequence: fixed-size embedding tables truncated at rank $K$ capture most corpus mass, but rare technical or proper-noun terms require special handling (subword tokenization, byte-pair encoding).

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Function, summation, oo, S
import collections, re

text = "to be or not to be that is the question"
tokens = text.split()
freq = collections.Counter(tokens)
ranked = sorted(freq.values(), reverse=True)

# Verify Zipf: plot rank * frequency (should be ~constant)
zipf_products = [(r + 1) * f for r, f in enumerate(ranked)]
print("rank × freq:", zipf_products)
```

Run this cell on any large text file to observe how the product $r \cdot f(r)$ stabilizes — empirical confirmation of Zipf's Law in fewer than ten lines.

### vector (0.0s)

## Vector

**Definition.** A *vector* in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an ordered $n$-tuple of real numbers $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$, which encodes both a *magnitude* and a *direction*. Formally, $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a vector space under componentwise addition and scalar multiplication:

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (u_1 + v_1,\, u_2 + v_2,\, \dots,\, u_n + v_n), \qquad c\mathbf{v} = (cv_1, cv_2, \dots, cv_n).$$

The *Euclidean norm* (length) of $\mathbf{v}$ is $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2}$. A vector of norm 1 is called a *unit vector*; dividing any nonzero vector by its norm yields the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{v}/\|\mathbf{v}\|$ pointing in the same direction.

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{u} = (3, -4)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (1, 2)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

- *Sum:* $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (4, -2)$.
- *Scalar multiple:* $2\mathbf{u} = (6, -8)$.
- *Norm of $\mathbf{u}$:* $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.
- *Unit vector:* $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \left(\tfrac{3}{5}, -\tfrac{4}{5}\right)$, satisfying $\|\hat{\mathbf{u}}\| = 1$.

**Key theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz inequality).** For any $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$,

$$|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|,$$

where the *dot product* is $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i$. Equality holds if and only if $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are linearly dependent (one is a scalar multiple of the other). A direct consequence is the angle formula $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}$, which gives a coordinate-free measure of the angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ between the two vectors.

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, sqrt, Rational

u = Matrix([3, -4])
v = Matrix([1,  2])

print("u + v       =", u + v)
print("2u          =", 2 * u)
print("||u||       =", sqrt(u.dot(u)))
print("unit u      =", u / sqrt(u.dot(u)))
print("u · v       =", u.dot(v))
print("cos(theta)  =", u.dot(v) / (sqrt(u.dot(u)) * sqrt(v.dot(v))))
```

Running this cell confirms $\|\mathbf{u}\| = 5$, $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = (3/5,\,-4/5)$, and $\cos\theta = -11/(5\sqrt{5})$, corresponding to an obtuse angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

### word_embedding (0.0s)

## Word Embedding

**Definition.** A *word embedding* is a function $\phi: \mathcal{V} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ that maps each word in a finite vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ to a dense vector in $d$-dimensional Euclidean space, where $d \ll |\mathcal{V}|$. The embedding is learned so that geometric relationships in $\mathbb{R}^d$ reflect semantic or syntactic relationships among words. Formally, if $w_i, w_j \in \mathcal{V}$ are semantically similar, we expect $\|\phi(w_i) - \phi(w_j)\|_2$ to be small. This contrasts with one-hot encoding, in which every word is represented by a standard basis vector $e_i \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}$; under one-hot encoding, all inter-word distances are equal and no semantic structure is encoded.

**Worked Example.** Consider a toy vocabulary $\mathcal{V} = \{\text{king}, \text{queen}, \text{man}, \text{woman}\}$ and suppose a learned embedding in $\mathbb{R}^2$ yields

$$\phi(\text{king}) = \begin{pmatrix}0.9\\0.1\end{pmatrix},\quad \phi(\text{queen}) = \begin{pmatrix}0.9\\0.9\end{pmatrix},\quad \phi(\text{man}) = \begin{pmatrix}0.1\\0.1\end{pmatrix},\quad \phi(\text{woman}) = \begin{pmatrix}0.1\\0.9\end{pmatrix}.$$

Computing the analogy vector $\phi(\text{king}) - \phi(\text{man}) + \phi(\text{woman}) = (0.9, 0.9)^\top = \phi(\text{queen})$ recovers the gender analogy $\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} \approx \text{queen}$ exactly. Real embeddings exhibit this structure approximately, not exactly, but the principle generalises to thousands of dimensions and millions of words.

**Key Theorem (Distributional Hypothesis, Firth 1957; formalised by Levy & Goldberg 2014).** The skip-gram model with negative sampling (Word2Vec) implicitly factorises the shifted pointwise mutual information matrix $M_{ij} = \mathrm{PMI}(w_i, c_j) - \log k$, where $c_j$ ranges over context words and $k$ is the number of negative samples. Consequently, the optimal embedding vectors satisfy $\phi(w_i)^\top \phi(c_j) \approx \mathrm{PMI}(w_i, c_j) - \log k$, grounding the geometric intuition in an information-theoretic objective.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy import Matrix, sqrt, Rational

# Toy embedding matrix: rows are word vectors
E = Matrix([
    [Rational(9,10), Rational(1,10)],   # king
    [Rational(9,10), Rational(9,10)],   # queen
    [Rational(1,10), Rational(1,10)],   # man
    [Rational(1,10), Rational(9,10)],   # woman
])

# Cosine similarity between king and queen
v1, v2 = E.row(0), E.row(1)
cos_sim = v1.dot(v2) / (sqrt(v1.dot(v1)) * sqrt(v2.dot(v2)))
print("cos(king, queen) =", cos_sim)   # close to 1

# Analogy: king - man + woman
analogy = E.row(0) - E.row(2) + E.row(3)
print("king - man + woman =", analogy) # should equal queen
```

Running this cell confirms $\cos(\phi(\text{king}), \phi(\text{queen})) = 1$ in this idealised example, and that the analogy arithmetic closes exactly. In practice, embeddings trained on large corpora achieve cosine similarities above $0.7$ for such canonical analogies.

### transformer_model (0.0s)

## Transformer Model

**Definition.** A *transformer model* is a sequence-to-sequence neural architecture that computes output representations entirely through attention mechanisms and position-wise feed-forward layers, without any recurrence or convolution. Given an input sequence of token embeddings $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{model}}}$, the model produces contextualized representations by stacking $L$ identical encoder blocks, each consisting of a multi-head self-attention sublayer followed by a feed-forward sublayer, with residual connections and layer normalization applied after each.

The central computation is *scaled dot-product attention*. For query matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}$, key matrix $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}$, and value matrix $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v}$, the attention output is

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.$$

The factor $\sqrt{d_k}$ prevents the dot products from growing large in magnitude, which would push the softmax into regions of near-zero gradient. Multi-head attention projects $Q$, $K$, $V$ into $h$ independent subspaces, computes attention in each, then concatenates and projects the results:

$$\text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\ldots,\text{head}_h)\,W^O, \quad \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V).$$

**Worked example.** Let $n = 2$, $d_k = 2$, and suppose

$$Q = K = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \quad V = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.$$

The raw score matrix is $QK^\top / \sqrt{2} = I/\sqrt{2}$. After softmax row-wise, each row becomes approximately $(0.731, 0.269)$ (the diagonal entry wins). The output is a convex combination of value rows, so token 1 attends primarily to itself — a sensible identity-like behavior confirming that the mechanism recovers local focus when no cross-token signal is present.

**Key theorem (Universal approximation via attention).** Any continuous sequence-to-sequence function on a compact domain can be approximated to arbitrary precision by a sufficiently deep transformer with ReLU feed-forward sublayers. This result, established by Yun et al. (2020), parallels the classical universal approximation theorem for MLPs but applies to the sequence-function setting.

**Lab cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

d_k = 2
Q = sp.Matrix([[1, 0], [0, 1]])
K = Q; V = Q
scores = Q * K.T / sp.sqrt(d_k)

def softmax_row(M):
    rows = []
    for i in range(M.rows):
        row = M.row(i)
        exps = [sp.exp(x) for x in row]
        s = sum(exps)
        rows.append([e / s for e in exps])
    return sp.Matrix(rows)

A = softmax_row(scores)
output = A * V
sp.pprint(output.applyfunc(lambda x: sp.nsimplify(x, rational=False)))
```

## Large Language Model

**Definition.** A *large language model* (LLM) is a parametric probability distribution $p_\theta$ over sequences of tokens drawn from a finite vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. Given a context sequence $x_{1:t} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_t)$ with each $x_i \in \mathcal{V}$, the model factorizes the joint distribution autoregressively:

$$p_\theta(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).$$

Each conditional factor is computed by a deep neural network — in contemporary practice, the Transformer architecture — whose $|\theta|$ parameters number in the billions. Training minimizes the negative log-likelihood (equivalently, cross-entropy loss) over a corpus $\mathcal{D}$:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{(x_{1:T}) \in \mathcal{D}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).$$

The term *large* is deliberately relative: it marks the empirical threshold above which models exhibit qualitative emergent capabilities absent in smaller counterparts.

**Worked Example.** Suppose $\mathcal{V} = \{\texttt{cat}, \texttt{sat}, \texttt{on}, \texttt{mat}\}$ and the context is $x_{1:2} = (\texttt{cat}, \texttt{sat})$. An LLM assigns a probability vector over $\mathcal{V}$ for position $t = 3$:

$$p_\theta(\cdot \mid \texttt{cat}, \texttt{sat}) = [0.02,\ 0.03,\ 0.90,\ 0.05].$$

Generation by *greedy decoding* selects $x_3 = \arg\max p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{1:2}) = \texttt{on}$. Sampling instead draws $x_3 \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{1:2})$, introducing stochasticity controlled by a temperature parameter $\tau$ that rescales logits as $z_i \leftarrow z_i / \tau$ before the softmax.

**Key Theorem (Universal Approximation for Sequences).** For any distribution $q$ over $\mathcal{V}^*$ with finite entropy and any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a Transformer $p_\theta$ with sufficiently many layers and heads such that $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p_\theta) < \varepsilon$. This result underpins the empirical scaling laws observed by Kaplan et al. (2020), where test loss decreases as a power law in $|\theta|$, dataset size, and compute.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

# Softmax at temperature tau
tau = sp.Symbol('tau', positive=True)
z = sp.Matrix([2, 1, 4, 1])          # raw logits
z_scaled = z / tau
exp_z = z_scaled.applyfunc(sp.exp)
probs = exp_z / exp_z.dot(sp.ones(4, 1))

# Evaluate at tau=1
p_numeric = probs.subs(tau, 1).applyfunc(sp.N)
print("Softmax probabilities:", p_numeric.T)
```

Running this cell at $\tau = 1$ recovers the normalized probability vector; reducing $\tau \to 0$ sharpens the distribution toward a one-hot argmax, while $\tau \to \infty$ flattens it toward uniform — illustrating the temperature–diversity trade-off central to LLM decoding strategies.
