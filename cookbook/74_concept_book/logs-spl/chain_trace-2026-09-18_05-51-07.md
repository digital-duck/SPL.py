## Receptor

A receptor is a protein — typically membrane-embedded, though some (steroid, thyroid hormone) are cytosolic or nuclear — that binds a specific ligand (a hormone, neurotransmitter, drug, or other signaling molecule) with defined affinity and stereoselectivity, and converts that binding event into a change in cellular behavior. The receptor is the molecular interface between pharmacology (what a drug does to a binding site) and physiology (what happens to the organism as a consequence). Selectivity — the receptor's ability to distinguish structurally related ligands — is what makes pharmacology possible at all: without it, no drug could act on one process without disturbing every other process that shares similar chemistry.

**Mechanism.** Ligand binding occurs at a pocket shaped by the receptor's tertiary structure, stabilized by hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals contacts complementary to the ligand's geometry and charge distribution. Binding affinity is described by the dissociation constant $K_d$, the ligand concentration at which half the receptor population is occupied at equilibrium; this is a standard pharmacology-course quantity, not a derived result, and follows from the law of mass action applied to a simple bimolecular binding equilibrium $R + L \rightleftharpoons RL$. Occupancy alone does not guarantee a biological response — efficacy, distinct from affinity, describes how effectively a bound ligand triggers the downstream conformational change. This distinction separates agonists (trigger the active conformation), antagonists (occupy the site without triggering it, blocking agonists competitively), and partial agonists (trigger a submaximal response). The major receptor superfamilies — G-protein-coupled receptors (seven-transmembrane, coupled to intracellular G-proteins and second messengers such as cAMP or IP3), ligand-gated ion channels (binding directly opens a channel pore, e.g. nicotinic acetylcholine receptor), receptor tyrosine kinases (dimerize and autophosphorylate upon binding, e.g. insulin receptor), and nuclear/intracellular receptors (bind lipophilic ligands and act directly as transcription factors) — each couple the same binding-and-transduction logic to a structurally distinct downstream machinery.

**Literature context.** Structural biology (X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM) has resolved active and inactive conformations for most major GPCR and RTK families, clarifying how binding propagates conformational change across the membrane. What remains open and actively investigated: allosteric modulation (ligands binding sites other than the orthosteric pocket to tune receptor behavior without directly activating it), biased agonism (a single receptor activating different downstream pathways depending on which ligand or modulator is bound — of major current interest in opioid and GPCR drug design because it may allow separating therapeutic from adverse effects), and receptor reserve/spare receptors, where a submaximal fraction of receptor occupancy already produces a maximal tissue response, complicating naive interpretation of $K_d$ as a predictor of clinical dose-response.

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable project: obtain or simulate dose-response data for a receptor-ligand pair (e.g. public radioligand-binding or functional assay datasets for a GPCR), fit occupancy and response curves to extract $K_d$ and $EC_{50}$, and compare the two. A gap between them is direct evidence of receptor reserve or of a nonlinear transduction step between occupancy and response — worth investigating computationally (e.g. via PDB structures and docking tools such as AutoDock) or, where lab access permits, via a competitive-binding assay comparing a candidate ligand against a labeled reference compound.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should distinguish affinity from efficacy explicitly, report $K_d$ and $EC_{50}$ (or equivalent) with confidence intervals rather than point estimates, and state plainly whether the data support a simple occupancy model or require invoking reserve, cooperativity, or biased signaling — and if so, what structural or functional evidence supports that inference rather than merely fits the curve.
