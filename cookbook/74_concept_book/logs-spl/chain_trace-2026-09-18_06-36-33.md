## 一

You already know 一 (yī) as "one" — the simplest possible stroke, a single horizontal line. But 一 is also doing quiet structural work across the writing system, and once you see it, you'll spot it everywhere.

First, as radical 1 (Kangxi radical), 一 is the ancestor of a whole family of characters. It's not just "the character that means one" — it's a *classifying unit* that dictionaries use to group characters by shared form or meaning. Characters like 二 (two) and 三 (three) build directly on it by stacking strokes, showing you a rare case where a number's *shape* literally encodes its *value*: one line, two lines, three lines.

Second, 一 shows up as an intensifier or totality marker, not just a count. In 一生 (yīshēng, "one's whole life") or 一切 (yīqiè, "everything"), 一 pushes toward *entireness* — "all of it," "the whole thing" — rather than the number 1 you'd use in arithmetic. This is a pattern worth noticing: a character's core meaning (unity, oneness) can stretch into related meanings (wholeness, totality) depending on context.

Third, in spoken math or when reading digits aloud one at a time (like a phone number or ID), 一 is sometimes pronounced *yāo* instead of *yī* — this avoids confusion with 七 (qī, "seven"), which sounds similar in noisy conditions.

1. 一个苹果 (yī gè píngguǒ)
2. 一生 (yīshēng)
3. 一年 (yī nián)

### 丿 (0.0s)

## 丿

You already know 丿 as one of the very first strokes you learn to draw — a slanted line falling from upper right to lower left, like a diagonal slash going the "wrong" way compared to English handwriting. Now let's look at what that stroke actually does inside the writing system.

丿 is Kangxi radical 4, and it's also a character on its own, pronounced *piě* (撇). As a standalone character it can mean "to fling" or "to skim past" — fitting, since the stroke itself feels like a quick, decisive flick of the brush. As a radical, though, its job is structural: it shows up as a component piece inside many characters, contributing shape rather than meaning. Compare it to 乀 (a rightward-falling stroke, called *nà* 捺) — together, 丿 and 乀 form the basic X-shaped balance you see in a character like 人 (person), where the two strokes lean against each other like a stick figure's legs.

Think of strokes as the "atoms" of a Chinese character, and radicals as atoms that are also indexed for dictionary lookup. Every character has exactly one designated radical used for classification, even if it's made of many strokes. 丿 being radical 4 means it's simple enough to stand near the front of stroke-count-based dictionary ordering (radicals are traditionally listed by increasing stroke count, 1 through 214).

**Pattern to remember:** direction and angle matter in stroke names — 丿 falls left, 乀 falls right, and combining directional strokes is how complex characters get their geometric balance.

**Practice:** Look at the character 八 (meaning "eight"). Identify which stroke is 丿 and which is 乀.

### 木 (0.0s)

## 木

Picture a tree: a trunk with branches spreading out at the top and roots below. That's the shape behind 木 (mù), a pictograph — a character drawn to look like the thing it means. The horizontal stroke is the branches, the vertical stroke is the trunk, and the two slanting strokes at the bottom are the roots digging in.

But 木 does more than sit alone meaning "tree" or "wood." It's a building block — a radical — and it plays two very different jobs depending on where it lands in a character.

**Job 1: lending meaning.** When 木 shows up as a meaning-radical, it tells you the character is connected to trees, wood, or plants in some way. Look at 林 (lín), "forest" or "woods":

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{林}_{\text{forest}}$$

Two trees standing together, literally becoming a forest. Or take 休 (xiū), "to rest":

$$\underbrace{亻}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{休}_{\text{rest}}$$

A person leaning against a tree — resting in its shade.

**Job 2: lending sound.** In other characters, 木 isn't about meaning at all — it just contributes a sound. In 沐 (mù), "to bathe" or "wash," the water radical (氵) carries the meaning, while 木 simply tells you how to pronounce it: mù, the same sound as the tree itself.

So one brick, two roles: sometimes 木 paints a picture of meaning, sometimes it hums a note of sound. Learning to tell which job it's doing is a real skill of reading Chinese.

**Practice:** Look at 相 (xiāng) — it contains 木. Can you find another radical inside it? Guess whether 木 is likely working for meaning or sound here, and explain your reasoning in one sentence.

### 朱 (0.0s)

## 朱

You already know 朱 (zhū) as vermilion, a bright reddish-orange that once colored royal seals and lacquerware. But look at its structure and you'll find it's not built the way most color characters are. Compare it to 红 (red), which pairs 纟 (silk) with a sound component 工 — a meaning-plus-sound formula. 朱 doesn't work that way at all.

朱 is built on 木 (tree), with one small extra stroke added near the middle of the trunk — a mark pointing to the tree's core. Historically, that mark singled out the reddish heartwood found in certain trees, like cinnabar-stained wood or the vermilion-rich core of some species. So 朱 isn't "silk colored red" — it's "tree, with attention drawn to its red center." The color meaning grew directly out of a physical thing being pointed at, not out of a phonetic borrowing.

This gives you a simple rule for reading marked characters: when 木 gains one small extra stroke, ask what part of the tree that stroke is highlighting — often it marks a specific, meaningful part rather than changing the whole word's category. The position of the mark matters: near the base of 木 gives 本 (root), near the top gives 末 (tip), and near the middle gives 朱 (core/heartwood, later "vermilion"). Same base character, same kind of pointer stroke, but the *location* of the mark tells you which part of the tree is meant.

**Practice**: Using the rule above — mark near the base = 本, mark near the middle = 朱, mark near the top = 末 — draw a rough sketch of a tree trunk and mark where each character's stroke would go. Then write one sentence explaining why the mark's height, not its shape, is what carries the meaning.
