### 木 (7.9s)

## 木

Picture a tree standing alone in a field: a straight trunk, with branches reaching out to the sides. That's exactly what 木 draws — one line straight down for the trunk, and two strokes spreading out for the branches. Simple, right? It looks like what it means.

But here's the surprising part: 木 doesn't just draw pictures of trees. It also carries a *sound*, like a nickname that sticks to other characters. When two trees stand together, 林, you get a forest — the meaning of 木 doubling up to build a bigger idea. When someone leans against a tree, 休, they're resting — 木 again lending its meaning of "wood, something solid to lean on." But in 沐, meaning "to bathe," 木 isn't about trees at all — it's just borrowed for its *sound*, "mu," while water 氵 carries the meaning.

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = 林 \text{ (forest)}$$

**Now you try:** Can you think of one word in English that changes meaning depending on which "job" it's doing — like "duck" the bird versus "duck" the action?

### 女 (7.8s)

## 女

Imagine you are asked to draw "a person" using only three quick strokes, kneeling gently with arms crossed in front. That's exactly what an ancient scribe did thousands of years ago to capture the idea of a woman — a simple kneeling figure, arms folded softly at her lap, like someone pausing quietly.

Why does this feel right? Because early writers didn't invent random shapes — they sketched what they saw. A kneeling posture was a common, respectful way of sitting in ancient times, so it became the picture for "woman."

This character doesn't need to be broken into pieces — it *is* the piece. That's what makes it special: 女 is a building block itself. Many characters about family, care, and relationships grow out of it, the way a single seed can grow into a whole garden of related words.

Now you try: Sit like the character — knees together, hands folded — and trace 女 in the air. Can you feel how the shape matches the pose?

### 人 (7.2s)

## 人

Imagine a person walking sideways — two legs, one arm swinging back, caught mid-step like a snapshot from an old photograph. That's exactly what the character 人 shows you: a simple stick-figure of a person walking, viewed from the side.

Why does this feel right? Because early writers didn't invent a random shape for "person" — they drew what they saw. A walking body has two legs that spread apart to balance, and 人 captures just that: one stroke leaning left, one leaning right, meeting at the top like two legs meeting at the hips.

This isn't just any character — it's one of the most important building blocks in the whole writing system. Many characters about people, actions, or roles borrow 人 as a piece, the way a builder reuses the same brick in many different walls.

Now you try: Stand up and take one big step. Look down at your legs. Do they look like 人? Try drawing it in the air with your finger, tracing the walking motion.

### 子 (6.2s)

## 子

Imagine a tiny bundle wrapped snugly in a blanket, only a round head peeking out. That's exactly what the character 子 pictures — a swaddled baby! Look closely and you can almost see it: a head on top, and two little arms wrapped down and tucked in below.

Why does this feel right? Because ancient writers loved to draw what they saw. A baby all bundled up looks small, round-headed, and still — and that's just how 子 looks on the page.

But 子 does more than mean "child." It also became a helpful little ending that turns other words into naming-words — the way English sometimes adds "-y" or "-let" (like "piglet"). Think of it as a small tag that says, "this is a thing you can point to."

$$
\underbrace{桌}_{table} + \underbrace{子}_{noun\ suffix} = 桌子\ (table)
$$

So 子 carries two jobs at once: sometimes it *is* the baby, and sometimes it just helps name things.

**Now you try:** Can you think of a word that changes meaning when you add a small ending to it, the way 子 does?

### 宀 (6.1s)

## 宀

Imagine you're building a tiny house out of blocks. First thing you place isn't a bed or a table — it's the roof, tilted like a little tent, covering the space below. That slanted shape is 宀. It doesn't stand alone very often; it's more like a promise: "something will live safely under here."

Think of 宀 as a shelter waiting to be filled. A roof by itself is just a shape, but the moment something sits beneath it — a pig, a woman, a piece of jade — that thing becomes *housed*, *settled*, *protected*. The roof changes the meaning of whatever it covers, the same way putting a toy under a blanket fort makes it feel safe and tucked away.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{豕}_{pig} = 家 \ (\text{home})
$$

Here, a pig under a roof becomes "home" — because in old farming life, keeping animals safely housed meant a family was settled.

**Now you try:** Next time you see a character with a little roof on top, ask: what's sheltered inside, and why does it need protecting?

### 马 (10.9s)

## 马

Picture a little horse galloping across a field — four legs, a mane, a tail. Long ago, someone drew that horse simply: a few strokes catching its legs and flowing mane. Over time the drawing got neater and more square, and it became the character 马.

Here is the surprising part: this horse doesn't always bring horses with it. Sometimes a character just wants the *sound* "ma," and 马 is happy to lend it out, keeping quiet about horses altogether.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{马}_{sound: ma} = 妈 \;(\text{mom})
$$

Notice the pattern: 女 (woman) plus the "ma" sound gives you the word for mom. 马 isn't galloping in here — it's just singing its sound, lending "ma" to a completely different meaning.

Now you try: Can you think of a person whose name sounds like a word for something totally unrelated, the way 马 sounds like "ma" without being a horse?

### 从 (5.3s)

## 从

Imagine you're walking to school, and your little cousin trails right behind you, matching your steps. That's the whole story of this character! Look at it: one person shape, then another person shape, one right after the other.

Why does this feel right? Because the character doesn't just *tell* you about following — it *shows* you. Two of the same little figure, walking in a line. The second person isn't doing anything fancy; they're just going where the first one goes. That's exactly what "follow" means.

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{a person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{another person}} = 从 \text{ (to follow)}$$

The first 人 leads the way, and the second 人 comes right after. No extra strokes, no decoration — just repetition, because following *is* repetition of someone else's path.

**Now you try:** Draw two stick figures in a row, one behind the other. Which one is "leading," and which one is 从-ing?

### 口 (7.3s)

## 口

Imagine drawing a little square — not sharp and boxy, but soft, like a mouth caught wide open mid-laugh, or mid-shout, or mid-bite. That square is 口, and it means "mouth."

Why does a square feel right for a mouth? Think about it: when you open your mouth wide — to yell "SURPRISE!" or take a giant bite of a sandwich — the shape your lips make is roundish, but ancient writers simplified it into something easy to draw quickly with a brush: four straight strokes forming a box. A mouth is an opening, a doorway in your face, and a square is just a simple, sturdy way to draw a doorway.

Here's the fun part: 口 doesn't just mean the mouth itself — it also shows up whenever something involves the mouth's jobs. Eating happens with 口. Speaking happens with 口. Even a question mark in Chinese punctuation borrows this shape, because questions come out of mouths too!

**Now you try:** Open your mouth and make its shape with your fingers. Does it look like a little square door?

### 日 (7.4s)

## 日

Long ago, someone looked up at the sun and wanted to draw it. The sun is round — so they started with a circle. But early writing was carved or painted with stiff brushes and knives, and circles are hard to make cleanly that way. So the circle got squared off at the edges, and a small mark was added inside to show it wasn't just any circle — it was glowing, alive with light.

That's the secret of 日: it's not really a picture of the sun anymore, it's a *reminder* of one. Once you know the story, the square shape with a line inside stops looking like a box and starts looking like a little window of daylight.

This character is special because it's a building block. Many words about brightness, time, and weather grow out of it — 日 is the seed, and other pieces get added to grow new meanings, like:

$$
\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} = \underbrace{明}_{\text{bright}}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw a circle, then gently square its corners, then add one short line inside. Does it start to look like sunlight trapped in a frame?

### 月 (7.6s)

## 月

Look up at the night sky when the moon is thin, like a fingernail clipping — that curved sliver is exactly the shape of this character. 月 was drawn to *be* a crescent moon, curved edge and all.

But here's a puzzle: the same shape shows up inside words about the body — like arm, or belly, or brain. Why would "moon" hide inside "leg"?

It doesn't, really. Long ago, there was a *different* symbol that meant "flesh" or "body part," drawn as a rounded shape with two little lines inside, almost like a slice of meat. Over centuries of being written faster and faster, that flesh-symbol and the moon-symbol drifted until they looked identical. So today, one shape, 月, secretly carries two family histories — sometimes it's the sky's moon, sometimes it's a body's flesh, and only the rest of the character tells you which ancestor is speaking.

$$
\underbrace{\text{crescent moon}}_{\text{sky ancestor}} \;\approx\; \underbrace{\text{flesh}}_{\text{body ancestor}} \;\rightarrow\; 月
$$

**Now you try:** Next time you see 月 in a character, ask — is this one about the sky, or about the body?

### 林 (5.6s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing all alone in a field. Now imagine a second tree right next to it. Suddenly it doesn't feel like "two trees" anymore — it feels like the beginning of something bigger, something shadier and cooler, a little clump of green.

That's the trick behind 林. Chinese doesn't always invent a brand-new picture for every new idea. Sometimes it just repeats a picture it already has! One 木 means a single tree. Put a second 木 right beside it, and the character doesn't mean "two trees" — it means *woods*, a small stand of trees growing together.

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \text{林 (woods)}$$

This is the simplest building rule in the whole writing system: repeat a piece, and you multiply its feeling. One tree is lonely. Two trees together feel like a little forest already forming.

**Now you try:** Draw one tree. Then draw a second tree touching it. Doesn't it already look like the edge of a woods?

### 水 (6.0s)

## 水

Picture a mountain stream, twisting and splashing over rocks, tiny drops flying off to the sides. That's exactly what the character 水 draws: a curving line for the flowing stream in the middle, with little strokes branching off like splashes.

Why does this feel right? Because water never moves in straight lines — it winds, it splits around obstacles, it sprays. The character captures that restless, branching motion instead of showing a still puddle.

Here's the clever part: when 水 wants to team up with another character to describe something wet, it changes shape and becomes 氵 — three simple drops lined up on the left side. Think of it as water "getting dressed" in a smaller outfit so it can stand beside its partner character.

$$
\underbrace{水}_{\text{water, standing alone}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{氵}_{\text{three drops, left-side helper}}
$$

Now you try: Next time you see a character with three little drops on its left edge, guess what it might mean — river, ocean, rain, tears? Water is hiding right there in the shape.

### 休 (5.9s)

## 休

Imagine you have walked a very long way. Your legs feel heavy, your eyes want to close, and you see a big tree with cool shade under it. What do you do? You lean right against the trunk and stop moving. Ahhh — that feeling of stopping to catch your breath is exactly what this character shows.

Look at the shape: on the left is a little person standing tall, 人. On the right is a tree, 木. Push them together, shoulder to trunk, and you get 休 — a person resting against a tree.

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \underbrace{休}_{rest}$$

That's the whole idea! No need to imagine anything extra — the picture already tells the story. A tired person, a shady tree, a moment of peace.

**Now you try:** Draw a stick figure leaning against a tree trunk. Can you see 人 in your person and 木 in your tree? Now write 休 right next to your drawing — see how the character matches your picture!

### 众 (5.7s)

## 众

Imagine one person standing alone — quiet, just one voice. Now imagine two more people walk up and stand beside them. Suddenly it's not quiet anymore. That's a crowd!

You already know 人 means "a person" — it even looks like two legs standing. When Chinese writers wanted to show *lots* of people, they didn't invent a brand-new symbol. They just wrote 人 three times, stacked together, like a little group huddling up.

This is the same trick you saw with 森 — one tree becomes a forest by tripling. Here, one person becomes a crowd, a multitude, "everyone," by the very same rule.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = \underbrace{众}_{\text{crowd}}
$$

Three is the magic number in Chinese writing for "many" — not exactly three, just "more than you can count on one hand."

**Now you try:** Can you think of another word where three of something means "a whole lot" — like three cheers, or three's a crowd?

### 吗 (6.5s)

## 吗

Imagine you say, "You like ice cream." That's just a fact. Now say it again, but at the very end, add a tiny hum — like a little question mark you can hear: "You like ice cream... ma?" Suddenly it's not a fact anymore. It's a question!

That little hum is 吗 (ma). You don't need to flip the words around or add "do" or "does" like in English. In Chinese, you just say your sentence normally, then tack 吗 onto the end, and — like magic — it becomes a question.

Look at what 吗 is made of:

$$\underbrace{口}_{\text{mouth, speaking}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sounds like "ma"}} = \text{吗 (question word "ma")}$$

The mouth radical tells you this character is about sound and speech, and 马 lends its pronunciation. Notice 吗 shares its sound with 妈 (mom) — same "ma," totally different meaning, all decided by the mouth part next to it!

**Now you try**: Take the sentence "You are happy" and add 吗 to the end. What do you get?

### 好 (5.5s)

## 好

Imagine a mother sitting with her child close beside her. That's it — that's the whole picture. Three thousand years ago, someone looking for a way to write "good" didn't reach for a rule or a measurement. They reached for this image: a woman, a child, together.

Why does this feel right? Because before anyone can explain what goodness *is*, they already know what it *looks like* — a home where someone is cared for. The character doesn't argue for goodness. It just shows you a scene, and your heart says "yes, that's good" before your mind finishes thinking.

The character is built from two pieces standing side by side:

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 好 \ (\text{good})$$

Neither piece alone means "good." Only together, watching over each other, do they become it.

**Now you try:** Next time you see 好, picture the two people standing together — and notice how the meaning arrives before any explanation does.

### 妈 (7.0s)

## 妈

Imagine you're building a word like a little machine, snapping two pieces together. One piece means "a female person" — that's 女. The other piece doesn't mean anything here except "make this sound like *ma*" — that's 马, which usually means horse, but not today! It's just lending its sound, like a friend lending you their voice.

Put them together, and you get 妈 — mā, "mother." No horses hiding inside this word at all. The left piece tells you *who* (a woman), and the right piece tells you *how to say it* (ma). This is a trick Chinese characters use all the time: one part for meaning, one part for sound.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{female person}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sounds like "ma"}} = \text{妈 (mother)}$$

**Now you try:** Cover up 马 and just look at 女. Can you think of another word you know that also uses 女 for "woman"? What sound-part might get attached to build it?

### 字 (5.3s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny child tucked safely inside a house, warm and cared for, learning and growing every day. That is exactly the picture hidden inside the character 字 (zì), which means "character" or "written word."

Look closely and you can see two smaller pictures stacked together. On top is 宀, a simple roof shape — think of it like the outline of a house. Underneath sits 子, a picture of a small child. A child under a roof: that's a home where something new is being raised.

Why does this feel right? Because that is exactly what a new Chinese character is — a "child" born from older, simpler pieces, and "raised" inside the shelter of the writing system until it becomes its own word.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = 字 \ (character)$$

Now you try: think of your own name. What simpler "pieces" — sounds, letters, or family names — was it built from?

### 安 (5.8s)

## 安

Picture a little girl coming home when it's getting dark outside. The wind is picking up, and the sky is turning gray. But then she steps through her front door, and — ahh — everything feels okay again. There's a roof over her head. She's safe.

That feeling — the deep, cozy relief of being somewhere safe — is exactly what the character 安 captures. Look at its shape: on top is 宀, a little roof, like the top of a house. Underneath is 女, a woman. A woman sitting calmly under a roof. Home. Shelter. Nothing to fear.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = \underbrace{安}_{\text{peace, safe}}
$$

That's why 安 shows up in words like 平安 (safe and sound) and 安全 (safety) — it always carries that same warm feeling of being protected.

**Now you try:** Next time you feel safe and cozy at home, picture the little roof over the calm woman. That's 安 — peace.

### 明 (6.3s)

## 明

Imagine you're in your room at night. You turn on the sun-shaped lamp — that's a little bit of light. Now you also turn on the moon-shaped nightlight — that's a little more light. Together, the room feels bright, glowing, easy to see in.

That's the idea behind 明 (míng), meaning bright or clear. The character puts two light-givers side by side: the sun, 日, and the moon, 月. Not one or the other — both together. The sun lights up the day, the moon lights up the night, and when you place them next to each other, you get the very idea of brightness itself, light so complete it covers all the hours.

$$
\underbrace{日}_{sun} + \underbrace{月}_{moon} = \underbrace{明}_{bright}
$$

It's a simple picture: two glowing shapes, standing together, making something brighter than either alone.

**Now you try**: Draw a sun and a moon next to each other. What word would you use to describe the light they make together — bright, clear, glowing? That feeling is exactly what 明 means.

### 森 (5.8s)

## 森

Imagine one tree standing alone on a hill. Now imagine two trees standing together — that's already starting to feel crowded, like a little grove. But what happens when you put three trees together? You don't just get "more trees" — you get something new: a whole forest, thick and shadowy, where sunlight has to fight its way down through the leaves.

That's the secret of 森 (sēn). One tree is 木. Two trees is 林, a small woods. Three trees is 森, a full forest. Each time you add another tree-brick, the meaning grows denser and wilder — the character is literally counting itself into a bigger idea.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \text{ (forest)}
$$

Now you try: draw three trees close together. Does your drawing start to feel like a forest instead of just "some trees"? That crowded, shadowy feeling is exactly what 森 is trying to show you.

### 沐 (6.0s)

## 沐

Imagine you just came in from playing in the mud. Your mom says, "Go take a 沐 (mù)!" You know exactly what she means — go wash up! But have you ever wondered why this character sounds like "mu," the same sound as the word for "tree" (木)?

Here's a secret: not every character gets its sound from a picture. Sometimes, a character borrows the *sound* of another character it doesn't even look like in meaning. In 沐, the water radical 氵 tells you what it's about — washing, water, cleaning. But the 木 part isn't a tree at all! It's just there to remind you how to say the word: "mu."

$$\underbrace{氵}_{\text{water — MEANING}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{"mu" — SOUND}} = 沐 \text{ (to wash)}$$

**Now you try:** Cover up the 氵 in 沐. What's left is 木, "tree." Now say "mù" out loud — hear how close it sounds to "mù" for tree? That borrowed sound is the clue!

### phonetic_borrowing (10.4s)

## Phonetic Borrowing

Imagine you already own a brick shaped like a tree — 木, which means "wood." One day you need to build a word that means "to bathe," and it just so happens that "bathe" sounds like "mù," the same sound as "wood." So instead of inventing a brand-new brick, you borrow 木 just for its *sound*, add a water radical to show what the word is really about, and — done! You've built 沐.

That's the trick behind phonetic borrowing: a piece stops being hired for its meaning and gets hired for its sound instead, like an actor voicing a totally different character. Any sound-brick can be reused this way in character after character, even when the new words have nothing to do with its original meaning.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{\text{sound: mù}} + \underbrace{氵}_{\text{meaning: water}} = 沐 \text{ (bathe)}
$$

Now you try: 木 also shows up in 沫 ("foam") for the exact same reason — can you guess why?

### semantic_composition (12.2s)

## Semantic Composition

Imagine you could draw an idea just by putting two pictures side by side. A person leaning against a tree — that's rest. This is how ancient Chinese writers built new meanings: not with sound, but by stacking pictures until a new idea popped out.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{休}_{\text{rest}}
$$

Why does this feel right? Picture someone tired from walking, finally leaning their back against a tree trunk. You don't need anyone to tell you what that means — the picture already whispers it.

Once you see this trick, you start noticing it everywhere. The sun and the moon together, shining at once, mean bright — the brightest thing you can imagine. A woman under a roof means peace. A mother beside her child means good. Ancient writers weren't just drawing objects; they were drawing little scenes, and letting the scene itself carry the meaning, the way a comic panel can say more than a caption ever could.

**Now you try:** Can you think of two simple pictures that, combined, might mean "happy" or "warm"? Sketch them and see what idea appears!

### phono_semantic_principle (7.0s)

## Phono Semantic Principle

Imagine you're building with LEGO, but instead of one giant custom brick for every single toy, you use just two kinds of pieces combined in new ways: one piece that hints at *what kind of thing* it is, and another that hints at *how it sounds*. That's the secret behind most Chinese characters — over 80% of them, in fact!

Each character is built like a tiny recipe: take one meaning-piece (it tells you the category — water, tree, heart, hand) and one sound-piece (it tells you roughly how to say the word), and glue them together.

$$\underbrace{氵}_{\text{water meaning}} + \underbrace{青}_{\text{sound: qīng}} = 清 \text{ (qīng, "clear")}$$

Notice how the water radical whispers "this is about liquid," while 青 tells your mouth what sound to make. Once you know about 200 of these building bricks, you can guess new characters you've never seen — just like reading a new LEGO set from familiar pieces.

**Now you try:** If 氵 (water) + 每 (měi) makes 海 (hǎi, "sea"), what do you think 氵+ 工 (gōng) might sound like?

### reading_unseen_characters (5.8s)

## Reading Unseen Characters

Imagine finding a locked door with no key — but the doorknob is shaped like a tiny picture clue. That's what happens when you meet a Chinese character you've never seen before. You are not stuck. You are a detective.

Every character is built from smaller, familiar pieces — bricks you already know. One brick often hints at the *meaning* (what family the word belongs to), and another hints at the *sound* (what it might rhyme with). Put the clues together, and you can make a smart guess before ever opening a dictionary.

Take a character built from a "water" brick and a sound brick that reads *qīng*:

$$
\underbrace{水}_{\text{water}} + \underbrace{青}_{\text{sound: qīng}} = 清
$$

The water brick whispers "this is probably about liquid or clearness." The sound brick whispers "probably rhymes with qīng." Together: *clear* — and it does mean clear!

**Now you try:** Find a character with a "grass" brick plus a mystery sound brick. Guess its meaning family before you look it up. Were you close?
