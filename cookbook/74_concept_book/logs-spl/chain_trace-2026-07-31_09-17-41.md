### data_privacy (23.1s)

## Data Privacy

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a dataset over a universe $\mathcal{X}^n$, and let $\mathcal{M}: \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathcal{Y}$ be a randomized mechanism. The mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-*differential privacy* (DP) if, for every pair of adjacent datasets $D, D' \in \mathcal{X}^n$ differing in exactly one record and every measurable event $S \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$:

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \leq e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta.$$

The pure case $\delta = 0$ is denoted $\varepsilon$-DP. The parameter $\varepsilon$ quantifies privacy loss; smaller $\varepsilon$ implies stronger protection at the cost of greater noise injection.

**Theorem (Gaussian Mechanism).** Let $f: \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathbb{R}^k$ have $\ell_2$-sensitivity $\Delta_2 f = \sup_{D \sim D'} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_2$. The mechanism $\mathcal{M}(D) = f(D) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_k)$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP for $\sigma \geq \frac{\Delta_2 f \sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}$.

*Proof sketch.* The privacy loss random variable $\mathcal{L} = \ln \frac{p_D(y)}{p_{D'}(y)}$ is Gaussian with mean $\|\Delta\|_2^2 / \sigma^2$ and variance $4\|\Delta\|_2^2 / \sigma^2$; bounding $\Pr[\mathcal{L} > \varepsilon]$ via Mills' ratio yields the $\delta$ expression. $\square$

**Literature context.** The foundational DP framework of Dwork et al. (2006) has been extended substantially: Rényi DP (Mironov, 2017) tightens composition bounds via moment accountants; zero-concentrated DP (Bun & Steinke, 2016) unifies several variants. Open questions include the optimal privacy-utility trade-off under local DP, and whether DP guarantees survive distribution shift at deployment.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement the Gaussian mechanism for private mean estimation on a real tabular dataset (e.g., UCI Adult). Vary $\varepsilon \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ and $\delta = 10^{-5}$; track empirical $\ell_2$ error against the noise-free estimator. Compute the theoretical error bound from the Gaussian mechanism calibration and compare to observed error, checking whether the bound is tight.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should formally state the adjacency relation used, derive the noise scale from first principles, present the empirical privacy-utility curve with error bars across multiple trials, and discuss the gap between theoretical upper bounds and observed utility loss — noting any regime where the bound becomes vacuous.

### ethics_in_data_science (16.7s)

## Ethics In Data Science

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ be a dataset drawn from joint distribution $P(X, Y)$ over feature space $\mathcal{X}$ and label space $\mathcal{Y}$, and let $h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ be a learned predictor. Ethical data science demands that $h$ satisfy normative constraints — fairness, privacy, transparency — alongside predictive accuracy $\mathbb{E}[\ell(h(X), Y)]$, where $\ell$ is a task loss.

**Theorem (Impossibility; Chouldechova 2017).** For a binary classifier with base rates $p_A \neq p_B$ across groups $A, B$, it is impossible to simultaneously satisfy calibration within groups, equal false positive rates, and equal false negative rates. That is, no $h$ can achieve all three constraints unless $p_A = p_B$.

*Proof sketch.* Express each constraint as a linear equation in the confusion-matrix entries conditional on group membership. Under unequal base rates the system is overdetermined; a standard counting argument yields a contradiction. $\square$

This result formalizes a tension that practitioners encounter empirically: optimizing for one fairness criterion degrades another. The literature distinguishes individual fairness (Dwork et al. 2012), group fairness (demographic parity, equalized odds), and causal fairness via do-calculus (Kusner et al. 2017). Privacy adds a further constraint: $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy bounds information leakage but provably degrades accuracy in proportion to dataset sensitivity (Dwork & Roth 2014). Transparency requirements (explainability) remain theoretically under-specified relative to fairness and privacy.

**Investigation.** Replicate the impossibility proof on the COMPAS recidivism dataset: compute calibration curves and error-rate parity simultaneously; quantify the trade-off surface as base-rate disparity $|p_A - p_B|$ varies via subsampling.

**Write-up.** A complete report should state which fairness criteria were targeted and why, derive the constraint equations for your classifier family, report the empirical trade-off curve with confidence intervals, and discuss which criterion aligns with the decision-maker's normative commitments and relevant regulatory guidance (e.g., EU AI Act Article 10).

### personally_identifiable_information (21.1s)

## Personally Identifiable Information

**Definition.** Personally Identifiable Information (PII) is any data element, or combination of data elements, from which a specific natural person can be identified with non-negligible probability. Formally, let $\mathcal{D}$ be a dataset over attribute space $\mathcal{A} = A_1 \times \cdots \times A_k$, and let $\mathcal{P}$ denote a population of individuals. A tuple $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{D}$ is PII with respect to an adversary with background knowledge $\mathcal{B}$ if there exists a function $f_{\mathcal{B}} : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{P}$ such that $\Pr[f_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}) = p^*] > \epsilon$ for some threshold $\epsilon$ and true subject $p^*$. The boundary between PII and non-PII is thus adversary-relative, not intrinsic to the data.

**Core result.** Narayanan and Shmatikoff (2008) demonstrated that the Netflix Prize dataset, released as "anonymized," permitted re-identification by linking sparse rating vectors against public IMDb records — a foundational attack formalizing that quasi-identifiers (attributes individually non-identifying) compose into effective identifiers. This motivates the $k$-anonymity condition (Sweeney, 2002): a release satisfies $k$-anonymity if every record is indistinguishable from at least $k-1$ others on the quasi-identifier set $Q \subseteq \mathcal{A}$. However, $k$-anonymity does not bound attribute disclosure, leading to the stronger $\ell$-diversity and $t$-closeness refinements.

**Literature context.** Differential privacy (Dwork et al., 2006) provides a composable, adversary-agnostic framework: a mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP if for all adjacent datasets $D, D'$ and output sets $S$, $\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^\varepsilon \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta$. Open questions persist around membership inference attacks on LLMs (Carlini et al., 2021, 2023), where training data extraction from neural weights constitutes a novel PII leakage vector not addressed by classical $k$-anonymity or even DP guarantees in practice.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the membership inference attack of Carlini et al. (2021) on a small language model fine-tuned on a synthetic corpus containing planted PII strings. Vary the memorization regime (sequence length, repetition count, learning rate) and measure extraction rate as a function of $\varepsilon$ under DP-SGD training. Characterize the privacy-utility tradeoff curve empirically.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should formally define the threat model (adversary capabilities, auxiliary knowledge $\mathcal{B}$), state the extraction success rate against a random-guess baseline, and quantify how DP noise magnitude shifts the tradeoff. Null results — failed extraction under sufficiently small $\varepsilon$ — are as reportable as positive extractions.

### regulatory_compliance (24.7s)

## Regulatory Compliance

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{S} = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F)$ be a finite automaton representing the state-transition semantics of a computational system, and let $\mathcal{R} = \{\varphi_1, \ldots, \varphi_k\}$ be a finite set of regulatory constraints expressed in linear temporal logic (LTL). The system $\mathcal{S}$ is *compliant* with $\mathcal{R}$ if and only if every execution trace $\pi \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{S})$ satisfies $\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k} \varphi_i$. In practice, $\mathcal{R}$ encodes requirements from instruments such as GDPR Article 22, the EU AI Act Annex III, or HIPAA §164.312, each translatable to safety or liveness properties over the system's observable behavior.

**Theorem (Compliance Verification Complexity).** Model-checking $\mathcal{S} \models \varphi$ for $\varphi \in$ LTL is PSPACE-complete in the size of $\varphi$ and $\mathcal{S}$ (Clarke et al., 1999). Consequently, exhaustive compliance verification is intractable for large state spaces, motivating abstraction-refinement and runtime monitoring as practical alternatives.

**Literature context.** The tension between expressiveness and decidability is central to regulatory engineering. Basin, Harvan & Klaedtke (2012) propose monitorable trace-based compliance. More recently, Governatori et al. (2023) examine normative systems over deontic logic, while Wachter, Mittelstadt & Russell (2017) raise the open problem of *counterfactual compliance*—whether a system that violates $\varphi$ can be repaired minimally. For LLM pipelines, compliance over stochastic traces remains largely unresolved.

**Suggested investigation.** Encode a representative regulatory clause (e.g., GDPR right-to-explanation) as an LTL formula $\varphi$, implement a runtime monitor over an LLM workflow execution log, and measure the false-positive rate under model non-determinism. Vary $|\mathcal{R}|$ and record verification time empirically against the PSPACE lower bound.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should formally state the monitored property, justify the LTL encoding against the natural-language clause, report precision/recall of the monitor, and discuss whether observed violations are attributable to specification ambiguity or genuine system non-compliance.

### hipaa (31.6s)

## Hipaa

**Definition.** The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA, 1996) establishes the legal and administrative framework governing the use and disclosure of *Protected Health Information* (PHI) by *covered entities* (health plans, clearinghouses, providers) and their *business associates*. PHI is defined as any individually identifiable health information transmitted or maintained in any medium. HIPAA's Privacy Rule (45 C.F.R. §164.514) specifies two de-identification standards: the *Safe Harbor* method, which requires removal of 18 enumerated identifiers, and the *Expert Determination* method, which demands that a statistical or scientific expert certify that residual re-identification risk is "very small."

**Formal framing.** Let $\mathcal{D}$ denote a dataset of $n$ records drawn from population $\mathcal{P}$, with quasi-identifier space $\mathcal{Q}$. Safe Harbor de-identification projects $\mathcal{D}$ onto a reduced attribute space $\mathcal{D}'$. The residual re-identification risk under adversarial background knowledge $\mathcal{B}$ can be bounded by $k$-anonymity: a dataset satisfies $k$-anonymity if every record shares its quasi-identifier tuple with at least $k-1$ others, so that $\Pr[\text{identify}(r \mid \mathcal{D}', \mathcal{B})] \leq 1/k$. Expert Determination implicitly targets $k \geq 1/\epsilon$ for some small $\epsilon$, though the threshold is unspecified in regulation. Differential privacy provides a stronger, composition-closed guarantee: a randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differentially private if for all adjacent datasets $\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{D}'$ and all outputs $S$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}) \in S] \leq e^{\epsilon} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}') \in S] + \delta.$$

**Literature context.** Sweeney (2002) demonstrated that 87% of Americans could be uniquely identified from $\{$ZIP, birth date, sex$\}$ alone, motivating the 18-identifier Safe Harbor list yet exposing its inadequacy against auxiliary data. Machanavajjhala et al. (2007) extended $k$-anonymity to $\ell$-diversity to counter attribute-inference attacks. The differential privacy framework (Dwork et al., 2006; Dwork & Roth, 2014) offers a rigorous alternative, and the Apple–Google exposure-notification protocols and the U.S. Census 2020 TopDown algorithm are landmark deployments. Open questions include: (i) calibrating $\epsilon$ to regulatory thresholds in a principled way, (ii) composition under repeated HIPAA disclosures across covered entities, and (iii) integrating generative models (LLMs trained on EHR corpora) under HIPAA constraints without formal DP guarantees.

**Suggested investigation.** Obtain a publicly available de-identified clinical dataset (e.g., MIMIC-III on PhysioNet, which itself requires a data-use agreement). Apply Safe Harbor de-identification, then measure empirical re-identification rate using a linkage attack against a demographic auxiliary table (e.g., U.S. Census microdata). Quantify the effective $k$ achieved and compare against a Laplace-mechanism DP release at $\epsilon \in \{0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$ on a summary statistic of clinical interest. Compute the privacy-utility tradeoff curve and identify the $\epsilon$ value at which DP matches Safe Harbor's empirical $k$.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up should state the threat model (honest-but-curious linker, bounded auxiliary knowledge), derive the $k$-anonymity achieved before and after the attack, report the $(\epsilon,\delta)$ parameters of the DP mechanism with justification for the noise calibration, and discuss whether the Expert Determination standard could be satisfied by the DP certificate. The report should conclude with a policy recommendation: does the chosen $\epsilon$ constitute "very small" risk under 45 C.F.R. §164.514(b)(1), and how should that threshold be formalized?
