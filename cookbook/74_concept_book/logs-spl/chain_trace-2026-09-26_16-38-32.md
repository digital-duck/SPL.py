### 一 (1.4s)

## 一

Hold up one finger. Just one. Before you can count two, three, or a hundred, you need this single idea: *oneness* — a single, whole, complete thing standing by itself.

The character 一 draws exactly what it means: one flat stroke. No curls, no extra parts — because "one" is the simplest idea there is. It's the starting point every other number grows from, the same way the number $1$ is the starting point of counting: $1, 2, 3, \dots$

But 一 means more than the number one. It also means *whole* or *entire* — like saying "the entire day" or "throughout the journey." Think of a single circle drawn without lifting your pencil: it's one continuous, undivided thing. That's the deeper feeling behind 一 — not just "a single item," but "one complete whole with nothing missing and nothing extra."

In Chinese writing, 一 is also a building block. It is the very first radical, numbered 1, meaning many characters that involve flatness, ground, or a single line start with this same stroke.

**Now you try:** Draw one straight horizontal line. That line *is* 一. Can you think of something in your day that comes as "one whole thing" — one sun, one you?

### 丨 (1.3s)

## 丨

Imagine a tally mark — the single line you scratch on paper to count "one." A farmer counting sheep going through a gate makes a mark for each one: 丨丨丨丨. In Chinese writing, this exact idea survives as its own character: 丨, meaning "line" or "vertical stroke." It shows up as a straight line dropping from top to bottom, like a plumb line hanging under gravity, or a flagpole standing straight up.

Why does a single line deserve to be its own character? Because in Chinese, meaning is built the way a shape is built — from a small set of basic strokes, the same way every letter you write is built from lines and curves. 丨 is one of the most fundamental strokes, and it becomes a building block inside many other characters, like 中 (middle) and 十 (ten), where a vertical line cuts through something to divide or mark it.

Now you try: draw a straight vertical line on paper, top to bottom, without lifting your pencil. Can you find that same line hiding inside another character you know?

### 十 (1.2s)

## 十

Hold up all ten fingers. That's it — that's the whole idea behind 十. When you count on your fingers and run out of them all at once, you've hit something special: a full group of ten, the size we use to organize every number after it.

Look at the character: a horizontal stroke 一 crossing a vertical stroke 丨, like two hands meeting, or a line marking a finished count. The horizontal line is "one" — a single mark. The vertical line cuts straight through it, as if to say: this isn't just one anymore, it's a whole new unit.

Here's the elegant part. Once you have ten, you don't need a brand-new symbol for eleven or twelve — you just say "ten-one" (十一), "ten-two" (十二). Ten becomes a building block, like a bag that holds exactly ten single things. This grouping-by-tens idea is the seed of place value, the system where where a digit sits changes what it's worth.

Now you try: count ten pebbles, put them in a cup. That cup is now one "ten." How many pebbles are two cups?

### 土 (1.2s)

## 土

Picture a farmer pushing a stick straight down into the ground, then laying another stick flat across the dirt at its base. That crossing point — where a mound of earth meets a marker — is exactly the shape of 土 (tǔ), meaning earth, soil, or clay.

Look closely and you'll see the character is built from two simpler pieces: 十 (the number ten, a cross shape) sitting on top of 一 (the number one, a flat line). The flat line is the ground; the cross above it is a small mound or plant pushing up through the soil. This is how many Chinese characters work — small, already-known pieces combine to build a new meaning, much like snapping together blocks you already recognize.

We can write this composition simply:
$$
土 = 十 + 一
$$

Here "+" means "stacked to form a new character," not arithmetic addition — the meaning comes from position, not sum.

Now you try: find one part of 土 in another character you know, and guess whether that part means "ground" there too.
