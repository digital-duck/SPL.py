## 丿

You already know 丿 as one of the very first strokes you learn to draw — a slanted line falling from upper right to lower left, like a diagonal slash going the "wrong" way compared to English handwriting. Now let's look at what that stroke actually does inside the writing system.

丿 is Kangxi radical 4, and it's also a character on its own, pronounced *piě* (撇). As a standalone character it can mean "to fling" or "to skim past" — fitting, since the stroke itself feels like a quick, decisive flick of the brush. As a radical, though, its job is structural: it shows up as a component piece inside many characters, contributing shape rather than meaning. Compare it to 乀 (a rightward-falling stroke, called *nà* 捺) — together, 丿 and 乀 form the basic X-shaped balance you see in a character like 人 (person), where the two strokes lean against each other like a stick figure's legs.

Think of strokes as the "atoms" of a Chinese character, and radicals as atoms that are also indexed for dictionary lookup. Every character has exactly one designated radical used for classification, even if it's made of many strokes. 丿 being radical 4 means it's simple enough to stand near the front of stroke-count-based dictionary ordering (radicals are traditionally listed by increasing stroke count, 1 through 214).

**Pattern to remember:** direction and angle matter in stroke names — 丿 falls left, 乀 falls right, and combining directional strokes is how complex characters get their geometric balance.

**Practice:** Look at the character 八 (meaning "eight"). Identify which stroke is 丿 and which is 乀.
