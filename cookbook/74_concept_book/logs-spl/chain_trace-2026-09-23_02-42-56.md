### normal_force (0.0s)

## Normal Force

When an object rests on a surface, the surface pushes back on it — not because the surface "wants" to, but because it resists being compressed. This reaction is the **normal force**, denoted $N$, and it always acts perpendicular ("normal" in the geometric sense) to the contact surface, pointing away from it and into the object. It is a contact force, arising only when two surfaces actually touch and press against each other.

The key insight is that $N$ is not a fixed, universal constant like gravity — its magnitude is determined case by case by requiring that the object not accelerate through the surface. This is Newton's third law in disguise: the object presses into the surface with some force, and the surface presses back with equal magnitude, opposite direction.

**Worked example.** A 5 kg box sits on a flat, horizontal table. Gravity pulls down on it with force $F_g = mg = (5\,\text{kg})(9.8\,\text{m/s}^2) = 49\,\text{N}$. Since the box has no vertical acceleration, Newton's second law in the vertical direction gives
$$
N - mg = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad N = mg = 49\,\text{N}.
$$
The surface must supply exactly enough force to cancel gravity — no more, no less.

**Problem-solving application.** The normal force changes whenever the situation changes the net vertical demand on the surface. Suppose the same box sits on the table, but someone pushes down on it with an additional 20 N. Vertical equilibrium now requires
$$
N - mg - 20\,\text{N} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad N = 49\,\text{N} + 20\,\text{N} = 69\,\text{N}.
$$

Now consider an inclined surface: the box rests on a ramp tilted at angle $\theta$ from horizontal. Only the component of gravity perpendicular to the ramp is balanced by $N$; the parallel component causes the box to slide (or is balanced separately by friction). Resolving gravity into components gives
$$
N = mg\cos\theta.
$$
Notice that as $\theta$ increases from $0°$ to $90°$, $N$ shrinks from $mg$ to zero — a vertical wall exerts no normal force on an object resting only under gravity, because there is no perpendicular component of weight left to balance.

The general problem-solving strategy is always the same: isolate the object with a free-body diagram, identify every force acting perpendicular to the contact surface, and apply Newton's second law along that perpendicular axis (often $\sum F = 0$ for objects that aren't accelerating in that direction) to solve for $N$. This same reasoning underlies friction calculations, since kinetic and static friction forces are both proportional to $N$, not to weight directly.

### friction (9.8s)

## Friction

Friction is the contact force acting parallel to the interface between two surfaces, always directed to oppose relative sliding or attempted sliding between them. It arises from microscopic irregularities and molecular bonding between surfaces in contact, and its magnitude depends on the normal force pressing the surfaces together. Because friction is a resistive force, it always points opposite to the direction of motion (kinetic friction) or opposite to the net applied force that would cause motion (static friction).

Two regimes matter. **Static friction** acts when surfaces are not sliding relative to each other; it adjusts in magnitude, up to a maximum, to exactly balance any applied force and keep the object at rest. **Kinetic friction** acts once sliding begins, and empirically has a nearly constant magnitude regardless of sliding speed. Both are modeled as proportional to the normal force $N$:

$$
f_s \leq \mu_s N, \qquad f_k = \mu_k N
$$

where $\mu_s$ and $\mu_k$ are the coefficients of static and kinetic friction, dimensionless constants determined experimentally for a given pair of surfaces, with $\mu_s$ typically slightly greater than $\mu_k$ — it takes more force to start an object moving than to keep it moving.

**Worked example**: A 10 kg crate sits on a floor with $\mu_s = 0.5$ and $\mu_k = 0.4$. The normal force is $N = mg = 10 \times 9.8 = 98\ \text{N}$. The maximum static friction is $f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N = 49\ \text{N}$. If you push with 40 N, the crate stays put, and static friction exactly matches your push at 40 N (not its maximum). Once your push exceeds 49 N, the crate starts sliding, and kinetic friction drops to $f_k = \mu_k N = 39.2\ \text{N}$ — noticeably less than what was needed to start the motion.

**Problem-solving application**: To find whether an object moves, compare the applied force to $f_{s,\max}$; if it moves, use $f_k$ in Newton's second law, $F_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{applied}} - f_k = ma$, to solve for acceleration. On an incline of angle $\theta$, replace $N = mg\cos\theta$ and compare gravity's component along the slope, $mg\sin\theta$, to $\mu_s N$ to determine whether the object slides at all — a technique used throughout mechanics problems involving ramps, pulleys, and braking distances.
