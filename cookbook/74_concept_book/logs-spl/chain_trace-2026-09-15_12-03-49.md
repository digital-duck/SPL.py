### 木 (7.2s)

## 木

Picture a single tree standing in a field — a straight trunk with branches reaching out on either side. Long ago, someone drew exactly that: a line down the middle for the trunk, and two strokes crossing it for the branches. That drawing became the character 木, meaning "tree" or "wood."

Here's what's clever: this little tree-brick doesn't just mean tree — it gets used in two completely different ways depending on the word it joins. Sometimes it brings its *meaning* along, like a worker bringing tools to a job. Other times it only brings its *sound*, like someone humming a tune without caring about the words.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree (meaning)}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree (meaning)}} = \text{林 (forest — two trees together)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree (meaning: rest)}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = \text{休 (a person leaning on a tree — to rest)}
$$

But in 沐 (to bathe), 木 isn't about trees at all — it's just lending its *sound*, "mu."

**Now you try:** Can you think of two friends who share a name but do totally different jobs?

### 女 (5.9s)

## 女

Long ago, someone looked at a person kneeling gently, hands folded, and turned that shape into a picture. That picture became the character 女, meaning "woman."

Look closely: the curved strokes are like arms crossed softly, the base like knees tucked under — a figure sitting in quiet grace. Ancient people didn't draw a face or hair. They drew a posture, a feeling, a way of being in the world.

Here's what makes 女 so special: it doesn't just mean "woman" by itself. It becomes a building block — a radical — that shows up inside many other characters about family and relationships. When you see 女 tucked inside a character, it's often a clue whispering, "this word has something to do with women, mothers, or family bonds."

For example:

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{好 (good)}
$$

A woman with a child — together, "good."

**Now you try:** Next time you spot 女 inside a new character, pause and ask: what family story might this character be telling?

### 人 (5.7s)

## 人

Stand up. Plant your feet apart, just a little. Lean forward slightly, like you're about to take a step. That shape — two legs, a bit of a lean — is exactly what someone drew thousands of years ago when they wanted to write "person."

Look at the character: 人. Two strokes, like two legs walking. No face, no arms, no details — just the idea of a person caught mid-step. That's the trick of these old picture-characters: they don't draw everything, they draw the one thing that makes you instantly think "oh, that's a person walking."

Because it captures something so basic — a person — this little shape shows up again and again inside other characters, almost like a spare part builders keep reaching for. When you see it tucked into a bigger character, it's often hinting that the meaning has something to do with people, standing, or walking.

**Now you try:** Stand like the character — legs apart, leaning forward. Can you feel why someone chose *this* shape, out of all possible shapes, to mean "person"?

### 子 (5.7s)

## 子

Picture a tiny baby, wrapped snugly in a blanket, arms tucked in, only a round head peeking out. That's exactly what the character 子 once looked like — a drawing of a swaddled infant, arms bundled at its sides.

Why does this feel right? Because babies really do look like little bundles! The top stroke is the head, and the curving lines below are the wrapped-up body. Ancient writers looked at a baby and simply drew what they saw.

But 子 grew beyond just "baby." Over time, it also became a helpful little ending that turns other words into nouns — the way English adds "-er" to "teach" to make "teacher." Attach 子 to many Chinese words and you get an everyday object: 桌子 (table), 椅子 (chair), 帽子 (hat).

$$
\underbrace{桌}_{table\ top} + \underbrace{子}_{noun\ suffix} = 桌子 \ (table)
$$

Now you try: Can you think of a round or bundled thing that might have inspired a picture-character somewhere in the world?

### 宀 (7.8s)

## 宀

Imagine a tiny house drawn with just a few strokes: a little dot on top like a chimney cap, and two slanted strokes reaching down like walls leaning together to keep the wind out. That's 宀 — not a whole house you'd live in by itself, but the *roof* over one. It never stands alone as a word. It's always sitting on top of something else, like a real roof always needs a room underneath it.

Think of 宀 as a promise of shelter. Whatever character sits beneath this roof gets protected, tucked in, made safe and settled. A pig under this roof becomes "home." A woman under this roof becomes "peace." The roof shape tells your eye: *something is being sheltered here* — before you even know what that something is.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{\text{something}}_{\text{sheltered thing}} = \text{a settled, housed idea}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw the little roof — dot, then two slanted strokes. Ask yourself: if you put a person under this roof, what feeling might that new character carry?

### 马 (10.7s)

## 马

Imagine a word detective who doesn't care about meaning at all — only about sound. That's 马. On its own, it draws a galloping horse, all mane and legs. But once it climbs onto other characters, something strange happens: it forgets what a horse looks like and becomes pure sound, the syllable "ma."

Why does this feel right? Think of it like a costume. The horse-shape puts on a "ma" name-tag and lends that sound to any character that needs it — the horse-*meaning* stays home.

Watch it work:

$$
\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: ma}} = 妈 \;(\text{mother})
$$

Mother has nothing to do with horses — 马 just donated its sound, while 女 (woman) told us this word is about a person.

**Now you try:** Cover the equation above. Can you draw it from memory, remembering that 马 is only lending its *voice*, not its *horse*?

### 从 (6.1s)

## 从

Picture two children walking to school. One is in front, and one walks right behind, matching every step. That second child is *following* — and that is exactly the idea behind 从 (cóng).

Look at the character itself: it is simply one person shape, 人, placed behind another person shape, 人. No extra strokes, no decoration — just a small figure trailing a bigger one. The picture *is* the meaning. When ancient writers wanted to show "follow," they didn't invent a symbol out of nothing; they drew the scene: person, then person again.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{a person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{another person, behind}} = \text{从 (to follow)}
$$

That's why 从 feels so natural once you see it — it's not a code to memorize, it's a tiny drawing of a line of people.

**Now you try:** Draw two stick figures, one behind the other, walking the same direction. Underneath, write 从. Can you think of a time *you* followed someone — a parent, a friend, a leader in a line?

### 口 (6.2s)

## 口

Imagine drawing a little square — just four lines meeting at the corners. What could it be? A window? A box? Look again, and see: it's a mouth, wide open, waiting to speak or take a bite.

That square shape *is* the mouth. Nothing hides inside it, no smaller pieces stacked together — it's simply what a mouth looks like when you trace its outline with the fewest strokes possible. The ancient writers who first drew this symbol were sketching what they saw: lips forming an open circle, simplified into a shape easy to carve or brush.

But this small square does more than sit still. It speaks — carrying words out into the world. It eats — taking food in. And when placed at the end of a sentence, it can turn a plain statement into a question, like a voice rising at the end, asking "really?"

One shape, three jobs: opening, speaking, wondering.

**Now you try:** Draw the square. Say a word out loud as you finish the last stroke — feel your own mouth open just like the shape you drew.

### 日 (6.5s)

## 日

Long ago, someone looked up at the sky and saw the sun: a round, glowing circle. They wanted to draw it, but a perfect circle is hard to carve neatly with a brush. So they squared it off — a box shape with a little line in the middle, like a single glowing eye.

Why does this feel right? Because a picture doesn't have to be exact to be true. The square box still remembers the round sun. The middle stroke is like the warmth or light glowing right inside it. It's the sun, simplified just enough for a brush to draw quickly, again and again.

This character isn't just "sun" — it's the *source* of brightness itself. Many words about light, day, and time grow out of it, like sunlight branching from one bright seed.

$$
\underbrace{\square}_{\text{a square frame}} + \underbrace{\text{—}}_{\text{glow inside}} = 日 \text{ (sun)}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw a circle for the sun, then draw 日 next to it. Which lines did the circle keep? Which did it lose?

### 月 (7.7s)

## 月

Look up at the night sky. You see a bright curved sliver of light — a crescent moon. Someone long ago looked at that same curve and drew it as a shape with two strokes inside, like ribs. That drawing became 月.

Here is the surprising part: that same shape shows up inside words about your body — like parts of your arm, your stomach, your face. Why would the "moon" symbol hide inside "body" words?

It's not really the moon there — it's a lookalike! Long ago, the picture for "flesh" (soft body tissue) looked almost the same as the moon picture. Over time, both pictures flattened into the identical shape 月. So today, 月 lives a double life: sometimes it means the moon in the sky, and sometimes it's a quiet clue that a character is about a body part.

$$
\underbrace{月}_{\text{moon (sky)}} \;=\; \underbrace{月}_{\text{flesh (body clue)}}
$$

One shape, two ancestors, two jobs.

**Now you try:** Next time you see 月 inside a character, ask: is this word about the sky, or about a body? Let the rest of the character be your clue!

### 林 (5.5s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing alone in a field. Now imagine a second tree right next to it. Just two trees — but already something new appears: a little patch of shade, a place where leaves rustle together. That's the beginning of woods.

The character 林 (lín) is built the simplest way possible: take the character for tree, 木, and plant it twice, side by side.

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林$$

One tree is just a tree. Two trees, standing together, become something bigger than either one alone — a small woods. This is the easiest building rule in Chinese characters: repeat a piece, and you get "more of it" or "a group of it." No new pieces needed, no tricky rules — just doubling.

**Now you try:** Draw 木 twice on a piece of paper, side by side, close together. That's 林! Can you think of other things that feel different in twos — like two friends becoming a "pair," or two hands clapping together?

### 水 (6.9s)

## 水

Imagine you are standing by a little stream, watching the water twist and turn as it flows over rocks. Look closely — a splash to the left, a splash to the right, and a little curl in the middle where the current bends. That is exactly what the character 水 is drawing for you!

The middle stroke is the stream itself, flowing down and curving like real water does. The strokes on either side are the splashes jumping off the sides. Once you see it that way, you will never forget it — 水 does not just mean water, it *looks* like water moving.

Here is something even more useful: when water shows up inside other characters, it often shrinks down into a simple little shape — three drops, 氵 — placed on the left side. So any character with 氵 on its left is probably about something wet, like rivers, oceans, or rain.

Now you try: think of three drops falling — splash, splash, splash. Can you draw 水, and then imagine shrinking it into 氵 to place beside another character?

### 休 (5.0s)

## 休

Imagine you've been walking all day under a hot sun. Your legs are tired, your feet ache — and then you spot it: a big, shady tree. You walk over and lean your back right against its trunk. Ahhh. That feeling of relief is exactly what this character captures.

Look at the shape: on the left there's a person 人, standing tall. On the right there's a tree 木, with branches reaching up. Put them side by side, and you can almost *see* someone leaning against a trunk to catch their breath. The picture doesn't just show a person and a tree near each other — it shows them snapping together into one single idea: resting.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{休}_{\text{rest}}
$$

Now you try: think of a time you rested against something — a wall, a pillow, a friend's shoulder. Could you draw *your* rest the way 休 draws its?

### 众 (5.5s)

## 众

Imagine one person standing alone on a big empty field. Now imagine two more people walk up and stand beside them. Suddenly it doesn't feel empty anymore — it feels like a crowd!

You already know that 人 means "a person." And you've seen that stacking a shape three times can mean "lots of it" — like 森 does with trees. Well, guess what — the same trick works with people too!

Stack three 人 shapes together, and you get 众 (zhòng) — which means "crowd" or "many people." One person alone is quiet. Two is a pair. But three or more? Now you've got a gathering, a group, a crowd!

$$
\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = \underbrace{众}_{crowd}
$$

**Now you try:** Close your eyes and picture just one person. Then picture a crowded playground with tons of kids running around. That busy, full-of-people feeling — that's exactly what 众 is drawing for you!

### 吗 (10.6s)

## 吗

Imagine you ask your friend, "You like ice cream." That's just a statement. But what if you want to *ask* them — "Do you like ice cream?" In English, you flip the words around or add "do." In Chinese, there's an easier trick: just add one tiny word at the very end — 吗 (ma) — and the statement magically becomes a question!

Look closely at 吗: on the left is 口 (mouth), reminding you this is something *spoken*, a little verbal nudge. On the right is 马, which just lends its sound — "ma" — nothing to do with horses here!

$$\underbrace{口}_{\text{mouth, spoken}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: ma}} = 吗 \text{ (question word)}$$

That's the whole trick — one small piece added to the end turns any statement into a question.

**Now you try**: Take the statement "你是学生" (You are a student) and add 吗 at the end. What question did you just create?

### 好 (6.8s)

## 好

Imagine a home three thousand years ago. Someone wants to draw a picture that means "everything is right in the world." What do they draw? Not gold, not a feast — they draw a mother with her child.

That's the character 好 (hǎo), meaning good. It's built from two smaller pictures standing side by side: 女, a woman, and 子, a child. Put a caring grown-up next to a little one, and ancient writers said: that's what goodness looks like.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 好 \;(\text{good})$$

Think about why this feels true. A house with someone caring for someone else does feel good — safe, warm, complete. The character isn't describing gold stars or perfect grades; it's describing togetherness.

Now you try: look at 好 and cover the right half. Can you still see the woman on the left? Now cover the left half — there's the child. Two people, one meaning: good.

### 妈 (6.9s)

## 妈

Imagine calling out for your mom in a crowded playground. You shout "Mā! Mā!" and she turns around. That sound, "mā," is exactly what this character is built to say out loud.

Look at the two pieces inside it. On the left sits 女, the piece that means "a female person." On the right sits 马, but here it isn't doing its horse job at all — it's just lending its sound, "ma." So the whole character is like a little machine: one half tells you *who* (a woman), the other half tells you *how to say it* (ma). Put them together and you get "mother."

This is a common trick in Chinese characters — pairing a meaning clue with a sound clue.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{female person}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sounds like } ma} = 妈 \ (\text{mother})
$$

**Now you try:** Cover the 马 side. Can you still guess this character is about a woman? Now cover the 女 side — can you guess how to pronounce it?

### 字 (6.7s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny child, tucked safely under the roof of a house, being raised and taught. That is the picture inside the character 字 (zì) — and it means "character," the very thing you are reading right now!

Why does this feel right? Because every Chinese character is like a child of the writing system. It is "born" from older, simpler pieces, and it "grows up" to have its own meaning and life, just like a child grows up under a family's roof.

Here is how the picture is built:

$$\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = 字 \text{ (character)}$$

A roof over a child — a home where something new is raised. This is such a perfect idea that scholars even named the whole study of character-families after it: ZiNets, or "字 networks," meaning "character networks." So 字 does not just mean "character" — it names the entire family tree of Chinese writing!

**Now you try**: Can you think of a character you know that seems to have a "parent" piece inside it, helping raise its meaning?

### 安 (7.3s)

## 安

Imagine a rainstorm outside. Where do you want to be? Inside, under a roof, safe and warm. That feeling — nothing bad can reach me here — is exactly what this character captures.

Picture a roof stretched over the top of the character, and underneath it, a woman sitting quietly. Not running, not worried, just settled. A house with someone calm inside it is the oldest picture of safety people could draw: shelter above, a person at rest below. That's the whole idea packed into one small picture.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = \underbrace{安}_{\text{peace, safe, settled}}
$$

You already know this feeling. It's why 平安 (píng ān) means "safe and sound," and why 安全 (ān quán) means "safety" — both carry that same "under-the-roof, nothing-to-fear" feeling inside them.

**Now you try:** Next time you feel calm and protected — under a blanket, in your room, with family nearby — picture the roof over the woman. That's 安.

### 明 (5.2s)

## 明

Imagine it's nighttime, and you're a little scared of the dark. Then your mom turns on a lamp — and suddenly you can see everything again! Now imagine you had two lamps instead of one. Even brighter, right?

That's exactly the idea behind this character. Long ago, someone looked up at the sky and noticed something wonderful: the sun gives light during the day, and the moon gives light at night. Two glowing friends, taking turns lighting up the world. So they drew them side by side — sun next to moon — to create a single character meaning "bright."

$$
\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} = \underbrace{明}_{\text{bright}}
$$

It's like saying: put all the light-givers together, and you get pure brightness — a room, a mind, or an idea that is clear and shining.

**Now you try:** Can you think of two things that, together, make something even brighter or clearer than either one alone — like two candles, or two good ideas?

### 森 (8.5s)

## 森

Imagine one tree standing alone — just 木. Now imagine two trees close together — that's 林, a small woods. But what if you kept walking and the trees never stopped? Not two, not five — trees in every direction, thick and deep. That's 森, a forest.

Notice what's happening: the character isn't describing a forest with a picture of a forest. It's stacking the same tree three times. One tree, two trees, three trees — the character grows exactly the way the meaning grows.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} \xrightarrow{+木} \underbrace{森}_{forest}
$$

This is the beautiful trick of these characters: quantity becomes meaning. A little more of something (two trees) becomes a woods. A lot more (three trees) becomes a whole forest. The brick count itself tells the story — you don't need a new symbol, just more of the same one.

**Now you try:** If one 木 is a tree and 林 is a small woods, what do you think a character made of *four* trees stacked together might mean? Draw it and give it your own meaning!

### 沐 (6.6s)

## 沐

Imagine you just finished playing outside, covered in dust, and you jump into a warm bath. That good, clean feeling is 沐 (mù) — "to wash" or "to bathe."

Here's something sneaky about this character: the left piece, 水 (water), tells you the *meaning* — obviously, washing involves water! But the right piece, 木 (tree), isn't a tree at all this time. It's only there to lend its *sound*, "mu." So 木 is just borrowing its voice, not its picture.

This is a new trick! Earlier characters used pieces for their pictures. But 沐 uses one piece for meaning and one piece for sound — like a puzzle with two different jobs.

$$\underbrace{水}_{\text{meaning: water}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{sound: ``mu''}} = \text{沐 (mù, ``to wash'')}$$

**Now you try:** Cover the 木 side and say "water." Now say "mu" out loud. Put them together — water + mu = wash!

### phonetic_borrowing (12.4s)

## Phonetic Borrowing

Imagine you already know how to draw a tree: 木. One day someone asks you to write the word for "wash your hair." You don't need a brand-new picture — you just borrow the sound of 木 (pronounced *mù*) and add three little water drops beside it: 氵. Now you have 沐, which sounds almost like *mù* but means washing, not trees. The tree-brick didn't get a new job because it looks like water — it got the job because it *sounds* right.

This is the sound-lending trick, called a phonetic. A piece isn't chosen for its picture-meaning anymore, only for its pronunciation, and a meaning-clue is bolted on beside it to keep things clear. Watch 木 (*mù*, tree) get hired again elsewhere: with a mouth radical 口 it becomes 呅, a rare character that simply borrows the *mù* sound. Same sound-brick, a totally different job!

$$\underbrace{木}_{sound: mù} + \underbrace{氵}_{meaning: water} = 沐$$
$$\underbrace{木}_{sound: mù} + \underbrace{口}_{meaning: mouth} = 呅$$

Now you try: 木 also appears in 沫 (foam) sitting beside 氵again. Can you guess which part is lending its sound, and which part is the meaning-clue?

### semantic_composition (12.6s)

## Semantic Composition

Imagine you want to draw the word "rest." You could try to paint tiredness, or a yawn — but that's hard to picture. So ancient Chinese writers tried something clever: instead of drawing one hard idea, they combined two easy pictures whose meanings, put together, point at the idea.

A person leaning against a tree — that's rest.

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \underbrace{休}_{rest}$$

This is called meaning-stacking. Sun plus moon, the two brightest things in the sky, together make "bright."

$$\underbrace{日}_{sun} + \underbrace{月}_{moon} = \underbrace{明}_{bright}$$

Writers used this trick again and again: woman plus child makes "good" — what could be better? Three trees crowd together to make "forest," and three people make "crowd." Once you know a picture's meaning, you can reuse it in brand new combinations to build ideas you've never seen before.

**Now you try:** Think of two simple pictures that, combined, could mean "peace." (Hint: what would make a home feel calm?)

### phono_semantic_principle (5.5s)

## Phono Semantic Principle

Imagine you could build almost any LEGO castle from just two kinds of bricks: one brick that tells you *what kind of thing* you're building, and one brick that tells you *what it sounds like when you say its name*. That's the secret behind more than 80% of all Chinese characters.

Every such character is a team of two parts. One part, the meaning piece, hints at the category — water, trees, animals, feelings. The other part, the sound piece, tells you roughly how to pronounce the whole character, even if its own meaning has nothing to do with the new word.

$$\underbrace{氵}_{\text{water}} + \underbrace{青}_{\text{sound: qīng}} = 清\ (\text{qīng, "clear"})$$

The water part says "this is about liquid." The 青 part just lends its sound. Together, they build a brand-new character you can guess.

**Now you try:** Take the meaning piece for "wood" (木) and the sound piece 青 again. What new character might you predict — and what could it mean?

### zinets_portal (5.2s)

## Zinets Portal

Imagine a giant web of glowing dots on a screen, each dot a Chinese character, connected by lines to the simpler characters that helped build it. This is the Zinets Portal. You tap one dot — say, a character meaning "bright" — and something magical happens: the portal doesn't just tell you what the character means. It looks at the lines leading into your dot, traces back to its simplest ancestors, checks what you've already tapped and learned before, and writes you a brand-new mini-lesson just for that gap.

No two learners get the same lesson twice, because no two learners have walked the same path through the web. If you already know "sun" and "moon," the portal skips ahead. If you don't, it starts there.

$$
\underbrace{\text{sun}}_{日} + \underbrace{\text{moon}}_{月} = \text{bright } (明)
$$
