### 人 (5.9s)

## 人

Stand up, put your feet apart, and stretch your arms down and out like you're about to take a step. Freeze right there. That's you — and that's also the character 人.

Look at the shape: two strokes, one leaning left, one leaning right, meeting at the top like a little roof. Ancient people looked at a person walking — seen from the side, one leg forward, one leg back — and simply drew what they saw. No decoration, no extra lines. Just a walking figure, caught in a single moment.

Because 人 means "person," it shows up inside all kinds of other characters whenever a word has something to do with people — someone resting, someone following, a crowd of someones together. It's like a tiny stick figure hiding inside bigger words, quietly reminding you: this is about humans.

Now you try: Stand like the character again, then draw 人 in the air with your finger. Can you spot 人 hiding inside another character you already know?

### 木 (5.3s)

## 木

Stand in a park and look at a tree. A straight trunk rising up, with branches spreading out to the sides — that simple shape is exactly what the character 木 draws. One line down for the trunk, a line across for the branches, and two little strokes at the bottom like roots digging into the ground.

Here's the fun part: 木 isn't just a picture. It's also a building block. Put two trees side by side and you get 林 — a grove, a small forest! Put a person resting against a tree and you get 休 — to rest, because who doesn't want to lean on a tree and relax?

But sometimes 木 isn't about trees at all — it just lends its *sound*. In 沐 (to bathe), the water radical 氵 gives the meaning, while 木 simply says "this sounds like *mù*." Same brick, different job each time!

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = 林 \; (\text{grove})$$

**Now you try:** Can you find another character with 木 hiding inside it? What job is it doing there — meaning, or sound?

### 女 (7.3s)

## 女

Imagine an old photograph from thousands of years ago — so old it's actually a carving, not a photo. In it, someone kneels quietly, hands folded, back curved gently like a bowed branch. That kneeling shape is the very old picture of a woman, and it became the character 女.

Why does this feel right? Ancient artists didn't draw a full body with all its details — they picked the one gesture that felt most familiar: a person sitting back on folded knees, calm and composed. Over centuries, that curved, kneeling outline was straightened into simple brush strokes, but you can still sense the bend of the knee and the tilt of the shoulders if you look closely.

Here's what makes 女 special: it doesn't just mean "woman" by itself — it becomes a building block for many other characters about family and relationships, like mother, older sister, or getting married. Whenever you see 女 tucked inside a bigger character, your brain can whisper, "this is probably about a person, a relationship, or family."

**Now you try:** Draw a simple kneeling stick figure. Then look at 女 — can you find the knee, the arm, and the curved back hiding inside the strokes?

### 子 (7.6s)

## 子

Imagine a tiny baby wrapped snugly in a blanket, arms tucked in, just a round head peeking out. That's what the character 子 was drawn to look like a very long time ago — a picture of a swaddled baby!

Why does this feel right? Babies are small, round, and bundled up — and 子 keeps that same cozy, compact shape. It's simple, with a little "head" on top and "arms" wrapped below.

But 子 grew up to do more than mean "child." Chinese speakers started attaching it to the end of other words, like a friendly little tag, to turn them into naming words (nouns). It's like adding "-let" or "-y" in English — think "piglet" or "doggy" — small endings that just say "this is a thing, a noun."

$$
\text{noun} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{noun-tag}} = \text{new noun}
$$

Now you try: Can you think of an English word that gets a special ending just to show it's a "thing" — like "-er" in "teach" + "-er" = "teacher"? 子 works the same friendly way!

### 宀 (6.1s)

## 宀

Picture a little roof drawn in one quick stroke — a peak at the top, then two slanted sides sweeping down, like a tiny tent or the sloped top of a house. This shape is called 宀, and it's known as the "roof radical." By itself, it isn't a word you'd say out loud — it's a piece, a shelter shape, waiting for something to sit underneath it.

Why does this feel right? Think about what a roof actually does: it doesn't just sit there — it protects whatever is under it from rain, wind, and cold. So when 宀 appears at the top of a Chinese character, it's a hint: "something is being sheltered, housed, or kept safe here."

You'll see 宀 crowning many characters about home and family — like 家 (home) or 安 (peaceful, safe). The roof shape gives you a clue before you even know the word's meaning!

**Now you try:** Draw 宀 on its own. Then imagine — if you were a tiny object sitting under this roof, what would you want to be protected from?

### 从 (7.6s)

## 从

Imagine you are walking to school, and your little brother trails right behind you, taking the exact same path, step for step. That's the picture of 从 (cóng) — one person walking, and a second person following close behind.

Look at the shape: two identical figures, 人 and 人, one right after the other. The character doesn't need a special symbol for "follow" — it just draws the following! The second person is literally copying the first person's position, like a shadow that walks.

That's why 从 means "to follow" or "from" — it shows movement that starts at one person and trails to the next, the same way a path starts at one place and leads to another.

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{a person walking}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{another person right behind}} = 从 \ (\text{to follow})$$

**Now you try:** Stand up and take three steps. Have a friend walk exactly behind you, matching your steps. That's 从 — you are the first 人, your friend is the second 人, and together you are "following."

### 日 (6.5s)

## 日

Look up at the sky at noon. What do you see? A bright circle, so round and so full of light that it almost hurts to stare at it. Long ago, someone wanted to draw that feeling — brightness, roundness, the one thing in the sky everyone recognizes — using just a brush and ink.

A circle is easy to imagine but hard to paint neatly with a brush, so the artist squared it off a little, and added a small mark in the middle, like a seed of light glowing inside. That became 日 — the character for "sun" or "day," since a day is simply the time the sun is out.

$$
\underbrace{\bigcirc}_{\text{sun's shape}} + \underbrace{-}_{\text{glowing mark}} = \boxed{\text{日}}
$$

This little square-circle isn't just the sun by itself — it becomes a building block. Many characters about brightness, warmth, or time borrow a piece of 日 inside them.

**Now you try:** Draw a circle, then flatten its top and bottom like a window. Add one line inside. That's 日 — can you feel the sun sitting inside it?

### 月 (6.3s)

## 月

Look up at the night sky. What shape do you usually see? Not a full round circle — more often a curved sliver, like a banana or a fingernail clipping. That curved shape became the character 月, drawn from a crescent moon.

But here's a puzzle: 月 also shows up inside words that have nothing to do with the sky — like 脸 (face), 肚 (belly), 腿 (leg). Why would "moon" hide inside "leg"?

It doesn't! Long ago, there was a *different* character shaped almost exactly like the moon — it meant "flesh," a piece of meat or body. Over centuries of writing, the flesh symbol and the moon symbol slowly changed shape until they looked identical. Two separate ancestors ended up wearing the same face.

So 月 is really two characters pretending to be one: moon in words about the sky, and "body-flesh" in words about the body.

$$
\underbrace{\text{crescent moon}}_{\text{sky meaning}} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{ancient flesh symbol}}_{\text{body meaning}}
$$

**Now you try:** Find 肩 (shoulder) and 明 (bright). Which 月 means moon, and which means body?

### 林 (5.6s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing alone in a yard. Now imagine standing where trees surround you on every side — trunks close together, leaves overlapping above your head. That's not just "a tree" anymore. That's woods.

Chinese writing has a beautifully simple trick for this: to show "more," draw the same picture twice. One tree, 木, is a single tree. Put two of them side by side, and you get 林 (lín) — woods. No new symbol was invented. The character just doubled itself, and the meaning grew right along with it.

This is the simplest building rule in the whole writing system: repetition as intensification. One becomes many just by copying itself.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{林}_{\text{woods}}
$$

**Now you try:** If one 木 is a tree and two make 林 (woods), what do you think three trees stacked together might mean? Draw it and guess before you check!

### 休 (5.6s)

## 休

Imagine you've been walking all day. Your legs are tired, the sun is hot, and up ahead you spot a big shady tree. What do you do? You walk right up to it and lean your back against the trunk, letting it hold you up while you catch your breath.

That's exactly the picture inside this character. On one side stands 人, a simple drawing of a person — just two legs and a body. On the other side stands 木, a tree with branches reaching up and roots reaching down. Put the person right next to the tree, leaning in, and you get 休 — rest.

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{休}_{\text{rest}}$$

The two pictures snap together into one idea, like a tiny comic strip frozen in a single frame.

**Now you try:** Cover the character and draw it from memory — a person, then a tree, standing side by side. Can you feel why leaning against a tree became the symbol for resting?

### 众 (5.4s)

## 众

Imagine one person standing alone on a big empty field. Now two friends come to join. Now a whole class of people arrives — so many that you can't count them one by one anymore. That feeling, when "some people" turns into "so many people," is exactly what 众 (zhòng) captures.

You already met this trick with 森, where three 木 (tree) piled up to become a whole forest. 众 does the very same thing, but with people instead of trees. One 人 is just a person. Two 人 side by side is still countable — a pair, a duo. But three 人 stacked together stop being individuals in your mind. They blur into a crowd, a mass, humanity itself.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = \underbrace{众}_{crowd}
$$

Now you try: Can you think of an English word that also means "so many, I stopped counting"? How is that different from just saying "three people"?

### 好 (7.4s)

## 好

Imagine a home three thousand years ago. What made people feel that everything was right with the world? Not gold, not a big house — just a mother sitting with her child. Ancient Chinese writers looked at that scene and turned it into a single word: 好, meaning "good."

Why does this feel right? Because a woman and a child together is one of the oldest pictures of safety, care, and warmth that humans share. The word doesn't describe "good" with abstract rules — it shows you a small, complete moment of happiness and says: *this* is what good looks like.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = \underbrace{好}_{good}
$$

Two simple pictures, placed side by side, become a feeling.

**Now you try:** Look around your own home. Is there a small everyday moment — someone reading to you, someone cooking dinner — that you would draw as your own picture of "good"?

### 字 (5.8s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny child tucked safely under a roof at night. That's the picture hiding inside this character: a roof on top, a child underneath. This character means "character" — as in, the very word for the writing symbols you're learning right now!

Why a child under a roof? Because new characters are like children — they're born from older, simpler pieces, and they grow up to become part of a family. Just as a child needs a home to be raised in, a new character needs older pieces to be built from.

Here's how it comes together:

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字 \text{ (character)}$$

This idea is so important that scholars even named a whole way of studying Chinese writing after it: ZiNets — networks of 字, tracing how characters connect like family trees.

**Now you try:** Can you think of something else that needs a "roof" (a starting structure) before it can grow, like a seed needing soil? Draw your own roof-and-child picture!

### 安 (7.6s)

## 安

Imagine a stormy night. Rain is pounding, wind is howling — but inside a small house, a woman sits safely under the roof, warm and unworried. That feeling of "nothing bad can reach me here" is exactly what this character captures.

Look at its shape: on top is a roof, like a little tent covering everything below. Underneath the roof sits a woman. A roof over a person already feels calmer than a person standing out in the open — but this character makes it specific: it's a home, with someone settled inside it.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = 安 \text{ (peace, safety)}$$

That's why 安 shows up in words like 平安 (safe and sound) and 安全 (safety) — anywhere something is protected, calm, or free from danger.

**Now you try:** Close your eyes and picture your own safest place — maybe your bed, your kitchen, or a parent's lap. Draw a tiny roof over a stick-figure person. That drawing *is* the feeling of 安.

### 明 (5.8s)

## 明

Imagine two lanterns hanging in the sky. One is the sun, glowing all day. One is the moon, glowing all night. Now imagine someone paints them side by side, on the same wall, at the same time — sun on the left, moon on the right. What would that picture mean?

It would mean *all the light there is*. Not just daylight, not just moonlight — every kind of brightness, put together. That's the idea behind 明 (míng). It doesn't just mean "sunny" or "moonlit." It means bright, clear, shining — the biggest kind of light you can imagine, because it borrows brightness from both sky-lights at once.

$$
\underbrace{日}_{sun} + \underbrace{月}_{moon} = \underbrace{明}_{bright}
$$

Sun gives its light. Moon gives its light. Together, they make brightness itself.

**Now you try:** Cover the sun (日) with your hand — does 明 still feel bright? Now cover the moon (月) instead. Notice how you need *both* pieces to make the full meaning of "bright."

### 森 (6.7s)

## 森

Imagine one tree standing alone — that's 木. Now put two trees together — 林, a little grove, maybe where two friends play. But what if trees just keep going, farther than you can count, farther than you can see? That's 森 — a whole forest, thick and wild.

Here's the wonderful part: you don't need a new picture to draw a forest. You just need one more tree! The character grows the same way the forest grows — brick by brick, tree by tree. Two trees felt like "a few." Three trees feels like "so many I can't count them" — and that's exactly the feeling a forest gives you.

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \text{ (forest)}$$

Notice the pattern: $木 \xrightarrow{+木} 林 \xrightarrow{+木} 森$. Each added tree makes the meaning bigger — from *one*, to *a few*, to *many*.

**Now you try:** Draw 木, then 林, then 森 in a row. Say what each one feels like — lonely, friendly, endless. Can you think of another word where "more pieces" means "more of something"?

### semantic_composition (11.8s)

## Semantic Composition

Imagine you could draw an idea instead of just an object. That's what ancient scribes did when a single picture wasn't enough. They took two picture-symbols and pushed them together — not to make a new sound, but to make a new *meaning*, like a little visual riddle.

Look at 休 (rest): a person leaning against a tree. Person + tree = a place to pause.

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \underbrace{休}_{rest}$$

This trick is called 会意 (huìyì) — "meeting of ideas." Scribes could keep combining familiar pieces to build brand-new meanings, like sun and moon together meaning "bright," since those are the two brightest things in the sky. Even 字 (character) itself is built this way — a child under a roof, meaning something is being "given birth to" and nurtured indoors.

**Now you try:** 好 combines "woman" + "child." 森 stacks three "trees." 众 stacks three "people." What ideas do you think they meant to create?

### heritage_and_classroom (6.6s)

## Heritage And Classroom

Imagine a girl named Mei who grew up hearing Chinese at home but never learned to read it. Every character looks like a brand-new wall to climb, and there are thousands of walls. Her teacher hands her a list to memorize by Friday. It feels impossible — like memorizing a phone book.

But what if Mei already knows more than she thinks? She knows *water*, she knows *tree*, she knows *sun*. These aren't new — they're bricks she already owns. A character like 海 (sea) isn't a mystery wall; it's water joined with something else, built the same way a LEGO castle is built from pieces she's held before.

$$\underbrace{氵}_{water} + \underbrace{每}_{every} = 海 \text{ (sea)}$$

Once Mei sees the bricks, she stops memorizing shapes and starts recognizing *stories*. A classroom full of heritage learners — kids who speak a language but can't yet read it — can be taught this way for free: show the bricks first, and the wall builds itself.

**Now you try**: Pick a word you know in any language. Can you find two smaller pieces hiding inside it?
