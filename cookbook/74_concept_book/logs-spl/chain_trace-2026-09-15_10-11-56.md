### 女 (6.0s)

## 女

Picture someone kneeling gracefully, hands folded in her lap, head slightly bowed — that's the shape ancient people saw and drew when they wanted to write "woman." Look at the character: the curved strokes are her arms crossing gently, and the base is her knees tucked beneath her.

Why does this shape feel right? Because long ago, kneeling wasn't just something women did — it was drawn as a picture of grace, care, and quiet strength. The character freezes that moment in a simple sketch.

Here's what makes 女 special: it's not just its own word, it's a building block. Add a roof above her for "peace" (安 — a woman safe at home), or place her next to a child for "good" (好 — woman and child together). Many family and relationship characters grow out of this one kneeling figure.

Now you try: Draw 女 slowly, saying "kneeling person" as you go. Then look around — can you spot 女 hiding inside any other characters you know?

### 马 (7.6s)

## 马

Picture a horse galloping across an open field — strong legs, a long face, a mane blowing in the wind. That's 马, the character for "horse," and if you squint at its shape, you can almost still see the horse standing on its four legs, tail flicking behind it.

Here's the surprising part: 马 doesn't always mean horse. Sometimes it just lends its *sound* to another character, like a friend who shows up to help even though they're not the main character. In 妈 (mother), 马 sits next to the "woman" piece and simply says "ma" — no horses involved. In 吗 (the little word that turns a sentence into a question), 马 sits next to "mouth" and again just says "ma."

So 马 has two jobs: sometimes it's the horse itself, and sometimes it's just a sound, hitching a ride inside bigger characters.

Now you try: say "ma" out loud. Can you feel how it's the same sound whether you're saying "mother" or asking a question — even though neither one is about horses at all?

### 妈 (6.1s)

## 妈

Imagine you call out "Mom!" the moment you walk in the door. That sound — *mā* — is one of the very first words a baby learns to say, in almost every language on Earth. Chinese writing gives this word a picture made of two smaller pictures side by side.

On the left sits 女, the symbol for "woman" — it once looked like a person kneeling gracefully. On the right sits 马, which means "horse," but here it isn't bringing any horses into the picture at all. It's only lending its *sound*, "ma," the way a stamp lends its shape without caring what ink is used.

So 妈 quietly says: "a female person, pronounced ma" — and that adds up to *mother*. One half tells you the meaning, the other half tells you how to say it. Together, they greet you at the door before the word even leaves your mouth.

**Now you try:** Cover the right side of 妈 with your finger. What's left is 女, "woman." Can you think of another word where a sound-piece rides along just to tell you *how* to say it, not *what* it means?
