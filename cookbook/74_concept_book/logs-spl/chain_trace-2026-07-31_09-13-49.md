## Data Privacy

### The Whisper Game

Imagine you tell your best friend a secret — just one person. Now imagine that friend tells two others, each of whom tells two more. Within ten steps, your secret has reached over a thousand ears. You never chose that. You never consented.

This is exactly what happens with personal data online, except the "whisper game" runs at the speed of light, across continents, inside systems you can't see.

### What Privacy Really Means

Privacy isn't about having something to hide. It's about *control*: who gets to know what about you, when, and for what purpose. Your medical history shared with your doctor is appropriate. That same history sold to an insurance company to raise your premiums is a betrayal.

The core tension is between **utility** and **exposure**. More data shared → more useful services, but also more risk. The goal of data privacy is to find the sweet spot.

### A Tiny Bit of Math

Suppose a dataset has $n$ people. A "query" asks something like "what is the average age?" A careful system adds random noise $\varepsilon$ to the answer:

$$\tilde{a} = a + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$$

Large $\sigma$ protects individuals (any one person's data drowns in noise), but makes the answer less accurate. Small $\sigma$ is precise but leaky. This trade-off has a formal name — **differential privacy** — and it guarantees that no single person's inclusion changes the output in a detectable way.

### Now You Try

You run a school survey asking students their home addresses. How could you publish neighbourhood-level statistics without revealing any individual's exact street? Sketch two strategies — one using grouping, one using noise — and describe which feels safer to you.

### ethics_in_data_science (31.6s)

## Ethics In Data Science

In 2014, Amazon built a hiring tool trained on a decade of résumés. The data reflected a decade of mostly male engineers being hired — so the model learned to penalise résumés that contained the word "women's" (as in "women's chess club"). The tool was quietly scrapped in 2018. Nobody programmed bias in. The data carried it silently.

This is the core tension in data science ethics: **your model inherits the values baked into your data**, whether you intend it or not.

Think of data as a photograph of the past. If the past was unfair, the photograph is unfair too. Training a model on that photograph doesn't capture truth — it captures history, including its injustices. Then the model makes decisions that entrench those injustices as if they were natural law. Subtle details — zip code, name, browsing history — carry the signal even when the sensitive attribute itself is removed.

Formalising one idea: for any group $G$ in your data, compute its **outcome rate** $r_G$ — the fraction of people in that group who received the positive outcome (hired, approved, admitted). A model trained to minimise average error will tend to reproduce the $r_G$ it saw in training, replicating historical gaps as if they were facts about the world.

Ethical data science asks three questions before deployment:

1. **Fairness** — do outcome rates converge across groups after the model is applied?
2. **Transparency** — can the person affected understand *why* a decision was made?
3. **Accountability** — who is responsible when it goes wrong?

**Now you try.** Take a dataset you know — school grades, movie ratings, job listings. Identify one group split that feels relevant. Estimate whether $r_G$ would differ across that split, and ask yourself: does the gap reflect the world as it *is*, or as it *was*?

### personally_identifiable_information (16.0s)

## Personally Identifiable Information

Imagine you receive an envelope in the mail. The envelope itself tells a story before you even open it: your name, your street address, the city where you live. A stranger who intercepts that envelope now knows exactly who you are and where to find you. That combination of details — name plus address — is what we call **personally identifiable information**, or PII.

The key intuition is not about any single fact in isolation, but about *linkability*. Your first name alone ("Maria") tells almost nothing. Your birthday alone ("March 14") is shared by millions. But the moment you combine first name, birthday, and zip code, the crowd of possible matches shrinks rapidly — often to just one person. You have been *identified*.

Think of it like a lock with multiple tumblers. One tumbler barely constrains the key. Three tumblers together open exactly one lock. PII works the same way: each additional attribute multiplies the identifying power.

We can make this precise. Suppose there are $N$ people in a population and an attribute splits them into groups of average size $g$. After combining $k$ independent attributes, the expected group size shrinks to roughly

$$g^k \quad \text{(assuming independence)}.$$

When $g^k \approx 1$, you have effectively singled out one person. A zip code might narrow a city of $500{,}000$ down to $g \approx 5{,}000$; add birth year ($g \approx 100$) and gender ($g \approx 2$), and $5{,}000 \times \frac{1}{100} \times \frac{1}{2} = 25$ — already a small crowd, and one more attribute isolates you completely.

**Now you try.** Your school has 800 students. If grade level ($k=1$) splits them into 4 equal groups, and then club membership ($k=2$) halves each group, how many students share your exact combination? What if you add your birth month?

### regulatory_compliance (20.9s)

## Regulatory Compliance

Imagine you open a small bakery. The city hands you a list of rules: your kitchen must stay above 60 °F, you can use no more than 500 watts of signage, and your noise level must stay below 70 decibels. Break *any single one* and the inspector closes you down. Satisfying all of them at once — that is regulatory compliance.

Notice the shape of the problem. Each rule draws a boundary. On one side: acceptable. On the other: violation. Your whole operation must live inside *every* boundary simultaneously. This is not about finding the best outcome; it is about finding an outcome that exists at all.

Mathematically, each regulation is an **inequality constraint**. If you have $n$ rules over $k$ operating parameters $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k$, each rule $i$ says:

$$f_i(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k) \leq b_i$$

The **feasible region** $\mathcal{F}$ is the set of all parameter combinations that satisfy every constraint at once:

$$\mathcal{F} = \{ \mathbf{x} \mid f_i(\mathbf{x}) \leq b_i \text{ for all } i = 1, \ldots, n \}$$

If $\mathcal{F}$ is empty, no compliant state exists — you must renegotiate the rules or change what you are building. If $\mathcal{F}$ is non-empty, you have room to operate, and *then* you can ask which point inside is best.

The key insight is the ordering: **feasibility before optimality**. Most real-world systems live under layers of hard constraints before any optimization begins.

**Now you try.** A food truck must keep costs below \$200 per day and serve at least 40 customers. Write two inequality constraints using $c$ (cost) and $q$ (customers). Sketch the feasible region on a simple grid — where does it sit?

## Hipaa

Imagine you visit a doctor for something embarrassing — a skin rash, a mental health concern, a test you'd rather keep private. You share that information because you have to, not because you want the world to know. Now picture your employer, your insurance company, and your nosy neighbour all receiving a copy of your file the next day. That feeling of violation — that's exactly what HIPAA was designed to prevent.

HIPAA stands for the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, passed in the United States in 1996. Its core intuition is simple: **your health information belongs to you, not to whoever happens to store it.** When you hand your medical history to a hospital, you're not giving it away — you're lending it, under strict rules about who may read it, share it, or profit from it.

The law creates two categories worth knowing. *Covered entities* are the people and organisations that collect your data directly: hospitals, clinics, insurance companies. *Business associates* are anyone those entities share data with — a billing company, a cloud storage provider, a software vendor. HIPAA's reach extends to both, forming a chain of accountability rather than a single locked door.

The protected information itself is called **PHI** — Protected Health Information. Anything that could identify you *and* relates to your health condition, treatment, or payment qualifies: your name combined with a diagnosis, a medical record number, even a photograph.

Violations carry fines that scale with negligence, from \$100 per incident up to \$1.9 million per category per year.

**Now you try:** Think of a fitness app that syncs with your doctor's system. Is the app a covered entity, a business associate, or neither? What would change if it sold your step-count data to an advertiser?
