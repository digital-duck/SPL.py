### angle (0.0s)

## Angle

You already sense that an angle measures how much two rays "open" from a shared vertex — like the hands of a clock spreading apart. You know the basics: a full turn is $360°$, a straight line is $180°$, and a right angle is $90°$, marked with a small square. Angles smaller than $90°$ are *acute*; those between $90°$ and $180°$ are *obtuse*. None of that is new — it's the vocabulary you're bringing with you.

Here's the one new idea: angles **add**, the same way lengths along a segment add. Formally, if ray $\overrightarrow{OB}$ lies between rays $\overrightarrow{OA}$ and $\overrightarrow{OC}$ — meaning $B$ is "inside" the opening of $\angle AOC$ — then

$$\angle AOB + \angle BOC = \angle AOC$$

This is the **Angle Addition Postulate**. It says a big rotation can always be split into two smaller ones that share a ray, and their measures will sum to the whole. This matters because it's not obvious just from staring at rays: distance and rotation behave differently in general, but this particular additive property is exactly what lets you treat angles algebraically — you can solve for an unknown piece of a rotation the same way you'd solve for an unknown piece of a segment.

**The pattern:** whole = part + part, applied to turning instead of stretching.

**Practice problem:** Ray $\overrightarrow{OB}$ splits $\angle AOC$ into two parts. If $\angle AOB = 35°$ and $\angle AOC = 90°$, find $\angle BOC$. Is $\angle BOC$ acute, right, or obtuse?

### distance (0.0s)

## Distance

You already know distance as "how far apart two things are" — steps between lockers, miles between cities. Now let's make that idea precise enough to use anywhere: on a map, in a graph, even in spaces that don't look like ordinary space at all.

A **distance function**, or **metric**, is a rule $d(x, y)$ that takes two points and outputs a single non-negative number: how far apart they are. But not just any rule counts as a real distance. To earn that name, $d$ must satisfy three properties for all points $x, y, z$:

$$d(x,y) \geq 0, \quad \text{and } d(x,y) = 0 \iff x = y$$

$$d(x,y) = d(y,x) \quad \text{(symmetry — the trip back is the same length)}$$

$$d(x,z) \leq d(x,y) + d(y,z) \quad \text{(triangle inequality — detours never shorten a trip)}$$

That last rule is the deep one. It says you can never do better by going through a third point than going straight — a straight path is always the shortest, or at worst tied.

The plain number line already shows all three properties at once: $d(x,y) = |x-y|$. Check it against the rules — $|x-y|$ is never negative and is zero only when $x=y$; $|x-y| = |y-x|$, so direction doesn't matter; and $|x-z| \leq |x-y|+|y-z|$, because absolute value can't overshoot a detour. Any rule that keeps all three properties, on any set of points, earns the name "metric" — the formula can change, but the three laws can't.

**Practice:** Let $d(x,y) = |x-y|$ on the number line. Verify the triangle inequality for $x=1$, $y=5$, $z=8$ — compute $d(1,8)$ and compare it to $d(1,5)+d(5,8)$. Are they equal or is one bigger? What does that tell you about whether $5$ lies "between" $1$ and $8$?

### angle_measure (0.0s)

## Angle Measure

When two rays share an endpoint, they open up like a fan, and the *amount* of that opening is what we call the angle's measure, expressed in degrees, with a full turn around a point equal to $360°$. You already know the usual categories — acute, right, obtuse, straight, reflex — sorted by how wide that opening is.

The new idea worth digging into here is that angle measure is *additive*: if a ray splits an angle into two smaller pieces, their measures add up to the whole. That is, if ray $\overrightarrow{OB}$ lies inside $\angle AOC$, then

$$m\angle AOB + m\angle BOC = m\angle AOC$$

This fact is called the **Angle Addition Postulate**. It's a postulate, not something you prove — it's simply accepted as a basic truth about how angle measure behaves, the same way we accept that segment lengths add up along a line. But it's a powerful starting point: once you accept it, you can build a big angle out of smaller known pieces, or, working backward, find a missing piece if you know the whole and one part.

The pattern to remember: **whole = part + part**, as long as the middle ray actually lies *inside* the larger angle (otherwise the pieces don't add up cleanly). This idea will come back again and again — it's the same logic behind adding arc measures, adding areas of shapes that share a boundary, and more.

**Practice problem:** Ray $\overrightarrow{OB}$ divides $\angle AOC$ into two parts, where $m\angle AOB = 35°$ and $m\angle BOC = 61°$. Find $m\angle AOC$, and classify it as acute, right, obtuse, straight, or reflex.

### line (0.0s)

## Line

You already know a line as "the shortest path between two points" — perfectly straight, stretching forever in both directions. That intuition is right, but in geometry a line is deeper than a picture: it's one of the **undefined terms** the whole system is built on, alongside the point. We don't define "line" using simpler ideas; instead, we describe its behavior with postulates (accepted starting rules) and let everything else in geometry be built from it.

Here's the key structure: a line has infinite length but zero width, and it extends in two opposite directions without end. We name a line by two points on it, written $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$, or sometimes with a single lowercase letter, like $\ell$. Later you'll meet the line's close relatives — the segment and the ray — which just keep a limited piece of that infinite extent, but the line itself is the foundation they're built from.

One rule (a postulate, not a proven theorem) governs lines: **through any two distinct points, there is exactly one line.** This single fact is why "connect the dots" always works unambiguously — two points can never be joined by two different straight lines. It's also why three points can be *collinear* (all on one line) or not, a distinction you'll use constantly in proofs later on.

**Practice problem:** Points $P$, $Q$, and $R$ all lie on the same line $\ell$. Explain, using the postulate above, why it would be impossible to draw a *second* straight line through $P$ and $Q$ that doesn't already pass through every point line $\ell$ passes through.

### point (0.0s)

## Point

You already know a point marks a location — like a pin dropped on a map. But in math, a point is stranger than that: it's an **undefined term**. Geometry doesn't build "point" from something simpler; instead, it uses point as one of the raw ingredients everything else is made from, alongside line and plane. This might feel unsatisfying at first — shouldn't every word get a definition? — but every system of knowledge has to start somewhere. If you tried to define point using simpler words, you'd eventually run out of simpler words.

What we *can* do is describe how a point behaves. A point has **no size** — no length, width, or height. It has **location only**. In the coordinate plane, we pin that location down with an ordered pair, $P = (x, y)$. That coordinate is the point's entire identity: two points are the same point exactly when their coordinates match.

This is the pattern to hold onto: a point is a *position*, not a *thing occupying space*. That's why a line — infinitely many points in a row — still has length, while any single point on it has none. Points are the floor; lines, angles, and shapes are all collections or arrangements of points built on top.

**Practice problem:** Plot the points $A = (2, 3)$ and $B = (2, -1)$ on a coordinate plane. What do you notice about their $x$-coordinates, and what shape of path would you get if you plotted every point with $x = 2$?

### line_segment (0.0s)

## Line Segment

You already know a line segment is a straight path between two endpoints — like a ruler laid between two dots. But now let's look at what makes it more than "just a piece of a line."

A full line stretches forever in both directions. A **line segment** is bounded: it has a beginning and an end. If the endpoints are points $A$ and $B$, we write the segment as $\overline{AB}$. This notation is *symmetric* — $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{BA}$ name the exact same segment, since it doesn't matter which endpoint you call "first."

The one new idea to master here is **length**: the distance between a segment's two endpoints. In the coordinate plane, if $A = (x_1, y_1)$ and $B = (x_2, y_2)$, the length is:

$$\text{length}(\overline{AB}) = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$$

Where does this come from? Picture the horizontal gap $x_2 - x_1$ and the vertical gap $y_2 - y_1$ as the two legs of a right triangle, with the segment itself as the hypotenuse. That's just the Pythagorean theorem in disguise — squaring the legs, adding them, and taking the square root to undo the squaring. The formula isn't a separate rule to memorize; it's the Pythagorean theorem applied to any two points, anywhere on the grid.

Notice that if $A$ and $B$ sit on the same horizontal or vertical line, one of the two differences becomes zero, and the formula collapses down to simple subtraction — a good sanity check that the general formula still makes sense in the simple case.

**Practice:** Find the length of the segment with endpoints $A = (1, 2)$ and $B = (7, 10)$.

### polygon (0.0s)

## Polygon

You already know a polygon as a flat shape with straight sides — a triangle, a square, a stop sign. Now let's look at its structure more carefully.

A polygon is a closed plane figure built entirely from line segments joined end to end, where each meeting point is a **vertex** and each segment is a **side**. "Closed" means the path of segments starts and ends at the same point, with no gaps and no crossings (for now, assume *simple* polygons — sides don't intersect each other). At every vertex, two sides meet and form an **interior angle**.

Here's the key structural fact: for any simple polygon with $n$ sides, the sum of its interior angles follows a clean rule:

$$S = (n-2) \times 180^\circ$$

Why $n - 2$? Because from any single vertex, you can draw diagonals splitting the polygon into $(n-2)$ non-overlapping triangles, and each triangle contributes $180^\circ$. A triangle ($n=3$) gives $S = 180^\circ$. A quadrilateral ($n=4$) gives $S = 360^\circ$. A pentagon ($n=5$) gives $S = 540^\circ$ — you can check this by drawing a pentagon and splitting it into three triangles from one corner.

Notice what makes this rule powerful: $n$ is a variable, not a fixed number. The same formula works whether your polygon has 3 sides or 30 — you're not memorizing separate facts for each shape, you're using one structural pattern that scales.

**Practice problem:** A simple polygon has an interior angle sum of $900^\circ$. Use the formula above to find $n$, the number of sides. What shape is this?

### rigid_motion (0.0s)

## Rigid Motion

You already know rigid motions as moves that don't distort a shape — slide it, turn it, or flip it, and it comes out congruent to where it started. Now let's see why "preserves distance" is exactly the right requirement, and nothing less.

A transformation $T$ of the plane is a **rigid motion** (also called an isometry) if for every pair of points $P, Q$:

$$|T(P)T(Q)| = |PQ|$$

That's the whole definition — one condition, applied to every pair of points in the shape. Here's the key structural fact: if a transformation preserves *all* distances, it automatically preserves angles too. Think about why: an angle at vertex $B$, formed by points $A$, $B$, $C$, is pinned down entirely by the three side lengths $|AB|$, $|BC|$, $|AC|$ — change any one of those lengths and the angle has to change with it. Since $T$ keeps every length fixed, it has no room to bend the angle. So once you've nailed down distance-preservation, angle-preservation comes along for free — you don't need it as a separate rule.

This is why translations, rotations, and reflections aren't three unrelated moves you memorize separately — they're all just different ways of satisfying that one equation, $|T(P)T(Q)| = |PQ|$, for every point pair. That single condition is the mathematical floor for calling two shapes "the same shape, same size": there must exist *some* transformation obeying it that maps one shape exactly onto the other.

**Practice problem:** Triangle $ABC$ has $|AB| = 5$, $|BC| = 7$, $|AC| = 8$. After a rigid motion, triangle $A'B'C'$ is the image. If $|A'B'| = 5$ and $|B'C'| = 7$, what must $|A'C'|$ equal, and why?

### congruence (0.0s)

## Congruence

You already know congruence as the "same size, same shape" idea — two figures that match perfectly when you slide, flip, or turn one onto the other. Now let's name precisely what "sliding, flipping, or turning" means mathematically.

The key idea is a **rigid motion**: a move — translation, rotation, or reflection — that never stretches or shrinks anything. Every pair of points stays exactly the same distance apart before and after the move. When a rigid motion takes shape $A$ exactly onto shape $B$, we say $A \cong B$: not just "these look alike," but "an honest distance-preserving move connects them."

This gives congruence real teeth. For triangles, checking congruence usually comes down to comparing corresponding sides and angles — if enough of them match up in the right way, you're guaranteed a rigid motion exists connecting the two triangles, even without drawing it out step by step. That's the whole point of shortcuts like "matching sides" or "matching angle between two sides": they're proof that a rigid motion is hiding underneath, without making you find it explicitly.

Here's what rigid motions can *never* do: change size. If you stretch a triangle bigger while keeping its angles identical, the two triangles look alike but aren't congruent — that relationship has a different name (similarity), and it's a looser cousin of congruence, one that drops the "same size" requirement.

**Practice problem:** Triangle $A$ has sides $5, 7, 9$. Triangle $B$ has sides $9, 5, 7$. Are they congruent? Explain using the idea of corresponding sides.

### triangle (0.0s)

## Triangle

You already know a triangle is a polygon with three sides, and its three interior angles always add up to $180°$. Now let's look at why that's true and what else it tells us.

Take any triangle with vertices $A$, $B$, $C$. Draw a line through $C$ that runs parallel to side $AB$. Because parallel lines create equal alternate angles, the angle on the left of that new line equals angle $A$, and the angle on the right equals angle $B$. Together with the original angle $C$, these three angles sit side by side along a straight line — and a straight line measures $180°$. That's the whole proof idea in one picture, no formula needed.

This single fact — $\angle A + \angle B + \angle C = 180°$ — turns out to be a powerful shortcut. If you know two angles of a triangle, you automatically know the third:

$$\angle C = 180° - \angle A - \angle B$$

It also explains a useful rule about a triangle's **exterior angle**: an exterior angle (formed by extending one side) always equals the sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles. That's because the exterior angle and its adjacent interior angle are supplementary ($180°$ together), so subtracting the interior angle from $180°$ leaves exactly $\angle A + \angle B$.

**Practice problem:** A triangle has one angle of $52°$ and another of $71°$. Find the third interior angle, and then find the exterior angle at the vertex where the third angle sits.

### area (0.0s)

## Area

Area measures how much two-dimensional space a shape covers — the "amount of flat surface" inside a boundary, expressed in square units like cm² or m². You've likely learned rules for specific shapes: $bh$ for a rectangle, $\frac{1}{2}bh$ for a triangle, $\pi r^2$ for a circle. Now it's worth seeing what these formulas actually share.

Every one of these shapes can have its area found by breaking it into triangles — a technique called *triangulation*. Any polygon with $n$ sides can be split into $n-2$ triangles, and since a triangle's area only needs a base and a perpendicular height, the whole polygon's area becomes a sum of those simpler triangle calculations:

$$
A_{\text{polygon}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n-2} \frac{1}{2} b_i h_i
$$

A circle isn't a polygon, but the same idea still works. Imagine slicing a circle into many thin triangular wedges meeting at the center, each with height $r$ (the radius) and a tiny base along the circle's edge. Add up all those wedge areas, and as the wedges get thinner and more numerous, the total gets closer and closer to $\pi r^2$.

So the deep rule is: **almost any area can be built by triangulating the shape and adding up triangle areas.** Rectangles, triangles, polygons, and even circles are really the same idea in different disguises.

**Practice problem:** A regular hexagon has side length $s = 4$ cm and can be split into 6 equilateral triangles meeting at its center, each with height (from center to side) $h = 2\sqrt{3}$ cm. Find the hexagon's total area.

### triangle_congruence_criteria (0.0s)

## Triangle Congruence Criteria

You already know the intuition: two triangles are congruent when they're exact copies of each other — same shape, same size, just possibly flipped or rotated. But checking all six measurements (three sides, three angles) every time is overkill. The real question geometry answers is: what's the *minimum* information that locks a triangle's shape completely?

It turns out three carefully chosen measurements are enough — but not just any three. The criteria are:

- **SSS**: all three sides equal ($AB = DE$, $BC = EF$, $CA = FD$)
- **SAS**: two sides and the *included* angle (the angle between them) equal
- **ASA**: two angles and the *included* side equal
- **AAS**: two angles and a *non-included* side equal

Why "included" matters: if you know two sides but not the angle between them, that angle can hinge open or closed like a door, producing different triangles with the same two side lengths. Fixing the included angle removes that freedom. Similarly, two angles already determine the third (since angles sum to $180°$), so ASA and AAS actually contain the same information — just naming a different side as known.

Notice what's *missing*: there's no "SSA" criterion (two sides and a non-included angle) as a general rule — that setup can produce two different triangles from the same data, which is why it's sometimes called the "ambiguous case."

**Practice problem:** Triangle $ABC$ has $AB = 7$, $\angle A = 40°$, and $\angle B = 65°$. Triangle $DEF$ has $DE = 7$, $\angle D = 40°$, and $\angle F = 75°$. Are the triangles congruent? Which criterion applies (or fails)?

### pythagorean_theorem (0.0s)

## Pythagorean Theorem

You already know the headline: in a right triangle with legs $a$, $b$, and hypotenuse $c$, $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2.$$ But look at what's actually being claimed. The left side is built from *lengths* — how far apart points are. The right angle, meanwhile, is a *rotation*: a 90° turn between the two legs. The theorem says these two completely different ideas — distance and angle — are locked together by a single algebraic equation. That's a big deal: if the side lengths satisfy $a^2+b^2=c^2$, the angle *must* be 90°, no protractor needed, and vice versa.

Here's the structural reason it works, and it comes down to one idea: comparing areas. Take a right triangle and build a square of area $c^2$ directly on the hypotenuse. Around it, arrange four copies of the same triangle so they tile a larger square of side $(a+b)$. That big square's area splits two ways: $(a+b)^2$, or four triangle-areas ($4 \cdot \tfrac12 ab$) plus the inner square $c^2$. Setting these equal and simplifying:

$$(a+b)^2 = 4\left(\tfrac12 ab\right) + c^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; a^2+b^2 = c^2.$$

Two ways of counting the same area must give the same number — that's the whole trick, no separate machinery required.

**Rule to remember:** the theorem only fires when the angle between $a$ and $b$ is exactly $90°$. Change the angle, and the equation stops holding.

**Practice:** A ladder 13 ft long leans against a wall, its base 5 ft from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?

### similarity (0.0s)

## Similarity

Two rigid shapes are *congruent* when one lands exactly on the other after a slide, turn, or flip. Similarity relaxes that idea: you're allowed to resize too. Formally, a similarity transformation is a rigid motion composed with a uniform scaling — a dilation by some factor $k > 0$ applied equally in every direction, followed by (or preceded by) rotation, reflection, or translation. We write $A \sim B$ when shape $B$ is the image of shape $A$ under such a transformation.

Because rigid motions already preserve angles and distance *ratios* (just not absolute distances), and dilation scales every length by the same $k$, the composition preserves two things exactly: angle measure, and the ratio between any two corresponding lengths. If sides of $A$ have lengths $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$, then the corresponding sides of $B$ satisfy

$$\frac{b_1}{a_1} = \frac{b_2}{a_2} = \frac{b_3}{a_3} = \cdots = k$$

This is the whole structure in one line: *same angles, proportional sides*. The single number $k$ is the key idea to hold onto — once you know it, you know exactly how every length in $B$ relates to the matching length in $A$. Find $k$ from one known pair of corresponding sides, then use it to find every other unknown side by cross-multiplication. No new tools are needed beyond this one ratio; the whole payoff of similarity is that it collapses into a single multiplier.

**Practice:** Triangle $A$ has sides $3, 4, 5$. Triangle $B$ is similar to $A$, and its shortest side is $7.5$. Find $k$, then find the other two side lengths of $B$.

### architecture_construction (12.2s)

## Architecture Construction

A tape measure and a chalk line are enough to swing a perfect right angle: mark a 3-unit segment, a 4-unit segment, and a 5-unit segment into a triangle, and the corner opposite the longest side is exactly $90°$. That's the 3-4-5 rule doing quiet, essential work — but a building isn't one triangle. It's dozens of connected shapes, and each one needs its own quantities before a single wall goes up.

Consider a rectangular room. Its floor area, $A = l \times w$, tells the contractor how much flooring to order. Its wall area — $2h(l+w)$ for the four walls, minus doors and windows — tells them how much drywall or paint is needed. These aren't separate skills from squaring the corners; they're the next question an architect asks once the shape is locked in: *given this correct geometry, how much material fills it?*

Now scale that idea up. A blueprint is a similar figure to the real building, related by a single scale factor $k$. If a wall is drawn $1\,\text{cm} = 50\,\text{cm}$ (so $k = 50$), every length in the real building is $50\times$ the drawing's length. But area doesn't scale by $k$ — it scales by $k^2$. A room drawn as a $2\,\text{cm} \times 3\,\text{cm}$ rectangle has a real floor area of $(2 \cdot 50)(3 \cdot 50) = 15{,}000\,\text{cm}^2$, not $50 \times (2\times3)$. Architects rely on this $k^2$ rule constantly — misjudge it, and you order a fraction of the flooring you actually need.

**Practice:** A blueprint uses a scale of $k = 40$. A rectangular room measures $3\,\text{cm} \times 5\,\text{cm}$ on the blueprint. Find the room's actual floor area in square centimeters, and check that a $90°$ corner drawn on the blueprint really would form a 3-4-5-style right angle if built at 3 m and 4 m along its walls.
