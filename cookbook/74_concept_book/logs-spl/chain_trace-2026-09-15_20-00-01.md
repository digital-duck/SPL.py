### 木 (30.3s)

## 木

Picture this: you're standing in a forest and spot one perfect tree. A trunk holds everything up, branches reach the sky. The character **木** (wood/tree) is a picture of exactly that—simple, clear, unmistakable.

But here's where 木 gets clever: it becomes a **building block** for other ideas.

What happens when you put two 木 together?

$$\underbrace{\text{木}}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{\text{木}}_{\text{tree}} = \text{林}$$

You get 林 (forest). Two trees, and the meaning multiplies just like in nature.

Sometimes 木 combines differently. Add a picture of a person:

$$\underbrace{\text{木}}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{\text{人}}_{\text{person}} = \text{休}$$

You get 休 (rest)—a person leaning against a tree. The tree now tells a *story* about resting.

But 木 can also be silent. In **沐**, it sits inside water (氵) and contributes only its *sound*—not meaning. Like an actor playing two roles: sometimes speaking, sometimes just making noise in the background.

This is the magic of Chinese characters: one simple picture can mean something, build new meanings when combined, or quietly lend its sound.

**Now you try:** Why might two trees feel like a forest? Why does resting against a tree make sense as a picture for "rest"?

### 女 (12.7s)

## 女

**Picture this**: Imagine you're drawing a simple figure sitting or kneeling. What shape do you see? Two legs folded, arms close to the body. This is how ancient writers saw the shape of a woman—simplified into lines. Today, this shape has become one of the most important building blocks in Chinese writing.

**Why it feels right**: The character 女 literally shows a person in a particular posture. When you see this shape inside a bigger character, it whispers to you: "This is about people, family, or relationships." It's like a visual clue that tells your brain what to expect.

**How it builds other characters**: 女 doesn't stand alone in most modern writing—it's a *radical*, a semantic building block. When you add other pieces to 女, you create new meanings:

$$\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{马}_{horse} = 妈 \text{ (mother)}$$

$$\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{良}_{good} = 娘 \text{ (young woman)}$$

Notice the pattern: the 女 shape tells you the *category* (family, people), while the added piece adds specific meaning.

**Now you try**: Look at these characters and spot the 女 hiding inside: 妹 (younger sister), 姐 (older sister), 娜 (a name). What do they all have in common?

### 人 (16.8s)

## 人

Picture this: you're watching someone walk down the street. From high above, what do you see? Two legs moving forward, a body between them. That simple shape—two lines like legs—is exactly what ancient Chinese writers drew to represent a person. They didn't draw a face or hands. They drew the most essential thing: *two legs in motion*.

The character 人 captures this core idea. It's not a detailed picture; it's the *idea* of personhood made visible. When humans move through the world, we move on two legs. That fundamental truth became a symbol that lasted thousands of years.

Once you understand 人 as "a walking figure," you start to see it everywhere in Chinese. Other characters build from it. When 人 appears inside or beside another character, it whispers: *people are involved here*. The meaning echoes across the writing system.

Try this: watch someone walk and sketch what you see from above—just two curved lines and a space between them. You've just reinvented the same idea that ancient writers discovered.

### 子 (24.4s)

## 子

**Picture this:** When you see a tiny kitten or puppy, something about its smallness makes you want to call it by a special name—something affectionate and close. In Chinese, the character 子 started this way: it meant a swaddled infant, a dependent little one. But over time, the language borrowed this feeling to describe other things too, turning simple words into nouns by adding 子 at the end.

**Why does this work?** The character 子 carries the warmth and closeness of childhood—something familiar, individual, and easy to count. When you add 子 to a word, you turn it into something you can point at and count: one, two, three. A table becomes 桌子 because tables are discrete objects you notice. An orange becomes 橙子, because a ripe fruit is something distinct you reach for. The 子 reminds us that these are real, separate things in the world.

**The pattern:** Many Chinese nouns follow this structure:

$$\underbrace{\text{core meaning}}_{\text{root}} + \underbrace{\text{子}}_{\text{makes it countable}} = \text{noun}$$

So 猫 (cat-essence) becomes 猫子 (a kitten, or simply "a cat").

**Now you try:** If 叶 means leaf, what do you think 叶子 means? Why would adding 子 help you understand it?

### 宀 (13.0s)

## 宀

**Picture this:** You're building a house, and you start with the most important part—the roof. Once that's there, everything underneath is safe and sheltered. The Chinese character-builders thought the same way. They created a radical that *looks* like a roof: 宀. Whatever sits underneath this roof is protected, contained, or part of a home.

**Here's the intuition:** Just like a real roof covers and shelters whatever is below it, this radical tells you: "Look inside—something is being housed or protected here." It's a visual clue before you even know the full character's meaning.

**How it builds:** The roof radical combines with other character pieces to create new meanings:

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{家 (home)}$$

A roof over a child? That's family—the place where children are sheltered. See how the picture tells the story?

### 马 (11.4s)

## 马

**Picture this:** Imagine you're creating a code where each picture stands for a sound. You draw a horse 🐴, and everyone knows to say "maaah" when they see it. Pretty clever, right? That's exactly what happened in Chinese writing thousands of years ago.

**The intuition:** The character 马 looks like a horse's head and legs if you squint—four vertical strokes for the legs, a curve for the neck and head. Ancient people noticed that when they said the word for "horse," it started with a nice, strong sound: *ma*. So 马 became the picture *and* the sound.

But here's where it gets really clever. Once everyone agreed that 马 meant *ma*, writers could use that same character in *other* words that also had the *ma* sound—even if those words had nothing to do with horses. In the word for "mother" (妈), you see 马 tucked inside, lending its *ma* sound. Same with the question-mark character (吗). The horse isn't really there anymore; it's just humming along, providing the sound.

### 从 (11.7s)

## 从

**Picture this:** Imagine you're at the mall and you want to stick with your friends. You walk behind Jenny, and she walks behind Marco. One person after another, following along. In Chinese, this idea—one person behind another—is captured in a single character: 从.

**Here's the clever part:** If you look at 从, you can actually see two people standing together. The first person is in front (leading), and the second person is close behind (following). This is exactly what "to follow" means—being where the person ahead of you is going.

The character is built from its pieces:

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = 从$$

Two people, one behind the other, walking the same path. That's what 从 shows us.

### 口 (14.3s)

Picture this: You're playing a guessing game with a friend. They open their mouth wide and make a big "O" shape—not to say anything, but to show you they're about to speak. That circle opening? That's what the Chinese character 口 draws.

The character 口 is a rectangle—like a door or window. And that makes sense, because your mouth is an opening. It's how your voice comes out when you speak. It's where food goes in when you eat. It's even what you make when you ask a question and look surprised. Open wide!

The beauty of this character is that it looks exactly like what it means: a small space, bounded by four simple lines, ready to let something in or let something out. Ancient Chinese writers saw a mouth and thought, "That's an opening. Let's draw an opening."

### 日 (20.4s)

## 日

Imagine you're an ancient person looking up at the sky. The sun blazes as a bright, glowing circle—but how do you draw it with just a brush? How do you show that it's *the* source of all brightness?

Here's what the ancient Chinese figured out: draw a square (simple, clean) and put a dot in the middle to show the light shining from inside. Two simple ideas, one powerful symbol:

$$\underbrace{\text{square}}_{boundary} + \underbrace{\text{dot}}_{brightness} = \text{日}$$

Over centuries, that picture became this character. It's not a realistic drawing anymore—it's a *symbol*. But the shape still works: a frame with light at its heart. Every time you see 日, your mind instantly knows—this is the sun, this is brightness itself.

### 月 (31.4s)

## 月

You step outside at night and look up. The moon isn't always round—sometimes it's just a thin smile in the sky, a crescent of light. The ancient Chinese people watched this shape night after night and drew it: a crescent, a curved slice of light. That's 月 (yuè), the character for moon.

But here's something magical: the same crescent shape appears *inside* characters about the body. Why would the ancient writers do this? Because flesh, like the moon, is soft and curved. They reused the moon shape as a building block, a family crest that marks any word about your body:

$$\underbrace{月}_{\text{flesh}} + \text{other parts} = \text{body-word}$$

Look at 肺 (lung), 肌 (muscle), 肥 (plump)—they all shelter 月 inside. One crescent shape, two ancestries: the moon in the night sky, and the flesh in your body.

Bodies are as real and observable as the moon itself. Next time you meet 月, pause and ask: "Is this the moon? Or is it showing me something about the body?" The crescent will guide you.

### 林 (13.3s)

## 林

**Picture stepping into the forest**

**How repetition creates power**

This is one of the simplest building rules in language: when you repeat something, you don't just get more of it—you get something *stronger*. One knock on a door is a tap. Two knocks sound intentional, like someone really wants in. One footstep is just noise. Many footsteps together? You hear an army coming. The second piece transforms the first.

**The composition**

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林 \text{ (forest)}$$

**Now you try**

### 水 (17.8s)

## 水

Picture a flowing stream on a summer morning. You dip your hand in—it's cool, it moves, it slips through your fingers. Water is always moving, always finding its way around obstacles. The ancient people who created Chinese characters watched this, and they wanted a character that *felt* like water.

Look at 水. There's a vertical line down the middle—like the river's heart, the main current. On both sides, little curves branch outward like ripples spreading across the surface. Even just looking at it, you can feel the water flowing down and spreading out.

Here's the clever part: 水 is so useful for describing things that hold liquid that it gets reused as a building block. When 水 stands alone, you write all four sides. But when it joins another character to describe something wet or liquid—like 河 (river) or 流 (to flow)—it shrinks to three drops on the left:

$$\underbrace{氵}_{\text{three water drops}} + \underbrace{\text{meaning part}}_{\text{other character}} = \text{word with water}$$

The 氵 whispers: "this word has water in it."

### 吗 (32.5s)

## 吗

**The Question Mark That Sounds Like a Horse**

Imagine you're asking a friend, "Do you want to play?" In English, you change your voice to show you're asking. In Chinese, there's a special character you add to the end of a sentence that says, "I'm asking a question!" That character is 吗 (pronounced "ma").

Here's the clever part: someone invented 吗 by combining two smart ideas. They took 口, which shows a mouth speaking, and added 马, which is the word for "horse"—and it just happens to sound like "ma." When you put them together, you get 吗, a question marker that sounds natural.

This is how Chinese characters teach you about language! The character builds like this:

$$\underbrace{\text{口}}_{\text{mouth}} + \underbrace{\text{马}}_{\text{sounds like "ma"}} = \underbrace{\text{吗}}_{\text{question particle}}$$

Once you learn this pattern, you'll see it show up again and again in Chinese. The mouth part and the horse-sound part are building blocks that inventors reused for many different characters.

**Now you try**: When you ask a question in your own language, how do you show it? With your voice? With your face? What's your signal that you're asking, not telling?

### 字 (12.7s)

Here's how this one is built:

### 沐 (31.6s)

### phonetic_borrowing (12.3s)

## Phonetic Borrowing

Picture this: You have a favorite toy you know really well. Its name sounds like "ring-ring." One day, a friend asks you to help them remember a new toy that also sounds like "ring-ring." So you say, "Just think of your toy—they sound the same!" That's what Chinese characters do with their sounds.

In Chinese writing, some characters are *sound-borrowers*. They steal a sound from an older, familiar character and use it to help spell a new word. The borrowed sound does the heavy lifting—it tells you how to say the new character.

Look at 木 (tree). It has its own sound. Later, someone wanted to write 沐 (to bathe), and they borrowed 木's sound for this new character. Same sound, new job. Or take 马 (horse)—its sound appears in 妈 (mother) and 吗 (a question word), like a sound working three different jobs.

The pattern looks like this:

$$\underbrace{木}_{\text{borrowed sound}} + \underbrace{\text{water idea}}_{\text{meaning hint}} = 沐 \text{ (bathe)}$$

It's a clever shortcut: instead of inventing a completely new way to show the sound, writers grabbed an old friend and gave it a second life.

**Now you try**: Say 马 out loud. Can you hear that same sound hiding inside 妈 and 吗? That's phonetic borrowing at work.

### phono_semantic_principle (25.5s)

## Phono Semantic Principle

**Picture this:** You're playing with LEGO, and you find a genius shortcut. Instead of building everything from scratch, you snap together two special pieces: one that tells you *what something is* and one that sounds like *how to say it*. Suddenly you can build almost anything.

Chinese characters use this exact trick.

Long ago, writers realized: why draw a whole picture every time? They mixed two clever pieces together:
- A **meaning piece** — a tiny symbol that hints at the *topic* or *type*
- A **sound piece** — a piece that *rhymes with* or *sounds like* how you say the word

Picture water rippling: the character for "river" puts the water-meaning piece next to a sound-piece that rhymes with the word. Now you've built a new character! The water-piece tells you "this is about water," and the sound-piece helps you pronounce it.

The magic: Learn roughly 200 basic pieces, and you unlock thousands of characters. You become a character detective — each piece is a clue.

$$\underbrace{\text{meaning piece}}_{\text{the topic}} + \underbrace{\text{sound piece}}_{\text{the sound}} = \text{new character}$$

**Now you try:** You see a character with the tree-piece inside it. What *kind* of thing is it likely about? What could you guess?

### reading_unseen_characters (9.4s)

## Reading Unseen Characters

**The superpower you didn't know you had**

Imagine walking through a market and seeing a word you've never met. Instead of feeling stuck, you notice something magical: the character is built from *smaller pieces you already know*. Like finding LEGO bricks inside a box—you can't see the whole picture yet, but you recognize the blocks.

When you meet a new character, pause and look for the bricks:

$$\text{left piece} + \text{right piece} = \text{new character}$$

The left piece often hints at *meaning* ("water," "wood," "person"). The right piece often hints at *sound*—it rhymes with words you already know.

**Now you try:** Think of a character as a puzzle box. If the box has a "water" brick, what kinds of things might live inside? If it has a "person" brick? What would you guess before looking it up?
