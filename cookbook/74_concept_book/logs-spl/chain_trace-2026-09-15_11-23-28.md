### 木 (6.0s)

## 木

Picture a tree standing in a park: one straight trunk, with branches spreading out to the sides. That's exactly what the character 木 draws — a horizontal line for the branches crossing a tall line for the trunk, with two small strokes at the bottom like roots digging in.

Once you can see the tree in 木, something fun happens: this little tree-brick gets used in two very different jobs depending on which character it joins.

Sometimes it brings its MEANING — trees, wood, plants. Put two trees side by side and you get 林, a forest! Add 木 next to a person resting against it, and you get 休, "to rest" — because who doesn't like resting under a tree's shade?

But sometimes 木 is only borrowed for its SOUND, "mu," like in 沐 (to bathe) — no trees involved at all!

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林 \text{ (forest, meaning)}$$

Now you try: find 木 hiding inside another character. Is it lending meaning, or just sound?

### 女 (6.4s)

## 女

Imagine an old drawing of someone kneeling gently, hands folded, head bowed — a quiet, graceful pose. That's the picture behind 女, one of the oldest characters for "woman." Long ago, people didn't write with letters like A-B-C; they drew little pictures of what they saw, and this one captured a person sitting calmly, at rest.

Here's why that feels right: think about how many characters describing family, care, and relationships seem to grow out of this same kneeling shape. That's because 女 became a building block — a seed — for other ideas connected to women, mothers, and family bonds. When character-makers needed to show something related to these ideas, they often reached for 女 and added another piece beside it.

We won't build a full character yet — just remember this shape:

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{kneeling woman}}
$$

Now you try: look at 女 and trace its curves with your finger. Can you see the bowed head and folded knees? Try sketching it from memory — no need to be perfect, just notice the pose.

### 人 (8.2s)

## 人

Imagine you are looking down from the sky at someone walking. You would see two legs, spread apart, taking a step. That is exactly what the character 人 draws — a simple stick figure of a person, caught mid-stride, one leg forward and one leg back like a little triangle without a top.

Long ago, people who invented Chinese writing needed a quick way to draw "person" without drawing a whole body with arms and a head. They kept only the legs, because legs are what make a person different from a rock or a tree — a person moves. So 人 became the picture of walking itself.

Here's why this matters: 人 is not just its own word for "person." It becomes a building block inside many other characters, standing quietly on the side to whisper "this is about a person" — like in 你 (you) or 他 (he).

Now you try: Stand up and take one big step. Freeze. Do your legs look like 人?

### 子 (5.8s)

## 子

Imagine a tiny bundle wrapped snugly in a blanket, with just a little head peeking out. That's exactly what the character 子 pictures — a swaddled baby! Look closely: the top part is the round little head, and the bottom stroke is the baby all wrapped up, arms tucked in.

Why does this feel right? Babies really do look like little bundles when they're carried — cozy, small, and protected. The ancient people who invented this character were drawing what they saw.

But 子 grew up to do more than mean "child." It became a helper that turns other words into naming-words — a bit like adding a friendly little tag at the end. Think of it as saying "the small round thing," "the wrapped-up thing" — this habit spread until 子 started riding along on all sorts of everyday nouns, like 桌子 (table) or 帽子 (hat), even though tables and hats aren't babies at all!

**Now you try:** Draw a tiny head with a wrapped body underneath. Can you see the baby hiding inside 子?

### 宀 (5.4s)

## 宀

Imagine a tiny house drawn with just two strokes — a dot on top like a chimney, and a wide sweeping line below like the roof sloping down on both sides. That's 宀. It never stands alone as a word you'd say out loud. Instead, it sits on top of other characters like a real roof sits on top of a real house, sheltering whatever is underneath.

Think about what a roof actually does: it doesn't just sit there — it protects, it gathers, it makes a space feel safe and settled. So whenever you see 宀 perched over another character, ask yourself: what is being sheltered here? A person under a roof feels safe. A pig under a roof becomes a home. A roof turns open space into "inside."

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{豕}_{\text{pig}} = 家 \text{ (home, family)}$$

**Now you try:** Next time you spot 宀 at the top of a character, cover up the roof with your finger and guess — what's hiding underneath, waiting to be sheltered?

### 马 (8.9s)

## 马

Picture a little horse, galloping along with its legs and tail flying behind it — that's what 马 (mǎ) looked like a long, long time ago, before it got simplified into the neat little shape we see today. It's one of the oldest picture-words in Chinese, and it truly does mean "horse."

But here's a fun twist: sometimes 马 stops being a horse at all! When it hops inside another character, it can just lend its *sound* — "ma" — without bringing any horse-meaning along. It's like a costume that keeps its label but changes its job.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{马}_{sound: ma} = 妈 \ (mother)
$$

Now you try: can you think of a costume — a word part — that keeps its *sound* but changes its *job* when it joins other words?

### 从 (6.6s)

## 从

Picture two friends walking to school. One walks in front, and the other walks right behind, matching every step. That second person is *following* — and that is exactly the idea behind 从 (cóng).

Look at the character itself: it is simply one 人 (person) drawn behind another 人 (person). No hidden symbols, no extra strokes — just a little picture of someone trailing another. That is why 从 means "to follow": the shape tells the story.

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person, behind}} = \underbrace{从}_{\text{follow}}$$

Once you see it, you can't unsee it — 从 is a tiny comic strip of two people, one chasing after the other.

**Now you try:** Draw two stick figures, one behind the other, both walking the same direction. Then write 从 next to your drawing. Can you think of a time you followed someone — a friend, a sibling, a parent? That's 从 in real life.

### 口 (5.5s)

## 口

Imagine you draw a little square. Not a full box — just an open shape, like a window with no glass. That's 口 (kǒu), and it means *mouth*.

Think about your own mouth. It's an opening in your face — a little gap where words come out and food goes in. The character copies that shape almost exactly: four strokes making a small square, open just enough to feel like a hole you could peek through.

口 is one of the oldest and simplest Chinese characters — it just *looks* like the thing it means. No hidden pieces, no story of combination, just a picture of an opening.

And 口 does everything a mouth does. It speaks — many characters about talking or shouting contain 口. It eats — characters about food or biting use it too. It even marks a question, the way your mouth opens when you ask "why?"

**Now you try:** Draw a small square on paper. Now imagine it's your mouth, opening to say your name out loud. That open square *is* 口.

### 日 (6.0s)

## 日

Look up at the sky. What shape do you see up there, glowing bright and round? A circle! Long ago, people in China wanted to draw a picture-word for the sun, so they started with that same round shape.

But here's the fun part: brushes and bamboo strips don't love perfect circles. Try drawing a circle with a paintbrush — it wants to wiggle into straighter lines! So over time, the round sun got squared off into a box. And inside that box, someone added a small mark, like a tiny glowing dot — a way of saying "this circle isn't just any shape, it's shining."

That squared-off circle-with-a-dot became 日 (rì), the character for "sun." It's one of the oldest and simplest characters — no smaller pieces build it, because it IS a piece. It's a foundation stone. Many characters about brightness, day, and time grow out of 日, the way sunlight itself starts every day.

**Now you try:** Draw a circle, then square its edges like a brush would. Add a dot inside. You've just drawn 日!

### 月 (6.3s)

## 月

Look up on a clear night and find a curved sliver of light — not a full circle, but a bent shape like a sliced-open orange peel. That crescent shape became 月, the character for moon. Ancient writers traced its bowed edges and the two little lines inside once stood for shadows or craters.

Here is the strange part: this same shape shows up in words that have nothing to do with the sky — 肝 (liver), 胃 (stomach), 脸 (face). Why would "moon" hide inside body parts? It doesn't, really. Long ago, a different character meaning "flesh" (肉) was drawn in a way that looked almost identical to the moon crescent. Over centuries of quick brush strokes, the two shapes merged into one. So 月 leads a double life: sometimes it means moon, sometimes it silently whispers "this is a body part."

$$\underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} \quad \text{and} \quad \underbrace{月}_{\text{flesh, in body words}}$$

Now you try: find three characters with 月 inside. Which one glows in the night sky, and which ones are hiding inside you?

### 林 (6.0s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing alone on a hill. Now imagine a second tree right next to it. Just two trees together already feel different from one tree — thicker, shadier, a little bit like a small world of their own. That's the idea behind 林 (lín), which means "woods."

Here's the trick: 林 is simply the character for tree, 木, written twice, side by side.

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林 \text{ (woods)}$$

This is one of the oldest and simplest rules for building Chinese characters: repeat a picture, and you multiply its meaning. One tree is just a tree. Two trees together become something new — a place, not just a thing. It's like saying a word twice for emphasis: "far, far away" feels bigger than "far." Two 木 side by side feels the same way — bigger than one tree, but still small compared to a whole forest.

Now you try: if one 木 is a tree, and two 木 make 林 (woods), what do you think three 木 stacked together might mean?

### 水 (7.0s)

## 水

Imagine standing by a river long, long ago, before anyone had invented writing. You want to draw "water" so a friend far away will understand. What do you draw? Not a perfect circle or a straight line — water never sits still! You draw the middle of the river rushing forward, and little splashes and eddies swirling out on each side.

That is exactly what 水 looks like: a central stroke like a current, with drops curling away from it. It captures the *feeling* of water — never one shape, always moving, always branching.

Here's something clever: when 水 sits inside other characters, it shrinks down to just three drops, written as 氵, placed on the left side. Think of it like water's "nickname" — a compact signal that says "something wet or flowing is happening here," even before you read the rest of the character.

$$
\underbrace{水}_{\text{water, standing alone}} \xrightarrow{\text{shrinks \& moves left}} \underbrace{氵}_{\text{three drops radical}}
$$

**Now you try:** Look for 氵 hiding on the left of other characters — can you guess which ones might mean "river," "ocean," or "swim"?

### 休 (7.8s)

## 休

Imagine you've been walking all day. Your legs are tired. What's the first thing you want to do? You spot a big shady tree — and you lean right against it. Ahhhh. That's rest.

Now look at the character 休. Do you see the little person standing there? And right next to them, a tree? The ancient writers who made this character had the exact same idea you just had — a tired person leaning on a tree trunk. They didn't need to explain it in words; they just drew the picture, and anyone looking at it could feel the tiredness melt away.

That's the magic of this character: two simple pictures, snapped together, become one bigger idea.

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \text{休 (rest)}$$

**Now you try:** Close your eyes and picture yourself leaning against a tree after a long walk. Can you feel why the person + the tree together mean "rest"? Try drawing 休 yourself, and imagine you're drawing that little nap.

### 众 (7.0s)

## 众

Imagine you're standing alone in an empty playground. Now two friends run in. Then more and more keep arriving, until you can't count them anymore — that's a crowd!

You already know that one person is written as 人, standing like two legs. What happens when a lot of people gather together? Chinese writing has a fun trick: to show "a lot" of something, you sometimes just repeat the picture three times, like you saw with 森 (forest), made of three trees. For people, the same trick applies! Take three 人 and stack them together, and you get 众 — a crowd, a multitude, everybody all at once.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = \underbrace{众}_{\text{crowd}}
$$

It's like three stick-figure friends huddling up — one becomes many, and many becomes a crowd.

**Now you try:** Can you draw three tiny 人 shapes squeezed together like a group hug? That's exactly what 众 looks like!

### 吗 (6.1s)

## 吗

Imagine you say "You like candy" to a friend. Now imagine you say the exact same words, but you're really asking them — you want to know if it's true. In English, your voice curls up at the end to show it's a question. In Chinese, there's an even simpler trick: you just add one tiny word at the end.

That word is 吗 (ma). Drop it onto any plain statement, and — like magic — it becomes a question. No word order to flip, no extra verb to sneak in. Just: statement + 吗 = question.

Look closely at 吗 itself. The left piece, 口, is a little mouth — it shows up in characters about speaking and sound. The right piece, 马, means "horse," but here it's only lending its sound: "ma." Put them together, and "mouth says ma" becomes the question word "ma."

$$
\underbrace{口}_{\text{mouth, speaking}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: ma}} = 吗 \text{ (question word "ma")}
$$

Now you try: take "You are happy" and turn it into a question by adding 吗 at the end. What do you notice changed — and what stayed exactly the same?

### 好 (5.3s)

## 好

Imagine peeking into an old, old house — three thousand years old — through a tiny window shaped like a picture. What would make someone, long ago, say "yes, this is good"? They didn't draw a smiley face or a thumbs-up. They drew a mother and her child, sitting close together.

That's the secret of 好 (hǎo). It's built from two smaller pictures: a woman, 女, and a child, 子. Put them side by side, and you get "good."

$$
\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = \underbrace{好}_{good}
$$

Why does this feel right? Because to the people who first drew it, nothing seemed more peaceful, more complete, or more full of promise than a mother and child together. A family safe and whole — that was their picture of goodness itself.

**Now you try:** Cover the character 好 with your hand. Can you rebuild it from memory using just 女 and 子? Then think of your own "good" moment — who is with you when things feel right?

### 妈 (6.7s)

## 妈

Imagine you're building a word like it's made of LEGO blocks. You want to say "mother" — someone who takes care of you, who is a woman. So you grab the piece that means "female person": 女. But 女 alone just means "woman" in general — not specifically your mom.

So you need a second clue, a little sound-tag stuck on the side, to say "this word is pronounced *ma*." You borrow the piece 马 — not because mothers have anything to do with horses, but because 马 already sounds like "ma," and Chinese writers love recycling sounds instead of inventing brand-new symbols every time.

Put the meaning-piece and the sound-piece together, and you get 妈 — mother.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{female person}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sounds like ``ma''}} = \text{妈 (mother)}$$

**Now you try:** Cover up 马 with your finger. What's left? Right — 女, "woman." The horse just lent its voice, not its meaning!

### 字 (7.7s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny child, tucked safely under the roof of a house. That is exactly the picture inside this character: a roof on top, and a child underneath it, sheltered and growing.

Why does this feel right? Because that is exactly what a character is — something raised and cared for, like a child at home, until it grows into a full member of the writing family. Every new character starts small, made from older, simpler pieces, and grows up under the "roof" of the rules that hold the whole system together.

Here is how the two pieces come together:

$$\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = 字$$

A roof plus a child makes "character" — a small thing being raised into something whole.

This is such an important idea that the whole family of Chinese characters can be studied as a network of characters "raising" each other — called ZiNets, named after this very picture.

Now you try: think of one character you know. Can you find its "roof" and its "child" — the pieces that raised it?

### 安 (7.3s)

## 安

Imagine a little girl comes home after playing outside all day. The sun is setting, and she's a little tired. She walks through her door, and right away, everything feels different — quiet, warm, familiar. That feeling? That's what this character is all about.

Look at the shape: there's a roof on top, like a little house covering everything. Underneath the roof sits a woman. When someone has a safe roof over their head and a home to return to, they feel calm inside. Nothing to worry about. Nothing to fear. That's the idea of "peace" — not loud celebration, but the quiet comfort of being somewhere you belong.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = \underbrace{安}_{\text{peace, safe, settled}}$$

You can see this same feeling in words like 平安 (safe and sound) and 安全 (safety) — both carry that same "protected under a roof" feeling.

Now you try: think of your own safest, coziest spot. Can you picture a roof over it, keeping you calm inside?

### 明 (7.4s)

## 明

Imagine it's nighttime, and you're scared of the dark. Then someone turns on both a lamp AND opens the curtains so moonlight pours in too. Suddenly the room isn't just lit — it's glowing, easy to see every corner of.

That's the feeling behind 明 (míng). It means bright, clear, shining — but not just any brightness. It's the biggest brightness you can imagine, because it's made from two lights put together.

Think about the sky: the sun 日 lights up the day, and the moon 月 lights up the night. What if you took both of them and placed them side by side, shining at the same time? You'd have double the light — nothing could stay hidden or dark. That's exactly what this character pictures.

$$
\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} = \underbrace{明}_{\text{bright}}
$$

Now you try: Can you think of two things that, put together, make something even brighter or clearer than either one alone — like two flashlights, or two friends explaining something together?

### 森 (5.8s)

## 森

Imagine one tree standing alone in a field. Now imagine two trees close together — that's already a little grove, 林. But what happens when the trees keep growing, thick and close, as far as you can see? You get a forest — deep, shadowy, a little mysterious. That's 森.

Notice something wonderful: the character doesn't just *say* forest, it *shows* it. One tree, 木, is a single brick. Stack two bricks and you get a small woods, 林. Stack a third brick on top and the meaning grows right along with the picture — now it's not just "more trees," it's a whole forest, dense enough to get lost in.

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \ (forest)$$

The number of trees drawn literally matches how much "tree-ness" the word means. Two feels cozy. Three feels vast.

**Now you try:** Draw one tree, then two, then three. Say out loud what each drawing feels like — lonely, friendly, or lost-in-the-woods!

### 沐 (5.8s)

## 沐

Imagine you're washing your hands. Your mom says "mù shǒu" — wash hands. But how would you *write* that "mù" sound if there were no character for "wash" yet?

Old writers had a clever trick. They took 水 (water) to carry the *meaning* — because washing is always about water. Then they needed a *sound*. They picked 木 (mù, meaning "tree") — not because washing has anything to do with trees, but simply because 木 already sounded like "mù"! So 木 just lent its voice, like borrowing a friend's name tag because it happens to match yours.

$$\underbrace{水}_{meaning: water} + \underbrace{木}_{sound: mù} = 沐 \text{ (to wash)}$$

This is a brand new trick in our book: one piece draws the *picture*, the other piece only whispers the *sound*.

**Now you try**: Cover the character and just remember — water for meaning, tree for sound. Can you say why 木 is there even though washing has nothing to do with trees?

### phonetic_borrowing (10.0s)

## Phonetic Borrowing

Imagine you already know how to draw a tree, 木, and you know it sounds like "mù." One day someone needs a character that means "to wash" but also sounds like "mù." Instead of inventing a brand-new picture, they borrow your tree! They just add three little water drops beside it, and — ta-da — 沐 (mù, "to wash") is born. The tree isn't doing tree-work anymore. It's doing sound-work.

This is the trick behind so many Chinese characters. A piece that started as a picture of one thing gets a second job: lending its sound to a totally different meaning. Take 马 (mǎ, "horse"). Add a mouth radical 口, and you get 吗 (ma), the little word that turns a sentence into a question.

$$\underbrace{马}_{sound: mǎ} + \underbrace{口}_{mouth} = 吗 \quad (question word)$$

**Now you try:** Next time you spot 马 hiding inside a character, listen for its "mǎ" echo — what other job might it be moonlighting for?

### semantic_composition (9.8s)

## Semantic Composition

Imagine you want to draw the word "rest." You cannot draw a feeling, but you can draw a story: a person, tired, leans against a tree. Two simple pictures, put side by side, tell a whole idea that neither picture could tell alone.

This is the meaning-stacking trick — combine pictures whose meanings add up to something new. A person next to a tree becomes rest.

$$
\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 休 \text{ (rest)}
$$

Once you know this trick, you start seeing it everywhere. The sun next to the moon becomes bright, because together they give more light than either alone. A woman next to a child becomes good. Three trees together become forest, and three people together become crowd. Even the idea of "character" itself is built this way: a roof over a child, meaning a new sign being raised, sheltered, and taught.

Now you try: what two simple pictures might combine to mean "peace"? (Hint: think of a woman, safe, under a roof.)

### phono_semantic_principle (5.7s)

## Phono Semantic Principle

Imagine you're building with LEGO, and you discover a magic trick: snap together just two special bricks — one that whispers the *meaning*, one that whispers the *sound* — and you can build almost any character you want. That's the secret behind more than 80% of all Chinese characters!

Here's why it feels right: one piece is like a picture clue (water, tree, heart, sun) telling you the *category* the word belongs to. The other piece isn't a picture at all — it's borrowed just for its *sound*, like a nickname that rhymes. Put them together, and you get a brand-new character whose meaning you can guess and whose sound you can almost say — even if you've never seen it before!

$$\underbrace{\text{氵}}_{\text{water (meaning)}} + \underbrace{\text{青}}_{\text{qīng (sound)}} = \text{清 (qīng, "clear")}$$

Learn about 200 meaning-and-sound bricks, master this one trick, and thousands of characters unlock themselves.

**Now you try:** The water brick 氵 plus a sound brick meaning "sheep" gives 洋 (yáng, "ocean"). Can you guess why water shows up again?

### reading_unseen_characters (11.1s)

## Reading Unseen Characters

Imagine you're hiking and spot a plant you've never seen. You don't panic — you notice it has thorns like a rose and berries like a currant, so you guess: "probably prickly, probably edible-looking." You're detecting a new character the same way!

Every Chinese character is built from smaller, familiar bricks. One brick often whispers the *meaning* (a category clue), another often whispers the *sound* (a pronunciation clue). When you meet a stranger character, you don't need a dictionary first — you look at its bricks and make an educated guess, like a detective.

Take a character built like this:

$$\underbrace{氵}_{\text{water}} + \underbrace{青}_{\text{sound: qīng}} = 清$$

The water brick says "this is probably about liquid, clean, clear." The right-hand brick tells you it probably sounds like "qīng." Put together — clear water! You guessed both meaning and sound before ever checking a dictionary.

**Now you try:** Find a character with 木 (tree) on one side. What might it mean? What might the other half tell you about its sound?
