### electric_charge (1.0s)

## Electric Charge

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter that comes in two types, conventionally labeled positive and negative. Protons carry positive charge, electrons carry negative charge, and neutrons carry none. Charge determines how particles interact electromagnetically: like charges repel, opposite charges attract. Charge is quantized — it exists only in whole-number multiples of the elementary charge, $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ coulombs — and it is conserved in every physical process, meaning the total charge of an isolated system never changes, even during chemical reactions, nuclear decay, or particle collisions.

An atom is electrically neutral when its number of protons equals its number of electrons. Ions form when atoms gain or lose electrons: a sodium atom (Na) loses one electron to become Na⁺, while a chlorine atom (Cl) gains one electron to become Cl⁻. This transfer of charge is what drives ionic bonding — the attraction between Na⁺ and Cl⁻ is a direct electromagnetic consequence of their opposite charges, and it explains why table salt (NaCl) holds together as a rigid crystal lattice. Because charge is conserved, the electron sodium loses is exactly the electron chlorine gains — nothing is created or destroyed, only transferred from one atom to the other.

To apply this concept in problem-solving: when analyzing a chemical reaction, always check that total charge balances on both sides of the equation, just as mass must balance — the sum of charges among reactants must equal the sum among products. When predicting whether a particular ion will form and remain stable, ask which arrangement of gained or lost electrons gives the atom a full outer electron shell, since that configuration minimizes the system's overall energy; sodium loses exactly one electron (not two) and chlorine gains exactly one electron (not two) because those specific transfers reach a stable, low-energy configuration. Electric charge — and the ions it produces — is the reason atoms bond into compounds, salts dissolve and conduct electricity in solution, and biological membranes maintain the voltage gradients that let neurons fire.

### velocity (1.1s)

## Velocity

Velocity is the instantaneous rate of change of an object's position with respect to time, formally defined as the derivative of the position function:

$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{x(t + \Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t}$$

Unlike average velocity, which measures displacement over a finite time interval, instantaneous velocity captures the object's motion at a single moment. This distinction matters because average velocity can mask important behavior — an object might average zero velocity over a trip while moving quickly in both directions. The derivative eliminates this ambiguity by shrinking the interval to zero, isolating the object's behavior at exactly one instant.

**Worked example.** Suppose a particle moves along a line with position function $x(t) = 3t^2 - 2t + 1$ (meters, with $t$ in seconds). To find velocity, differentiate:

$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = 6t - 2$$

At $t = 2$ seconds, $v(2) = 6(2) - 2 = 10$ m/s. Note that $v(t)$ is itself a function of time — the particle's velocity changes continuously as $t$ changes, which is precisely what differentiation captures that a single average-velocity calculation cannot.

**Problem-solving application.** Velocity analysis becomes essential when position is nonlinear, since the rate of change itself varies. Consider $x(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t$. Setting $v(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9 = 0$ and solving gives $t = 1$ and $t = 3$ — the instants when the particle momentarily stops before reversing direction. This technique, finding critical points of velocity, underlies motion analysis in physics (projectile turning points), economics (marginal rate transitions), and any application where identifying moments of zero rate of change reveals structural behavior in a system. Mastering the derivative relationship between position and velocity is the foundation for later extending to acceleration, $a(t) = dv/dt$, and for solving kinematics problems where velocity, not position, is the given or sought quantity.

### magnetic_field (14.1s)

## Magnetic Field

### Definition

A **magnetic field** $\mathbf{B}$ is a vector field: at every point in space it assigns a vector describing the magnetic influence there. We define it operationally through the force on a moving charge. A charge $q$ moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ through a field $\mathbf{B}$ experiences

$$\mathbf{F} = q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B},$$

with magnitude $F = |q|\,v\,B\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. The direction follows the right-hand rule (reverse it for negative $q$). The SI unit is the tesla: $1\,\text{T} = 1\,\text{N}/(\text{C}\cdot\text{m/s})$.

Three consequences follow directly from the cross product:

- No force acts on a charge at rest ($v=0$) or one moving parallel to $\mathbf{B}$ ($\theta=0$).
- The force is always perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, so $\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$. A magnetic force does **no work** and changes a particle's direction, never its speed.
- Magnetic field lines form closed loops. Mathematically, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0$, because no isolated magnetic poles have been observed.

### Worked example

A proton ($q=1.60\times10^{-19}$ C, $m=1.67\times10^{-27}$ kg) moves at $v=3.0\times10^{6}$ m/s perpendicular to a uniform field of $B=0.50$ T. Find the force and the path.

**Force:** since $\theta=90^\circ$,

$$F = qvB = (1.60\times10^{-19})(3.0\times10^{6})(0.50)=2.4\times10^{-13}\ \text{N}.$$

**Path:** the force is perpendicular to the velocity and has constant magnitude, so it acts as a centripetal force and the proton moves in a circle. Setting $qvB = mv^2/r$ gives

$$r=\frac{mv}{qB}=\frac{(1.67\times10^{-27})(3.0\times10^{6})}{(1.60\times10^{-19})(0.50)}\approx 6.3\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}.$$

The period $T = 2\pi r/v = 2\pi m/(qB)$ does not depend on speed. This property is the basis of the cyclotron.

### Problem-solving application

**Mass spectrometer.** Ions of charge $q$ are accelerated through a potential difference $V$, so $\tfrac12 mv^2 = qV$. They then enter a field $B$ perpendicular to their motion and curve with radius $r = mv/(qB)$. Eliminating $v$:

$$m=\frac{qB^2r^2}{2V}.$$

Measuring $r$, the distance at which ions strike a detector, therefore determines the mass.

*Practice:* singly charged ions are accelerated through $V=1000$ V and enter $B=0.20$ T. An ion lands with $r=0.102$ m. Then $m = (1.60\times10^{-19})(0.04)(0.0104)/(2000)\approx 3.3\times10^{-26}$ kg, about 20 atomic mass units. This is consistent with neon-20.

**General strategy:**

1. Sketch $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, and find the direction of $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$. Flip it if $q<0$.
2. Decompose $\mathbf{v}$ into components parallel and perpendicular to $\mathbf{B}$. The perpendicular part drives circular motion and the parallel part is unchanged. If both are present, the path is a helix.
3. Apply $qv_\perp B = mv_\perp^2/r$ and solve for the unknown.

### electric_field (0.9s)

## Electric Field

### Definition

The **electric field** at a point in space is the electric force that would act on a positive test charge placed there, divided by that test charge:

$$\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q}, \qquad [E] = \text{N/C} = \text{V/m}.$$

Dividing by $q$ removes the test charge from the description. What remains depends only on the source charges and on the position of the point. The field is a property of space around the sources, whether or not a test charge is present. $\vec{E}$ is a vector, and it points in the direction a positive charge would be pushed.

For a point source $Q$, Coulomb's law gives the force on a test charge $q$ at distance $r$. Dividing by $q$ gives

$$E = \frac{k|Q|}{r^2}, \qquad k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2.$$

The field points away from $Q$ if $Q>0$ and toward $Q$ if $Q<0$. It falls off as $1/r^2$.

**Superposition.** With several sources, the net field is the vector sum of each source's field:

$$\vec{E}_{\text{net}} = \sum_i \vec{E}_i.$$

### Worked example

A point charge $Q = +3.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at the origin. Find the field at $r = 0.30\ \text{m}$ along the $+x$ axis, then find the force on an electron placed there.

$$E = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(3.0\times10^{-9})}{(0.30)^2} = 300\ \text{N/C},$$

directed along $+x$, away from the positive charge.

The force on the electron ($q=-1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) uses $\vec{F}=q\vec{E}$:

$$\vec{F} = (-1.60\times10^{-19})(300\,\hat{x}) = -4.8\times10^{-17}\,\hat{x}\ \text{N}.$$

The negative sign shows the force points opposite the field, toward the source. A negative charge is pulled against $\vec{E}$.

### Problem-solving application

**Two charges.** Place $+2.0\ \text{nC}$ at $x=0$ and $-2.0\ \text{nC}$ at $x=0.20\ \text{m}$. Find $\vec{E}$ at the midpoint, $x = 0.10\ \text{m}$.

1. Each charge is $0.10\ \text{m}$ from the midpoint, so each field has magnitude
$$E_i = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(2.0\times10^{-9})}{(0.10)^2} \approx 1800\ \text{N/C}.$$
2. The positive charge pushes a test charge away from itself, in the $+x$ direction. The negative charge pulls it toward itself, which is also the $+x$ direction.
3. The two fields point the same way, so they add:
$$\vec{E}_{\text{net}} \approx 3600\,\hat{x}\ \text{N/C}.$$

**Strategy for any problem:**
- Sketch the sources and the field point.
- For each source, find the magnitude $k|Q|/r^2$ and the direction (away from $+$, toward $-$).
- Resolve into components, then add the components. Do not add magnitudes unless the vectors are collinear.
- To get a force, compute $\vec{F}=q\vec{E}$. Check that its direction is along $\vec{E}$ for $q>0$ and opposite for $q<0$.

**Check your answer.** Far from a collection of charges with net charge $Q_{\text{net}}$, the field should look like that of a single point charge $Q_{\text{net}}$. At the midpoint above, the field is strong because the two charges are close and their fields reinforce. Far from a net-neutral pair, the fields nearly cancel, and the result falls off faster than $1/r^2$.

### lorentz_force (12.9s)

## Lorentz Force

A charged particle moving through a magnetic field feels a force that depends on its charge, its speed, and the field's strength and direction. This is the **magnetic part of the Lorentz force**. For a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ in a field $\vec B$:

$$\vec F = q\,\vec v \times \vec B, \qquad F = |q|\,v\,B\sin\theta$$

Here $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$. The force vanishes when the charge moves parallel to the field ($\theta = 0^\circ$) and is largest when it moves perpendicular to it ($\theta = 90^\circ$).

**Direction.** The force is perpendicular to both $\vec v$ and $\vec B$. Use the right-hand rule: point your fingers along $\vec v$, curl them toward $\vec B$, and your thumb gives the direction of $\vec F$ for a positive charge. For a negative charge, reverse it.

**Consequence: no work is done.** Since $\vec F \perp \vec v$, the power is $\vec F\cdot\vec v = 0$. A magnetic force changes the particle's direction but never its speed or kinetic energy.

### Worked example

A proton ($q = 1.60\times10^{-19}$ C) moves at $v = 3.0\times10^{6}$ m/s at $30^\circ$ to a field of $B = 0.50$ T. Find the force magnitude.

$$F = qvB\sin\theta = (1.60\times10^{-19})(3.0\times10^{6})(0.50)\sin 30^\circ = 1.2\times10^{-13}\ \text{N}$$

Although tiny in newtons, this is huge for a proton, whose mass is only $1.67\times10^{-27}$ kg. Its acceleration is about $7\times10^{13}\ \text{m/s}^2$.

### Problem-solving application: circular motion

When $\vec v \perp \vec B$, the force has constant magnitude $qvB$ and always points sideways to the motion. This is exactly the condition for uniform circular motion, with the magnetic force supplying the centripetal force:

$$qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{mv}{qB}$$

The period is

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{qB},$$

which is independent of speed. This is the principle behind the cyclotron and the mass spectrometer.

**Problem.** An electron ($m = 9.11\times10^{-31}$ kg) enters a $2.0\times10^{-3}$ T field perpendicular to it at $4.0\times10^{7}$ m/s. Find the radius of its path.

$$r = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{(9.11\times10^{-31})(4.0\times10^{7})}{(1.60\times10^{-19})(2.0\times10^{-3})} \approx 0.11\ \text{m}$$

**Strategy summary.**
1. Identify $q$, $v$, $B$, and the angle between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$.
2. Compute the magnitude with $F = |q|vB\sin\theta$.
3. Find the direction with the right-hand rule, and flip it for negative charges.
4. If $\vec v\perp\vec B$, set $F = ma_c$ to get the radius and period.
5. If $\vec v$ has a component parallel to $\vec B$, that component is unaffected, so the path is a helix.

### cathode_ray_tube (14.7s)

## Cathode Ray Tube

A **cathode ray tube (CRT)** is an evacuated glass tube in which an *electron gun* produces a beam of electrons, accelerates it with a high voltage, and steers it onto a phosphor-coated screen. Where the beam strikes, the phosphor glows. Sweeping the beam across the screen rapidly builds up an image.

### How it works

1. **Emission.** A heated cathode releases electrons by thermionic emission.
2. **Acceleration.** Electrons pass through a potential difference $V_a$ and gain kinetic energy. For $V_a$ well below about 50 kV, the nonrelativistic result is accurate:

$$eV_a = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV_a}{m}}$$

3. **Deflection.** Deflection plates apply an electric field, or coils apply a magnetic field, to bend the beam.
4. **Display.** The beam hits the phosphor, which converts kinetic energy into light.

**Magnetic deflection.** A beam of speed $v$ crossing a uniform field $B$ perpendicular to its velocity feels a force $F = evB$. This force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so the electrons follow a circular arc of radius

$$r = \frac{mv}{eB}.$$

**Electric deflection.** Between parallel plates, the field $E = V_d/d$ exerts a constant sideways force $eE$. This gives a parabolic path, as in projectile motion.

### Worked example

An electron gun uses $V_a = 2.0\ \text{kV}$. Find the speed, then the radius of curvature in a field $B = 1.0\ \text{mT}$.

**Speed:**
$$v=\sqrt{\frac{2(1.60\times10^{-19})(2000)}{9.11\times10^{-31}}}\approx 2.65\times10^{7}\ \text{m/s}$$

This is about 9% of the speed of light, so the nonrelativistic formula is acceptable.

**Radius:**
$$r=\frac{(9.11\times10^{-31})(2.65\times10^{7})}{(1.60\times10^{-19})(1.0\times10^{-3})}\approx 0.15\ \text{m}$$

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** In the same tube, electric deflection plates of length $L = 4.0\ \text{cm}$ are separated by $d = 1.0\ \text{cm}$ and carry $V_d = 50\ \text{V}$. Find the sideways displacement of the beam as it leaves the plates.

**Solution.** The electrons keep their forward speed $v$, so the time between the plates is $t = L/v$. The sideways acceleration is $a = eV_d/(md)$. The displacement is

$$y = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = \frac{eV_d L^2}{2mdv^2}.$$

Substitute $mv^2 = 2eV_a$ from the acceleration step:

$$y = \frac{V_d L^2}{4dV_a}$$

The charge and mass cancel, so the result depends only on voltages and geometry. Numerically,

$$y=\frac{(50)(0.040)^2}{4(0.010)(2000)}=1.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 1.0\ \text{mm}.$$

**Design insight.** Deflection is proportional to $V_d/V_a$. A higher accelerating voltage gives a brighter, faster beam, but the beam is harder to steer. Designers therefore use long plates or deflection coils placed far from the screen. A small angle at the plates then becomes a large displacement at the screen.
