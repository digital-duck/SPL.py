### cyclic_component (33.3s)

## Cyclic Component

Imagine six friends play a card-swap game. Alice gives her card to Bob, Bob passes to Carol, and Carol hands hers back to Alice — a closed loop of three. Meanwhile Dan and Eve swap directly, and Frank keeps his own card. When the music stops the game has split into three independent loops: one of length 3, one of length 2, one of length 1. Each loop is a *cyclic component*.

Why does this feel right? Whenever you rearrange a list — shuffling seats, routing packages, renaming files — you can always trace the fate of any single item. Start anywhere: follow where it goes, follow *that* destination, and keep going. You must eventually land where you started, because the list is finite and every item has exactly one destination. That closed trail is one cyclic component. Pick any unvisited item and repeat. Keep going until nothing is left unaccounted for.

The key insight is that **every permutation decomposes into disjoint cycles**. They never overlap, and together they cover every element exactly once. The permutation is completely captured by listing its cyclic components.

This is made crisp by *cycle notation*. The permutation that sends $1\mapsto 2\mapsto 3\mapsto 1$, swaps $4\leftrightarrow 5$, and fixes $6$ is written:

$$\sigma = (1\ 2\ 3)(4\ 5)(6)$$

Applying $\sigma$ twice reverses the 3-cycle, collapses the 2-cycle back to the identity, and leaves $6$ alone:

$$\sigma^2 = (1\ 3\ 2)(4\ 5)(6)$$

**Now you try.** Write $\sigma = (1\ 3\ 5\ 2)(4\ 6)$ as a two-row function table. Then compute $\sigma^4$ and identify which elements have returned to their original positions.

### sequence (17.0s)

## Sequence

Imagine you're saving money for a bicycle. Week one you have $5. Week two, $10. Week three, $15. You don't need a formula to see what's happening — your brain already feels the pattern. That feeling *is* the concept of a sequence.

A **sequence** is simply a list of numbers written in a definite order, where the position of each number matters. The first number isn't interchangeable with the second; order is everything. Think of your savings as a story: each chapter reveals the next number, and skipping a chapter breaks the narrative.

We write a sequence using subscripts to name each term by its position:

$$a_1, \; a_2, \; a_3, \; \ldots, \; a_n, \; \ldots$$

Here $a_n$ is the $n$-th term — the value at step $n$. In the savings story, $a_n = 5n$, so $a_1 = 5$, $a_2 = 10$, and so on. The rule $a_n = 5n$ is called the **general term** or **closed form** — a single expression that generates any term directly without listing all the ones before it.

Not every sequence grows so neatly. The sequence $1, -1, 1, -1, \ldots$ alternates forever and never settles. The sequence $1, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \ldots$ shrinks toward zero but never quite arrives. Each is a valid sequence — a list in order — and studying where they *head* is the next great question.

**Now you try.** Consider the sequence defined by $a_n = n^2$. Write out the first five terms. Does the gap between consecutive terms grow, shrink, or stay constant? What does that tell you about how fast the sequence is accelerating?

### trend (35.3s)

## Trend

Imagine you track your morning coffee temperature every minute after pouring it. Minute one: 85°C. Minute two: 78°C. Minute three: 71°C. The exact numbers jump around depending on the room, the mug, your measurement. But something deeper is obvious — the coffee is *cooling down*. That persistent direction hiding inside the noise is the trend.

A trend is not a fact about any single data point. It is a fact about the *whole sequence* — the underlying drift that would remain if you smoothed away every accidental wiggle. Think of it as the river current beneath choppy surface waves. Waves vary; the current flows steadily in one direction.

The simplest way to formalise this intuition is to ask: **on average, how much does the measurement change each step?** That average change is the trend. If temperatures drop roughly 7°C every minute, the trend is −7°C per minute. We call this number $b$. Positive $b$ means the sequence is rising; negative means falling; zero means no overall drift. Finding the $b$ that best fits all the points at once is a matter of calculation, but the key insight is simpler than any formula: *$b$ is the trend*. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Trends appear everywhere — stock prices, global temperature, your running pace over weeks of training. Spotting one lets you separate *signal* (where things are headed) from *noise* (the random detours along the way).

**Now you try.** A plant measures 2 cm, 5 cm, 8 cm, 11 cm over four weeks. What is the trend $b$ — how many centimetres per week does it grow on average? Does anything surprise you about how steady it is?

### noise (22.2s)

## Noise

Imagine you're trying to listen to a friend across a crowded café. Their voice is the *signal* — the information you actually want. Everything else, the clinking cups, the background music, the nearby conversations — that's *noise*. You can still understand your friend, but only because their voice is louder than the surrounding chaos.

Data works exactly the same way.

Whenever you measure something in the real world — temperature, stock prices, the time it takes a webpage to load — your measurement is never perfectly clean. Some of it reflects the true underlying pattern you care about. The rest is random interference that crept in through imperfect instruments, environmental fluctuations, or sheer chance. That interference is noise.

Here's the key intuition: **noise has no memory**. It doesn't know what yesterday's reading was. Each noisy hiccup is independent of the last. If you flip a coin to add $+1$ or $-1$ to each measurement, the coin doesn't remember its previous results. This independence is noise's defining fingerprint, and it's what separates it from genuine patterns hidden in your data.

Formally, we model a single observation as

$$y = f(x) + \varepsilon$$

where $f(x)$ is the true underlying signal and $\varepsilon$ is noise — typically drawn independently from a distribution with mean zero, such as $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. The parameter $\sigma$ controls how loud the café gets.

**Now you try.** You record a room's temperature every minute for an hour. You notice the readings jump around even when no one opens a window. List two real-world sources that could be contributing to $\varepsilon$ in your thermometer's readings. Which do you think is larger?

### seasonal_component (29.1s)

## Seasonal Component

Picture a coffee shop's weekly sales. Monday is slow, Friday buzzes, Saturday peaks, Sunday dips again. Come next week, the same rhythm repeats. That repeating, calendar-driven pulse is the seasonal component — the part of any pattern that cycles back on a predictable schedule.

**Why does this feel right?**

Human life runs on cycles: days, weeks, months, years. Ice cream sells more in July than January. Electricity spikes every morning when people shower. Retail explodes every December. These are not random fluctuations — they are *structured* repetitions baked into the calendar. Once you see that, you realise any measurement of the world carries two very different kinds of movement: long-run drift (trend) and this rhythmic heartbeat (season).

**Building the intuition**

Imagine peeling an orange. The trend is the whole orange — the general size and weight. The seasonal component is one repeating segment. If you know the segment shape, you can predict where it sits in next week's slice before you even look.

That image leads naturally to one formal idea. If your time series is $y_t$, you can write:

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

where $T_t$ is the trend you already know, $S_t$ is the seasonal component, and $R_t$ is leftover noise. This section's single new object is $S_t$ — the piece that repeats with period $m$. Everything else in the equation is a placeholder we will unpack in later sections.

**Now you try**

Take your weekly step-count for the past month. Label each day of the week. Do Saturdays look consistently higher than Tuesdays? If yes, you have just spotted a seasonal component with period $m = 7$ — without touching any algebra at all.

### time_series (13.4s)

## Time Series

Imagine you check your city's temperature every morning at 8 a.m. and write it down. After a year, you have 365 numbers. But here's what makes this interesting: the *order* matters. Tuesday's reading depends on Monday's. Winter readings cluster near freezing; summer readings cluster near scorching. If you shuffled those 365 numbers into a random order, you'd lose something real — the shape of the seasons, the memory of yesterday's cold front.

That's the heart of a time series: a sequence of measurements where *position in time* is as important as the value itself.

**Why does order matter?** Because the world has inertia. Prices don't jump randomly from $5 to $500 overnight. Your heart rate right now is close to what it was one second ago. Time-ordered data carries *autocorrelation* — the technical name for "today resembles yesterday."

Formally, a time series is a collection of observations indexed by time:

$$x_1,\ x_2,\ x_3,\ \ldots,\ x_T$$

where $x_t$ is the value at time step $t$. The gap between steps (one second, one day, one year) is called the **sampling interval**. Keep it consistent, or the math gets messy.

Two patterns to look for immediately:

- **Trend** — does the overall level drift upward or downward over time?
- **Seasonality** — does a pattern repeat on a fixed cycle (daily, weekly, yearly)?

A city's electricity demand has both: a long-term rise as the population grows, and a daily spike every evening when people come home.

**Now you try.** Take your phone's step count for the last 30 days. Sketch it roughly. Can you spot a weekly rhythm — fewer steps on weekends, perhaps? That's seasonality revealing itself, hiding in plain sight.

### trend_cycle_component (31.0s)

## Trend Cycle Component

Imagine you're tracking the price of avocados at your local market every week for five years. Some weeks prices spike because of a frost in Mexico. Every summer, prices dip a little as supply picks up. But zoom out far enough and you notice something quieter underneath all of that noise: avocados, on the whole, have been creeping upward in price year after year as global demand grows.

That slow, underlying drift is the **trend**. The medium-term waves — maybe a two-year boom-and-bust cycle driven by planting decisions — are the **cycle**. Why bundle them together? Because in practice they are almost impossible to separate cleanly. A cycle that lasts decades looks indistinguishable from a trend over any reasonable window of data. Statisticians make peace with this by treating them as one smooth signal and calling it the **trend-cycle component**: the quiet backbone of a time series once you peel away seasonal patterns and random shocks.

Formally, if $y_t$ is your observed series, the classical decomposition writes:

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

where $T_t$ is the trend-cycle, $S_t$ is the seasonal component, and $R_t$ is the residual. This equation isn't magic — it's just a promise that every observation can be explained by these three layers stacked on top of each other. The challenge, as we'll see in the next section, is figuring out how to actually pull $T_t$ out of the data.

**Now you try:** Take any monthly dataset — electricity bills, rainfall, your step count. Sketch the series on paper. Can you trace a slow, smooth shape underneath the wiggles? That shape is the trend-cycle, and you've already found it by eye.

### seasonal_trend_decomposition_loess (15.0s)

## Seasonal Trend Decomposition Loess

Imagine you run an ice-cream shop and you're watching your weekly sales figures for three years. Two things are obviously happening at once: sales spike every summer and dip every winter, *and* there's a slow upward drift as your shop grows more popular. When you look at any single week, you can't tell whether a spike is "it's July" or "business is genuinely picking up." Your eye naturally wants to peel those two effects apart.

That peeling is exactly what **STL** (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess) does. It says: every observation is the sum of three layers:

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

where $T_t$ is the **trend** (the slow drift), $S_t$ is the **seasonal** pattern (the repeating summer/winter rhythm), and $R_t$ is the **remainder** — the leftover noise after both are removed.

The clever part is *how* it estimates the trend. Instead of fitting one straight line through everything, it uses **Loess** (locally weighted smoothing): to estimate the trend at any point in time, it pays more attention to nearby weeks and less to faraway ones, fitting a small weighted line in each neighbourhood. This lets the trend bend and flex — it can accelerate, plateau, or shift direction — without you having to specify the shape in advance.

The algorithm cycles back and forth, refining the seasonal estimate, re-estimating the trend from what's left, and repeating until both stabilise. It's like tuning a guitar by ear: adjust one string, recheck the others, repeat.

**Now you try:** Take any dataset that repeats annually — monthly rainfall, electricity bills, website traffic. Sketch the three layers you'd expect by hand before running STL. Which layer would surprise you most if it weren't flat?
