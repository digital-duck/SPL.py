### independent_samples (0.0s)

## Independent Samples

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space. Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ defined on this space are *independent* if, for every pair of Borel sets $A, B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$,

$$P(X \in A,\; Y \in B) = P(X \in A)\,P(Y \in B).$$

More generally, a collection $\{X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\}$ forms an *independent sample* (or *i.i.d. sample* when each $X_i$ has the same distribution) if every finite sub-collection satisfies the analogous product rule. Independence means that knowing the outcome of any subset of variables conveys no probabilistic information about the remaining ones.

---

**Worked Example.** Roll a fair six-sided die twice. Let $X$ be the outcome of the first roll and $Y$ the second. Both are uniform on $\{1,\ldots,6\}$ with $P(X = k) = \tfrac{1}{6}$ for each $k$. Then

$$P(X = 2,\; Y = 5) = \frac{1}{36} = \frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{6} = P(X=2)\,P(Y=5).$$

This equality holds for every pair $(j,k)$, confirming independence. Contrast this with sampling *without replacement* from a finite urn: after drawing one ball the composition changes, so successive draws are dependent and the product rule fails.

---

**Key Theorem (Factorisation of the Joint Density).** If $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are continuous and independent with marginal densities $f_{X_1}, \ldots, f_{X_n}$, their joint density factors as

$$f_{X_1,\ldots,X_n}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f_{X_i}(x_i).$$

*Proof sketch.* By definition, the joint CDF factors: $F(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=\prod_i F_{X_i}(x_i)$. Differentiating with respect to each $x_i$ in turn yields the density product. $\square$

This factorisation is the engine behind likelihood-based inference: the log-likelihood of an i.i.d. sample is a *sum* $\sum_i \log f_\theta(x_i)$, which is far more tractable than a generic joint log-density.

---

### standard_error_diff_means (0.0s)

## Standard Error Diff Means

**Definition.** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n_1}$ be an independent random sample from a population with mean $\mu_1$ and variance $\sigma_1^2$, and let $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_{n_2}$ be an independent random sample from a second population with mean $\mu_2$ and variance $\sigma_2^2$. The *standard error of the difference of sample means* is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of $\bar{X} - \bar{Y}$, defined as

$$\mathrm{SE}_{\bar{X}-\bar{Y}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}.$$

When the population variances are unknown, they are replaced by the sample variances $s_1^2$ and $s_2^2$, giving the estimated standard error

$$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\bar{X}-\bar{Y}} = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}.$$

**Worked Example.** A nutritionist records the daily calcium intake (mg) for two independent groups. Group A ($n_1 = 40$) has $\bar{x} = 950$, $s_1 = 120$; Group B ($n_2 = 35$) has $\bar{y} = 870$, $s_2 = 95$. The estimated standard error of the difference $\bar{X} - \bar{Y}$ is

$$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}} = \sqrt{\frac{120^2}{40} + \frac{95^2}{35}} = \sqrt{\frac{14400}{40} + \frac{9025}{35}} = \sqrt{360 + 257.86} \approx 24.85 \text{ mg}.$$

The observed difference is $950 - 870 = 80$ mg, so the standardised test statistic is approximately $t = 80 / 24.85 \approx 3.22$, indicating a statistically significant gap.

**Key Theorem.** If both samples are drawn from normal populations, then $\bar{X} - \bar{Y}$ is exactly normally distributed. By independence and linearity of variance,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}) = \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) + \operatorname{Var}(\bar{Y}) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}.$$

Taking the square root yields $\mathrm{SE}_{\bar{X}-\bar{Y}}$. When normality is not assumed, the Central Limit Theorem guarantees that $\bar{X} - \bar{Y}$ is approximately normal for large $n_1, n_2$, preserving the formula's practical validity.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, sqrt, Rational, N

s1, s2 = 120, 95
n1, n2 = 40, 35
se = sqrt(Rational(s1**2, n1) + Rational(s2**2, n2))
print("Exact SE:", se)
print("Numeric SE:", N(se, 6))
```

Running this cell confirms $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}} \approx 24.85$ and demonstrates that SymPy preserves exact rational arithmetic before numerical evaluation, a useful habit when precision matters.

### degrees_of_freedom_two_sample (0.0s)

## Degrees Of Freedom Two Sample

**Definition.** When comparing the means of two independent populations, the *degrees of freedom* (df) quantify the amount of independent information available to estimate variability. For two samples of sizes $n_1$ and $n_2$ drawn from populations with unknown and potentially unequal variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$, the exact df is given by the **Welch–Satterthwaite approximation**:

$$\nu = \frac{\left(\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\dfrac{\left(s_1^2/n_1\right)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \dfrac{\left(s_2^2/n_2\right)^2}{n_2 - 1}}$$

where $s_1^2$ and $s_2^2$ are the sample variances. The result $\nu$ is generally non-integer and is rounded down to the nearest integer in practice. When equal variances are assumed, the pooled formula $\nu = n_1 + n_2 - 2$ applies instead.

**Worked Example.** A pharmacologist measures reaction times (in ms) for two drug conditions. Group A ($n_1 = 10$) yields $s_1^2 = 120$; Group B ($n_2 = 15$) yields $s_2^2 = 45$. Compute:

$$\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} = \frac{120}{10} = 12, \qquad \frac{s_2^2}{n_2} = \frac{45}{15} = 3$$

$$\nu = \frac{(12 + 3)^2}{\dfrac{12^2}{9} + \dfrac{3^2}{14}} = \frac{225}{16 + 0.643} \approx \frac{225}{16.643} \approx 13.52 \;\longrightarrow\; 13$$

The test statistic therefore follows a $t$-distribution with 13 degrees of freedom, not the naive $n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 23$.

**Key Theorem (Welch, 1947).** Under $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$, the statistic

$$T = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}}$$

is approximately $t_\nu$-distributed with $\nu$ given by the Welch–Satterthwaite formula. This approximation controls the Type I error rate more accurately than the pooled $t$-test whenever $\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational, floor

s1_sq, s2_sq, n1, n2 = 120, 45, 10, 15
a = s1_sq / n1
b = s2_sq / n2
nu = (a + b)**2 / (a**2 / (n1 - 1) + b**2 / (n2 - 1))
print(f"Welch df = {nu:.4f}, rounded = {int(nu)}")
```

Running this cell confirms $\nu \approx 13.52$, so critical values are read from $t_{13}$, yielding a more conservative—and more honest—inference than the equal-variance alternative.

### matched_paired_samples (0.0s)

## Matched Paired Samples

**Definition.** A *matched paired sample* (or *paired sample*) is a design in which each observation in one group is linked to exactly one observation in a second group by a deliberate matching criterion—such as the same subject measured twice, or two subjects sharing a common covariate (age, weight, baseline score). Formally, let $(X_1, Y_1), (X_2, Y_2), \ldots, (X_n, Y_n)$ be $n$ pairs. Define the within-pair difference $D_i = X_i - Y_i$. Under the paired design the analysis reduces to the one-sample problem on the differences $D_1, \ldots, D_n$, assumed i.i.d. with mean $\mu_D$ and standard deviation $\sigma_D$.

**Worked Example.** A physiologist measures resting heart rate (bpm) for ten participants *before* and *after* an eight-week aerobic training programme. Let $X_i$ denote the pre-training rate and $Y_i$ the post-training rate. The ten differences are

$$D_i = X_i - Y_i \in \{4, 7, 2, 9, 5, 6, 3, 8, 4, 7\}.$$

Then $\bar{D} = 55/10 = 5.5$ bpm and $s_D \approx 2.22$ bpm. The paired $t$-statistic is

$$t = \frac{\bar{D} - 0}{s_D / \sqrt{n}} = \frac{5.5}{2.22/\sqrt{10}} \approx 7.83,$$

which, compared against a $t_{9}$ distribution, yields $p \ll 0.001$, providing strong evidence that training reduces heart rate.

**Key Theorem (Paired $t$-test).** Suppose $D_1, \ldots, D_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_D, \sigma_D^2)$ with $\sigma_D$ unknown. Under $H_0: \mu_D = 0$, the statistic

$$T = \frac{\bar{D}}{S_D / \sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}.$$

A two-sided level-$\alpha$ test rejects $H_0$ when $|T| > t_{n-1,\,\alpha/2}$. The pairing removes between-subject variability from the error term, yielding a more powerful test than an independent two-sample $t$-test whenever the within-pair correlation $\rho$ satisfies $\rho > 0$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy / SciPy).**

```python
from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

pre  = np.array([72, 80, 68, 85, 74, 79, 70, 83, 71, 76])
post = np.array([68, 73, 66, 76, 69, 73, 67, 75, 67, 69])
D    = pre - post

t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_1samp(D, popmean=0)
print(f"D̄ = {D.mean():.2f},  s_D = {D.std(ddof=1):.2f}")
print(f"t = {t_stat:.3f},  p = {p_value:.4f}")
```

Running this cell reproduces the example above and illustrates that the inference is entirely equivalent to applying `ttest_1samp` to the difference vector—confirming that pairing collapses a two-sample problem into a one-sample problem on $D$.

### pooled_proportion (0.0s)

## Pooled Proportion

**Definition.** When testing whether two independent populations share the same proportion, it is necessary to estimate that common proportion under the null hypothesis $H_0: p_1 = p_2$. The *pooled proportion* $\hat{p}$ is the combined success rate across both samples:

$$\hat{p} = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$

where $x_i$ is the number of successes and $n_i$ is the sample size in group $i$. Because the null hypothesis asserts a single underlying probability, pooling the data yields a lower-variance estimator of that common $p$ than either $\hat{p}_1$ or $\hat{p}_2$ alone.

---

**Worked Example.** A university studies whether pass rates differ between two teaching formats. In the lecture cohort, 180 of 240 students passed; in the online cohort, 140 of 200 students passed. Compute the pooled proportion:

$$\hat{p} = \frac{180 + 140}{240 + 200} = \frac{320}{440} \approx 0.7273$$

This single value is then used to construct the standard error of the difference $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$ under $H_0$:

$$SE = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})\!\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)} = \sqrt{0.7273 \times 0.2727 \times \left(\frac{1}{240} + \frac{1}{200}\right)} \approx 0.0407$$

The test statistic is $z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{SE} = \frac{0.75 - 0.70}{0.0407} \approx 1.23$, which does not exceed the critical value $z_{0.025} = 1.96$, so we fail to reject $H_0$ at the 5% level.

---

**Key Theorem.** Under $H_0: p_1 = p_2 = p$ and the large-sample conditions $n_i \hat{p} \geq 5$ and $n_i(1-\hat{p}) \geq 5$, the statistic

$$Z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n_1}+\tfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

by the Central Limit Theorem applied jointly to both samples. Pooling is essential here: substituting separate estimators $\hat{p}_1$ and $\hat{p}_2$ into the denominator would produce a valid confidence-interval standard error but an invalid test standard error, because it does not enforce the null constraint.

---

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import Rational, sqrt, N

x1, n1 = 180, 240
x2, n2 = 140, 200

p_pool = Rational(x1 + x2, n1 + n2)
se = sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (Rational(1, n1) + Rational(1, n2)))
p1_hat = Rational(x1, n1)
p2_hat = Rational(x2, n2)
z = (p1_hat - p2_hat) / se

print("Pooled proportion:", N(p_pool, 4))
print("Standard error:   ", N(se, 4))
print("z-statistic:      ", N(z, 4))
```

Running this cell reproduces the numerical results above exactly, and symbolic arithmetic confirms that no floating-point rounding error enters the calculation.

### matched_pairs_test (0.0s)

## Matched Pairs Test

**Definition.** Let $(X_1, Y_1), (X_2, Y_2), \ldots, (X_n, Y_n)$ be $n$ paired observations drawn from a bivariate population. Define the difference scores $D_i = X_i - Y_i$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$. The *matched pairs t-test* (also called the paired t-test) assesses the null hypothesis

$$H_0: \mu_D = 0$$

against a one- or two-sided alternative, where $\mu_D = E[D_i]$ is the population mean difference. The test statistic is

$$t = \frac{\bar{D}}{s_D / \sqrt{n}},$$

where $\bar{D} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n D_i$ is the sample mean of the differences and $s_D = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (D_i - \bar{D})^2}$ is their sample standard deviation. Under $H_0$, and assuming the $D_i$ are i.i.d. normal, $t \sim t_{n-1}$.

**Worked Example.** A physiologist measures resting heart rate (bpm) in eight participants before and after a six-week aerobic programme:

| Participant | Before ($X_i$) | After ($Y_i$) | $D_i = X_i - Y_i$ |
|:-----------:|:--------------:|:-------------:|:-----------------:|
| 1 | 72 | 68 | 4 |
| 2 | 85 | 79 | 6 |
| 3 | 78 | 75 | 3 |
| 4 | 90 | 83 | 7 |
| 5 | 66 | 65 | 1 |
| 6 | 74 | 70 | 4 |
| 7 | 82 | 76 | 6 |
| 8 | 69 | 66 | 3 |

We compute $\bar{D} = 34/8 = 4.25$ and $s_D \approx 1.91$. The test statistic is

$$t = \frac{4.25}{1.91/\sqrt{8}} \approx \frac{4.25}{0.675} \approx 6.30.$$

With $\nu = 7$ degrees of freedom, the two-tailed $p$-value satisfies $p \approx 0.0004 \ll 0.05$, so we reject $H_0$ and conclude that the programme significantly reduced resting heart rate.

**Key Theorem.** The paired design is strictly more powerful than the independent two-sample $t$-test when within-pair correlation $\rho > 0$. Specifically, the variance of the difference satisfies $\operatorname{Var}(D) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y$, which is smaller than $\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2$ whenever $\rho > 0$. Pairing therefore eliminates between-subject variability from the error term, concentrating inferential power on the treatment effect.

**Lab Cell (SymPy/SciPy).**

```python
from scipy import stats
before = [72, 85, 78, 90, 66, 74, 82, 69]
after  = [68, 79, 75, 83, 65, 70, 76, 66]
t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_rel(before, after)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.3f}, p = {p_value:.4f}")
```

### two_means_known_sd_test (0.0s)

## Two Means Known SD Test

**Definition.** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n_1}$ be a random sample from a population with mean $\mu_1$ and *known* standard deviation $\sigma_1$, and let $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_{n_2}$ be an independent random sample from a population with mean $\mu_2$ and *known* standard deviation $\sigma_2$. The **two-sample $z$-test** evaluates the null hypothesis

$$H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = \delta_0$$

against a one- or two-sided alternative, where $\delta_0$ is a specified constant (commonly $0$). The test statistic is

$$Z = \frac{(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}) - \delta_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}},$$

which follows a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ exactly under $H_0$, by the independence of $\bar{X}$ and $\bar{Y}$ and the Central Limit Theorem.

**Worked Example.** A researcher compares mean resting heart rates in two cities. City A: $n_1 = 50$, $\bar{x} = 72.4$ bpm, $\sigma_1 = 8$ bpm. City B: $n_2 = 60$, $\bar{y} = 69.1$ bpm, $\sigma_2 = 7$ bpm. Test $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ against $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$ at $\alpha = 0.05$.

$$Z = \frac{(72.4 - 69.1) - 0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{64}{50} + \dfrac{49}{60}}} = \frac{3.3}{\sqrt{1.28 + 0.817}} = \frac{3.3}{\sqrt{2.097}} \approx \frac{3.3}{1.448} \approx 2.28.$$

The two-tailed critical value is $z_{0.025} = 1.96$. Since $|Z| = 2.28 > 1.96$, we reject $H_0$ and conclude that the mean heart rates differ significantly between the two cities ($p \approx 0.023$).

**Key Theorem.** Under $H_0$ and the assumption of independence, $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ for all sample sizes $n_1, n_2 \geq 1$ when $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ are truly known. The power against the alternative $\mu_1 - \mu_2 = \delta$ is $\Phi\!\left(z_{\alpha/2} - \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}}\right) + \Phi\!\left(-z_{\alpha/2} - \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}}\right)$, showing that power increases with larger $|\delta|$ or larger sample sizes.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

x_bar, y_bar = 72.4, 69.1
sigma1, sigma2 = 8, 7
n1, n2 = 50, 60
delta0 = 0

se = np.sqrt(sigma1**2 / n1 + sigma2**2 / n2)
z_stat = (x_bar - y_bar - delta0) / se
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z_stat)))

print(f"Z = {z_stat:.4f}, p-value = {p_value:.4f}")
```

### two_means_unknown_sd_test (0.0s)

## Two Means Unknown SD Test

### Definition

The **two-sample $t$-test for independent means with unknown population standard deviations** addresses the following problem: given two independent random samples drawn from normally distributed populations, determine whether the population means $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ differ significantly, without prior knowledge of $\sigma_1$ or $\sigma_2$.

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_{n_1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_2} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ be independent samples. Two cases arise based on whether $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$.

**Equal variances (pooled).** The pooled variance estimator is

$$s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2},$$

and the test statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{X} - \bar{Y}}{s_p\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n_1}+\tfrac{1}{n_2}}}$$

follows a $t$-distribution with $\nu = n_1 + n_2 - 2$ degrees of freedom under $H_0\colon \mu_1 = \mu_2$.

**Unequal variances (Welch's test).** When equality of variances cannot be assumed, the statistic

$$t_W = \frac{\bar{X} - \bar{Y}}{\sqrt{\tfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\tfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

is referred to a $t$-distribution with Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom

$$\nu_W = \frac{\left(\tfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\tfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\tfrac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1}+\tfrac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}.$$

### Worked Example

A pharmacologist measures systolic blood pressure (mmHg) in two treatment groups: Group A ($n_1=12$, $\bar{x}=135$, $s_1=8$) and Group B ($n_2=10$, $\bar{x}=128$, $s_2=11$). Assuming unequal variances and $\alpha=0.05$, compute Welch's statistic:

$$t_W = \frac{135-128}{\sqrt{\tfrac{64}{12}+\tfrac{121}{10}}} = \frac{7}{\sqrt{5.33+12.1}} = \frac{7}{\sqrt{17.43}} \approx 1.677.$$

The Welch degrees of freedom evaluate to approximately $\nu_W \approx 16.1$. The critical value $t_{0.025,16} \approx 2.12$, so $|t_W| < t^*$ and we fail to reject $H_0$.

### Key Theorem

**Welch–Satterthwaite Approximation.** Under $H_0\colon \mu_1=\mu_2$, the statistic $t_W$ is approximately $t_{\nu_W}$-distributed, where $\nu_W$ is as defined above. This approximation is conservative: the true Type I error rate does not exceed the nominal $\alpha$.

### Lab Cell

```python
from sympy.stats import StudentT, P, Lt
from sympy import symbols, sqrt, Rational, N

x_bar, y_bar = 135, 128
s1, s2, n1, n2 = 8, 11, 12, 10

se = sqrt(Rational(s1**2, n1) + Rational(s2**2, n2))
t_stat = Rational(x_bar - y_bar, 1) / se

num = (Rational(s1**2, n1) + Rational(s2**2, n2))**2
den = Rational(s1**4, n1**2*(n1-1)) + Rational(s2**4, n2**2*(n2-1))
nu = N(num / den)

print(f"t_W = {N(t_stat):.4f},  df = {nu:.2f}")
```

### two_proportions_test (0.0s)

## Two Proportions Test

**Definition.** Let $X_1 \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_1, p_1)$ and $X_2 \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_2, p_2)$ be independent random variables representing the number of successes in two separate populations. The *two proportions test* assesses the null hypothesis

$$H_0: p_1 = p_2 \quad \text{against} \quad H_a: p_1 \neq p_2 \; (\text{or one-sided variants}),$$

using sample proportions $\hat{p}_1 = X_1/n_1$ and $\hat{p}_2 = X_2/n_2$. Under $H_0$, both samples share a common proportion $p$, estimated by the *pooled proportion*

$$\hat{p} = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{n_1 + n_2}.$$

The test statistic is

$$Z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\!\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1} + \dfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}},$$

which converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n_1, n_2 \to \infty$, provided $n_i \hat{p}_i \geq 5$ and $n_i(1-\hat{p}_i) \geq 5$ for each $i$.

**Worked Example.** A clinical trial finds that 48 of 200 patients in group A experienced side effects, while 35 of 180 patients in group B did. Thus $\hat{p}_1 = 0.240$, $\hat{p}_2 = 0.194$, and

$$\hat{p} = \frac{48 + 35}{200 + 180} = \frac{83}{380} \approx 0.2184.$$

The standard error is $\sqrt{0.2184 \times 0.7816 \times (1/200 + 1/180)} \approx 0.0422$, giving $Z \approx (0.240 - 0.194)/0.0422 \approx 1.09$. The two-tailed $p$-value is $2\Phi(-1.09) \approx 0.276$, so we fail to reject $H_0$ at the $\alpha = 0.05$ level.

**Key Theorem (Asymptotic Normality of $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$).** Under $H_0$ and the regularity conditions above, the Central Limit Theorem applied independently to each sample yields $\hat{p}_i \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(p, p(1-p)/n_i)$. Independence then gives $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,\, p(1-p)(1/n_1 + 1/n_2))$, and substituting $\hat{p}$ for the unknown $p$ preserves the limiting distribution by Slutsky's theorem.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy.stats import Normal, cdf
from sympy import sqrt, Rational, N

n1, x1 = 200, 48
n2, x2 = 180, 35
p1_hat = Rational(x1, n1)
p2_hat = Rational(x2, n2)
p_pool = Rational(x1 + x2, n1 + n2)

SE = sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (Rational(1, n1) + Rational(1, n2)))
Z_stat = (p1_hat - p2_hat) / SE
Z = Normal('Z', 0, 1)
p_value = 2 * N(cdf(Z)(-abs(Z_stat)))
print(f"Z = {float(Z_stat):.4f}, p-value = {p_value:.4f}")
```

## Two Sample Test Classification

**Definition.** A *two-sample test* is a statistical hypothesis test that compares a parameter of two independent populations, $\mathcal{P}_1$ and $\mathcal{P}_2$, using samples $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_m)$ and $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)$ drawn from each. Two-sample tests are classified along three orthogonal axes: (1) the *scale* of the outcome variable (continuous vs. categorical), (2) the *distributional assumptions* imposed on the populations (parametric vs. nonparametric), and (3) the *nature of the null hypothesis* (equality of means, variances, or entire distributions). This classification determines which test statistic and reference distribution are appropriate.

Formally, let $\mu_1, \mu_2$ denote population means and $\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2$ denote variances. The null hypothesis takes one of three canonical forms:

$$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2, \qquad H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2, \qquad H_0: F_1 = F_2,$$

where $F_1, F_2$ are the respective cumulative distribution functions.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a researcher records exam scores for two teaching methods: Group A ($m = 30$ students, $\bar{x} = 72$, $s_x = 8$) and Group B ($n = 30$ students, $\bar{y} = 68$, $s_y = 9$). The outcome is continuous and approximately normal, so this falls in the *parametric, continuous, means* cell of the classification. The appropriate test is the two-sample $t$-test with test statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \bar{y}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_x^2}{m} + \dfrac{s_y^2}{n}}} = \frac{72 - 68}{\sqrt{\dfrac{64}{30} + \dfrac{81}{30}}} \approx \frac{4}{2.28} \approx 1.75.$$

Under $H_0$, $t$ follows a $t$-distribution with degrees of freedom estimated by the Welch–Satterthwaite formula. If instead the normality assumption is untenable, the Mann–Whitney $U$-test occupies the *nonparametric, continuous, distributions* cell and requires no moment assumptions.

**Theorem (Test Validity Criterion).** A two-sample test statistic $T(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ controls the Type I error at level $\alpha$ if and only if, under $H_0$, the distribution of $T$ is fully determined by the chosen reference family and the sample sizes $m, n$. Misclassification — applying a parametric test when distributional assumptions fail — inflates the true rejection rate beyond $\alpha$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from scipy import stats
import sympy as sp

x = [72, 68, 75, 70, 74]   # truncated sample A
y = [65, 69, 67, 71, 63]   # truncated sample B

t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var=False)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f}, p = {p_val:.4f}")

# Symbolic Welch degrees of freedom
sx, sy, m, n = sp.symbols('s_x s_y m n', positive=True)
df_welch = (sx**2/m + sy**2/n)**2 / (sx**4/(m**2*(m-1)) + sy**4/(n**2*(n-1)))
sp.pprint(sp.simplify(df_welch))
```

## Real World Two Group Comparisons

**Definition.** A *two-group comparison* is a statistical procedure that tests whether a numerical quantity of interest differs systematically between two distinct populations. Formally, let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n_1} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma^2)$ and $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_{n_2} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma^2)$ be independent random samples. The null and alternative hypotheses are

$$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2.$$

When the common variance $\sigma^2$ is unknown, the appropriate test statistic is the *pooled two-sample $t$-statistic*

$$t = \frac{\bar{X} - \bar{Y}}{s_p \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n_1} + \tfrac{1}{n_2}}}, \qquad s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2},$$

which follows a $t$-distribution with $\nu = n_1 + n_2 - 2$ degrees of freedom under $H_0$.

**Worked Example.** A university nutrition study records daily calcium intake (mg) for two groups: $n_1 = 12$ athletes and $n_2 = 10$ non-athletes. The sample means are $\bar{X} = 1{,}102$ and $\bar{Y} = 874$, with sample standard deviations $s_1 = 145$ and $s_2 = 132$.

Compute the pooled variance:

$$s_p^2 = \frac{11(145)^2 + 9(132)^2}{20} = \frac{231{,}605 + 156{,}816}{20} = 19{,}421, \quad s_p \approx 139.4.$$

The test statistic is

$$t = \frac{1102 - 874}{139.4\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{12}+\tfrac{1}{10}}} \approx \frac{228}{60.5} \approx 3.77.$$

With $\nu = 20$ degrees of freedom and $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-tailed), the critical value is $t_{0.025,20} \approx 2.086$. Since $3.77 > 2.086$, we reject $H_0$ and conclude that athletes consume significantly more calcium.

**Key Theorem (Welch–Satterthwaite Correction).** When the assumption $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$ is doubtful, the degrees of freedom are approximated by

$$\hat{\nu} = \frac{\left(\tfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \tfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\tfrac{s_1^4}{n_1^2(n_1-1)} + \tfrac{s_2^4}{n_2^2(n_2-1)}},$$

yielding a conservative $t$-test that remains valid without the equal-variance restriction.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**
```python
from scipy import stats
athletes     = [1102, 980, 1210, 1050, 1175, 1088, 1130, 1020, 1095, 1160, 1140, 1005]
non_athletes = [874, 790, 910, 855, 920, 840, 865, 900, 830, 895]
t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(athletes, non_athletes, equal_var=True)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.3f}, p = {p_val:.4f}")
```
