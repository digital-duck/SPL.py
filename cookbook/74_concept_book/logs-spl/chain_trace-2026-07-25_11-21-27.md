## Matrix Vector Product

**Definition.** Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix with entries $a_{ij}$, and let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a column vector. The *matrix–vector product* $A\mathbf{x}$ is the vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ whose $i$-th entry is

$$b_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}\, x_j, \quad i = 1, \ldots, m.$$

Equivalently, $A\mathbf{x}$ is a *linear combination of the columns of $A$*, weighted by the entries of $\mathbf{x}$:

$$A\mathbf{x} = x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{a}_2 + \cdots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n,$$

where $\mathbf{a}_j$ denotes the $j$-th column of $A$. This column-space interpretation is central to understanding systems of linear equations.

**Worked Example.** Consider

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Applying the row-dot-product rule:

$$b_1 = (2)(1) + (-1)(2) = 0, \qquad b_2 = (3)(1) + (4)(2) = 11.$$

Hence $A\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 11 \end{pmatrix}$. Alternatively, via the column view: $1 \cdot \begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix} + 2 \cdot \begin{pmatrix}-1\\4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2-2\\3+8\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\11\end{pmatrix}$, confirming both interpretations agree.

**Key Theorem** *(Linearity of the Matrix–Vector Product).* For any $m \times n$ matrix $A$, vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v}, \qquad A(c\,\mathbf{u}) = c\,(A\mathbf{u}).$$

*Proof sketch.* Both identities follow immediately from the distributivity and homogeneity of the summation $\sum_j a_{ij} x_j$ over its inputs. This pair of properties characterizes $A$ as a *linear map* from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$, establishing the fundamental correspondence between matrices and linear transformations. $\blacksquare$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import Matrix

A = Matrix([[2, -1], [3, 4]])
x = Matrix([1, 2])

b = A * x          # matrix–vector product
print("A * x =", b)

# Verify linearity: A(u + v) == Au + Av
u = Matrix([1, 0])
v = Matrix([0, 1])
assert A * (u + v) == A * u + A * v
print("Linearity confirmed.")
```

Running this cell prints $\begin{pmatrix}0\\11\end{pmatrix}$ and verifies the linearity property symbolically, bridging the algebraic definition with direct computation.

### diagonal_matrix (0.0s)

## Diagonal Matrix

**Definition.** A square matrix $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is called a *diagonal matrix* if every off-diagonal entry is zero; that is, $d_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i \neq j$. Such a matrix is written compactly as $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n)$, where $d_k = d_{kk}$ denotes the $k$-th diagonal entry. The zero matrix and the identity matrix $I_n = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1, \ldots, 1)$ are canonical special cases.

**Worked Example.** Let $D = \operatorname{diag}(3, -1, 4)$. Explicitly,

$$D = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Computing the product $D\mathbf{x}$ for $\mathbf{x} = (2, 5, 1)^\top$ gives

$$D\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \cdot 2 \\ (-1) \cdot 5 \\ 4 \cdot 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ -5 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Each coordinate of $\mathbf{x}$ is scaled independently by the corresponding diagonal entry, confirming the geometric interpretation: $D$ stretches or reflects each coordinate axis without mixing them.

**Key Theorem.** *Diagonal matrices form a commutative subalgebra of $\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$.* Specifically, if $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_n)$ and $E = \operatorname{diag}(e_1, \ldots, e_n)$, then:

1. $D + E = \operatorname{diag}(d_1 + e_1, \ldots, d_n + e_n)$,
2. $DE = \operatorname{diag}(d_1 e_1, \ldots, d_n e_n)$,
3. $DE = ED$.

*Proof sketch.* For entry $(i,j)$, the $(i,j)$ entry of $DE$ equals $\sum_{k=1}^n d_{ik} e_{kj}$. Since $d_{ik} = 0$ for $k \neq i$ and $e_{kj} = 0$ for $k \neq j$, the only surviving term occurs at $k = i = j$, yielding $d_{ii} e_{ii}$. Symmetry of this expression in $D$ and $E$ establishes commutativity. $\square$

A further consequence is that $D^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(d_1^{-1}, \ldots, d_n^{-1})$ whenever all $d_k \neq 0$, and that $\det(D) = \prod_{k=1}^n d_k$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import diag, Matrix, symbols

d1, d2, d3 = symbols('d1 d2 d3')
D = diag(d1, d2, d3)
E = diag(3, -1, 4)

print("D =", D)
print("det(E) =", E.det())          # -12
print("E^{-1} =", E.inv())
print("DE == ED:", D * E == E * D)  # True
```

Running this cell verifies the theorem symbolically and reinforces that diagonal matrix arithmetic reduces to elementwise scalar operations.

### similar_matrices (0.0s)

## Similar Matrices

**Definition.** Two $n \times n$ matrices $A$ and $B$ are called *similar* if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ such that

$$B = P^{-1}AP.$$

The matrix $P$ is called a *change-of-basis matrix*, and the transformation $A \mapsto P^{-1}AP$ is a *similarity transformation*. Similarity is an equivalence relation: it is reflexive ($A = I^{-1}AI$), symmetric, and transitive. Geometrically, $A$ and $B$ represent the same linear transformation $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, but expressed with respect to different ordered bases. The columns of $P$ carry the new basis vectors written in the old coordinate system.

**Worked Example.** Let

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Compute $P^{-1}$. Since $\det P = -3$,

$$P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-3}\begin{pmatrix} -2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & -1/3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then

$$B = P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The result $B$ is diagonal, revealing the eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$ of $A$ directly on its main diagonal. The columns of $P$ are precisely the corresponding eigenvectors.

**Key Theorem.** Similar matrices share all of the following invariants: (i) the same characteristic polynomial $\det(\lambda I - A)$; (ii) the same eigenvalues with identical algebraic and geometric multiplicities; (iii) the same determinant; (iv) the same trace; and (v) the same rank. None of these quantities depends on the choice of basis. Conversely, two diagonalizable matrices are similar if and only if they have the same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities.

*Proof sketch.* For the characteristic polynomial: $\det(\lambda I - B) = \det(\lambda I - P^{-1}AP) = \det(P^{-1}(\lambda I - A)P) = \det(\lambda I - A)$, using multiplicativity of the determinant. All remaining invariants follow because they are expressible in terms of the characteristic polynomial. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, symbols

A = Matrix([[4, 1], [2, 3]])
P = Matrix([[1, 1], [1, -2]])

B = P.inv() * A * P
print("B =", B)                          # diagonal form
print("char poly A:", A.charpoly())
print("char poly B:", B.charpoly())      # must match
print("eigenvals A:", A.eigenvals())
```

### diagonalizable_matrix (0.0s)

## Diagonalizable Matrix

**Definition.** An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$ is called *diagonalizable* if there exists an invertible matrix $P \in M_n(\mathbb{F})$ and a diagonal matrix $D \in M_n(\mathbb{F})$ such that

$$A = P D P^{-1}.$$

Equivalently, $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $\mathbb{F}^n$ has a basis consisting entirely of eigenvectors of $A$. The columns of $P$ are those eigenvectors, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the corresponding eigenvalues.

---

**Worked Example.** Let

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The characteristic polynomial is $\det(A - \lambda I) = (3 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda)$, giving distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.

For $\lambda_1 = 3$: solving $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ yields $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0)^{\top}$.

For $\lambda_2 = 2$: solving $(A - 2I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ yields $\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1, 1)^{\top}$.

Setting $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $D = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, one verifies that $AP = PD$, confirming $A = PDP^{-1}$.

---

**Key Theorem** *(Sufficient Condition for Diagonalizability).* If an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, then $A$ is diagonalizable.

*Proof sketch.* Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. With $n$ such eigenvectors in $\mathbb{F}^n$, they form a basis, so $P$ is invertible and $A = PDP^{-1}$ holds. $\square$

*Remark.* Distinctness is sufficient but not necessary. A matrix with repeated eigenvalues may still be diagonalizable if each eigenvalue's geometric multiplicity (dimension of its eigenspace) equals its algebraic multiplicity.

---

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, pprint

A = Matrix([[3, 1], [0, 2]])

P, D = A.diagonalize()   # raises MatrixError if not diagonalizable
pprint(D)                # diagonal eigenvalue matrix
pprint(P)                # eigenvector columns
print(P * D * P**-1 == A)  # True
```

Running this cell displays $D = \operatorname{diag}(2, 3)$ and the corresponding $P$, and confirms the factorization identity. Try replacing $A$ with a non-diagonalizable matrix such as $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ to observe the raised exception.

### eigenvalue_eigenvector (0.0s)

## Eigenvalue Eigenvector

**Definition.** Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$). A nonzero vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is called an *eigenvector* of $A$ if there exists a scalar $\lambda$ such that

$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}.$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is called the *eigenvalue* associated with $\mathbf{v}$. Geometrically, multiplying by $A$ does not rotate $\mathbf{v}$; it only stretches or reflects it by the factor $\lambda$.

To find eigenvalues, rearrange the defining equation as $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. For a nonzero solution $\mathbf{v}$ to exist, the matrix $A - \lambda I$ must be singular, which requires

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0.$$

This is the *characteristic equation* of $A$. Expanding the determinant yields a degree-$n$ polynomial in $\lambda$, called the *characteristic polynomial*. Each root of this polynomial is an eigenvalue of $A$.

**Worked Example.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic polynomial is

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 3-\lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 2-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (3-\lambda)(2-\lambda) = 0,$$

giving eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$. For $\lambda_1 = 3$, solve $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

For $\lambda_2 = 2$, solving $(A - 2I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ yields $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Theorem (Spectral Theorem).** If $A$ is a real symmetric matrix ($A = A^\top$), then all eigenvalues of $A$ are real, and eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Consequently, $A$ admits the decomposition $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$, where $Q$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ is an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$. Then $\bar{\mathbf{v}}^\top A \mathbf{v} = \lambda \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$. Since $A$ is symmetric, the left side is also real, forcing $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. Orthogonality of eigenvectors follows from $(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)\langle \mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{v}_j \rangle = 0$. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, symbols, latex

A = Matrix([[3, 1], [0, 2]])
lam = symbols('lambda')

char_poly = A.charpoly(lam)
print("Characteristic polynomial:", latex(char_poly.as_expr()))

eigendata = A.eigenvects()
for val, mult, vecs in eigendata:
    print(f"lambda = {val}, multiplicity = {mult}, eigenvector = {vecs[0].T}")
```

Running this cell confirms $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 2$ and their associated eigenvectors, bridging the symbolic derivation to computational verification.

### diagonalization_characterization (0.0s)

## Diagonalization Characterization

**Definition.** A square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is said to be *diagonalizable* if there exists an invertible matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and a diagonal matrix $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n)$ such that

$$A = P D P^{-1}.$$

Equivalently, $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $\mathbb{R}^n$ possesses a basis consisting entirely of eigenvectors of $A$. The columns of $P$ are those eigenvectors, and the corresponding diagonal entries of $D$ are the associated eigenvalues.

**Worked Example.** Consider the matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The characteristic polynomial is $\det(A - \lambda I) = (4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 5)$, yielding eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 2$ and $\lambda_2 = 5$. Solving $(A - 2I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ gives eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_1 = (-1, 2)^\top$; solving $(A - 5I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1, 1)^\top$. Since these two vectors are linearly independent, we set

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix},$$

and verify that $AP = PD$, confirming $A = PDP^{-1}$.

**Theorem (Diagonalizability Criterion).** A matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is diagonalizable if and only if for each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, the geometric multiplicity (dimension of the eigenspace $\ker(A - \lambda_i I)$) equals the algebraic multiplicity (multiplicity of $\lambda_i$ as a root of the characteristic polynomial). In particular, any matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is automatically diagonalizable, since each geometric multiplicity is at least one and cannot exceed the corresponding algebraic multiplicity of one.

*Proof sketch.* Eigenvectors from distinct eigenspaces are linearly independent. When all multiplicities match, the eigenspaces together span $\mathbb{R}^n$, furnishing the required eigenvector basis. The converse follows by counting dimensions.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, pprint

A = Matrix([[4, 1], [2, 3]])
P, D = A.diagonalize()   # raises if not diagonalizable
pprint(P)
pprint(D)
assert A == P * D * P.inv()
```

Running this cell prints $P$ and $D$ and silently passes the assertion, confirming the decomposition symbolically.

### matrix_power_via_diagonalization (0.0s)

## Matrix Power Via Diagonalization

**Definition.** A square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is *diagonalizable* if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that

$$A = P D P^{-1}, \quad D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n),$$

where $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues of $A$ and the columns of $P$ are the corresponding eigenvectors. This factorization is powerful because diagonal matrices are trivially raised to any power: $(D^k)_{ii} = \lambda_i^k$. Consequently, computing $A^k$ for large $k$ reduces to scalar exponentiation rather than repeated matrix multiplication.

**Derivation.** Observe that

$$A^2 = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1}) = PD(P^{-1}P)DP^{-1} = PD^2P^{-1}.$$

By induction, for any positive integer $k$,

$$\boxed{A^k = P D^k P^{-1}}, \quad D^k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \lambda_2^k, \ldots, \lambda_n^k).$$

This reduces an $O(n^3 k)$ chain of matrix products to a single similarity transformation at cost $O(n^3)$, independent of $k$.

**Worked Example.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. The eigenvalues satisfy $\det(A - \lambda I) = (3-\lambda)(2-\lambda) = 0$, giving $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 2$. The eigenvectors are $v_1 = (1, 0)^\top$ and $v_2 = (-1, 1)^\top$, so

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

For $k = 5$, $D^5 = \operatorname{diag}(243, 32)$, and therefore

$$A^5 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 243 & 0 \\ 0 & 32 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 243 & 211 \\ 0 & 32 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Key Theorem.** An $n \times n$ matrix with $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors is diagonalizable. In particular, any matrix with $n$ *distinct* eigenvalues satisfies this condition.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import Matrix
A = Matrix([[3, 1], [0, 2]])
P, D = A.diagonalize()          # returns P, D such that A = P D P**-1
print(P * D**5 * P**-1)         # verify A^5 symbolically
```

### fibonacci_closed_form (0.0s)

## Fibonacci Closed Form

**Definition.** The Fibonacci sequence $\{F_n\}_{n \geq 0}$ is defined by the linear recurrence
$$F_0 = 0, \quad F_1 = 1, \quad F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2} \quad (n \geq 2).$$
The *closed form* (Binet's formula) expresses $F_n$ directly as a function of $n$, without reference to prior terms:
$$F_n = \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}},$$
where $\varphi = \dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.618$ is the *golden ratio* and $\psi = \dfrac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx -0.618$ is its conjugate. Both $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are roots of the *characteristic equation* $x^2 = x + 1$.

**Proof sketch.** Assume a solution of the form $F_n = r^n$. Substituting into the recurrence gives $r^2 = r + 1$, with roots $r = \varphi$ and $r = \psi$. By linearity, the general solution is $F_n = A\varphi^n + B\psi^n$. Applying initial conditions $F_0 = 0$ and $F_1 = 1$ yields $A = 1/\sqrt{5}$ and $B = -1/\sqrt{5}$, producing Binet's formula.

**Worked example.** Compute $F_7$.

Direct recursion gives $0,1,1,2,3,5,8,13$, so $F_7 = 13$. Using Binet's formula:
$$F_7 = \frac{\varphi^7 - \psi^7}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{(1.6180\ldots)^7 - (-0.6180\ldots)^7}{\sqrt{5}} \approx \frac{29.034 - (-0.034)}{2.236} = \frac{29.068}{2.236} \approx 13.00.$$
Because $|\psi| < 1$, the term $\psi^n/\sqrt{5}$ decays rapidly; for $n \geq 1$, $F_n$ is the nearest integer to $\varphi^n/\sqrt{5}$.

**Key theorem (exponential growth rate).** $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{F_{n+1}}{F_n} = \varphi.$ This follows immediately from Binet's formula, since $(\psi/\varphi)^n \to 0$.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import sqrt, Rational, simplify, Symbol, fibonacci

n = Symbol('n', integer=True, nonneg=True)
phi = (1 + sqrt(5)) / 2
psi = (1 - sqrt(5)) / 2

binet = (phi**n - psi**n) / sqrt(5)

# Verify against the built-in for n = 0..10
for k in range(11):
    val = simplify(binet.subs(n, k))
    assert val == fibonacci(k), f"Mismatch at n={k}"

print("Binet's formula verified for n = 0 … 10")
```
