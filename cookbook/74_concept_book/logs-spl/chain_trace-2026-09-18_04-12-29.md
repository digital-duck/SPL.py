### proposition (42.4s)

## Proposition

**Definition.** Under the propositions-as-types (Curry–Howard) correspondence, a proposition $P$ is not a truth-valued sentence but a type — an object of the same syntactic category as $\mathbb{N}$ or $A \to B$. A *proof* of $P$ is a term $p$ inhabiting that type, written $p : P$. The proposition is *true* exactly when its type is inhabited, i.e., when some term of that type can be constructed; it is *false* (or at least unproven) when no such term is exhibited. This replaces the classical bivalent semantics $\llbracket P \rrbracket \in \{\text{true}, \text{false}\}$ with a constructive semantics: truth is witnessed evidence, not an abstract label. The single new primitive to internalize here is the *function type* $P \to Q$, read simultaneously as "the proposition $P \Rightarrow Q$" and as "the type of programs transforming a $P$-witness into a $Q$-witness."

**Theorem (Curry–Howard, implicational fragment).** A derivation of $P \Rightarrow Q$ in intuitionistic natural deduction is definitionally identical to a well-typed $\lambda$-term of type $P \to Q$, and $\beta$-reduction of the term corresponds to proof normalization (cut-elimination).

*Proof sketch.* By induction on derivations. The introduction rule for $\Rightarrow$ — discharging a hypothesis $x:P$ to conclude $P \Rightarrow Q$ from a derivation of $Q$ — is exactly $\lambda$-abstraction $\lambda x^P.\,q : P \to Q$. Modus ponens is application: given $f : P \to Q$ and $p : P$, the term $(f\,p) : Q$ witnesses the conclusion. A derivation containing an introduction immediately followed by an elimination (a "detour," or logically, an unnecessary lemma) reduces to a shorter derivation exactly as $(\lambda x.\,q)\,p$ $\beta$-reduces to $q[p/x]$; this is checked by induction on the structure of the detour. $\blacksquare$

**Literature and open problems.** The correspondence originates with Curry–Feys (1958) and Howard's unpublished 1969 note (Howard 1980), and is developed systematically in Sørensen–Urzyczyn's *Lectures on the Curry–Howard Isomorphism* and Girard–Lafont–Taylor's *Proofs and Types*, where the same function-type primitive is shown to extend, connective by connective, to the full propositional and predicate calculus (conjunction as pairing, disjunction as a tagged sum, quantifiers as dependent products and sums in Martin-Löf's 1984 type theory — the setting relevant to SPL's treatment of propositions as first-class dependent types). Classical logic (with excluded middle or double-negation elimination) does *not* fit the pure isomorphism without extra structure; Griffin's (1990) observation that call/cc types as $((P\to\bot)\to\bot)\to P$ opened the study of classical Curry–Howard via control operators, still an active area (delimited continuations, polarized/focused logics of Girard and Laurent). A genuinely open problem: what is the correct computational reading of the univalence axiom and higher inductive types in Homotopy Type Theory, where proofs of equality themselves form a type with nontrivial higher structure (HoTT Book, 2013).

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a small typed $\lambda$-calculus checker (or use Agda/Lean restricted to the $\to$-fragment) and formalize a single nontrivial implicational tautology, e.g. $(P \to (Q \to R)) \to ((P \to Q) \to (P \to R))$; produce the explicit inhabiting term, then exhibit a derivation with an avoidable detour and mechanically $\beta$-reduce it, comparing the reduced term to the term obtained from a cut-free natural-deduction proof of the same proposition.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the translation rule used for $\Rightarrow \leftrightarrow \to$, exhibit the full derivation alongside its term, verify the $\beta$-reduction/normalization correspondence step by step on the chosen example, and note explicitly which further connectives (conjunction, disjunction, quantifiers) are deferred to a follow-up investigation rather than treated here.

### term (60.5s)

## Term

**Definition.** Fix a type system with type formation rules yielding a collection of types $\mathcal{T}$ (e.g. base types like $\mathbb{N}$, function types $A \to B$, and — anticipating dependent type theory — types indexed by terms). For each type $A \in \mathcal{T}$, the *terms of type* $A$, written $t : A$, are the inhabitants of $A$: the objects the type formation rules license as evidence that $A$ is nonempty. A **judgment** $t : A$ is a formal assertion, derivable from typing rules, that $t$ has type $A$. Terms are built inductively: variables, constants, applications $f\,a$, and abstractions $\lambda x. b$ are all terms, and the typing rules determine which types they may carry. Crucially, a `def` binds a name to a term together with its type — `def n : \mathbb{N} := 5` names the term $5$ of type $\mathbb{N}$ — and this naming is *definitional*, not merely notational: the name and the term it abbreviates are interchangeable by the rule of $\delta$-reduction, which unfolds a defined name to the term it stands for.

**Theorem (uniqueness of typing, simply typed setting).** In the simply typed $\lambda$-calculus, every term $t$ has at most one type derivable from a given typing context $\Gamma$.

*Proof.* By induction on the structure of $t$. If $t$ is a variable $x$, its type is read off directly from $\Gamma$, which assigns each variable a single type by well-formedness of contexts. If $t = f\,a$, any derivation must assign $f$ some type $B \to C$ and $a$ type $B$; by the induction hypothesis these are unique, forcing $C$ unique. If $t = \lambda x. b$, a derivation assigns $x$ a type $B$ (fixed by the abstraction's annotation or inferred uniquely under context extension $\Gamma, x:B$), and by induction $b$ has a unique type $C$ in $\Gamma, x:B$, giving $t$ the unique type $B \to C$. $\blacksquare$

This uniqueness fails once types are permitted to depend on terms themselves, as in Martin-Löf type theory — a first sign that "term" and "type" interact more richly than the simply typed picture suggests.

**Literature context.** The term/type distinction and the judgment $t:A$ originate with Martin-Löf's intuitionistic type theory (Martin-Löf, 1984), whose dependent types are the natural next step beyond what is developed here. Pierce's *Types and Programming Languages* (2002) develops the syntactic metatheory (progress, preservation) for terms across a hierarchy of calculi, including the uniqueness result proved above. An open question sharpened by the $\delta$-reduction rule already introduced: should two terms of the same type ever be identified by the type checker itself on grounds other than unfolding definitions — that is, does definitional equality via $\delta$-reduction admit a principled extension to a broader notion of computational equality, and if so, is it decidable?

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a small simply typed $\lambda$-calculus with a `def` mechanism, and mechanically verify the uniqueness-of-typing theorem above (e.g. in Lean or Coq) by structural induction on term syntax. Then add a single dependent type former (e.g. `Vec n A`, a vector type indexed by its length) and locate exactly where the induction in the uniqueness proof breaks — which case fails, and why.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the typing rules used, present the induction precisely (not merely "by induction"), and give the specific term/context pair for which the dependent extension admits more than one valid typing derivation, together with a brief argument for why the simply typed proof's inductive step no longer goes through in that case.

### type (50.6s)

## Type

**Definition.** In Lean 4's dependent type theory, every well-formed term $t$ carries a *type* $T$, written $t : T$, established by an inference judgment $\Gamma \vdash t : T$ relative to a typing context $\Gamma$ of free variable declarations. Types themselves are terms: closed types such as $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathrm{Prop}$, and $\mathrm{List}\,\alpha$ live in a hierarchy of universes $\mathrm{Sort}\,0 \subset \mathrm{Sort}\,1 \subset \cdots$ (with $\mathrm{Prop} := \mathrm{Sort}\,0$ and $\mathrm{Type}\,u := \mathrm{Sort}(u+1)$), so that "$T$ is a type" is itself the judgment $\Gamma \vdash T : \mathrm{Sort}\,u$ for some universe level $u$. The command `#check e` invokes the elaborator's type inference algorithm on `e`, returning the unique (up to definitional equality) $T$ such that $\Gamma \vdash e : T$; this is the one new primitive this section introduces, and the rest of the section is built around understanding what it reports.

**Theorem (uniqueness of types up to definitional equality).** In the Calculus of Inductive Constructions underlying Lean, if $\Gamma \vdash t : T_1$ and $\Gamma \vdash t : T_2$, then $T_1 \equiv T_2$, where $\equiv$ is the kernel's definitional equality relation (generated by reduction and unfolding of definitions, and closed under congruence).

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the typing derivation. The only nontrivial case is application: if $t = f\,a$, inversion on $\Gamma \vdash f\,a : T$ forces $\Gamma \vdash f : \Pi x{:}A,\, B\,x$ and $\Gamma \vdash a : A$ with $T \equiv B\,a$; both derivations of $t$'s type must factor through this shape by injectivity of $\Pi$-formation, and the induction hypothesis on $f$ gives uniqueness of $\Pi x{:}A,\,B\,x$ up to $\equiv$, hence of $B\,a$. $\square$

This *type uniqueness* (or *subject uniqueness*) property is what makes `#check` well-defined as a function rather than a search.

**Literature.** The theorem is proved for the Calculus of Constructions in Coquand–Huet (1988) and extended to inductive types in Paulin-Mohring's thesis; Lean's kernel is documented in de Moura et al., *The Lean Theorem Prover* (CADE 2015), and Carneiro's metatheoretic study of Lean's type theory formalizes the reduction rules precisely. An open direction concerns Lean 4's extensions to the classical Calculus of Inductive Constructions — proof irrelevance for `Prop`, a primitive quotient former, and recursion compiled via well-founded induction — whose interaction with definitional equality is asserted by the implementation to preserve confluence and decidability but is not yet published as a machine-checked metatheoretic proof.

**Suggested investigation.** Reconstruct, by hand and then in Lean via `#check`, the typing derivation for a term such as `fun (n : Nat) => List.replicate n n`, tracking universe levels through `List : Type u → Type u`. Then construct a term with two syntactically different but definitionally equal types (e.g. via a `let`-bound function versus its unfolding) and confirm `#check` reports the same result for both, illustrating the uniqueness theorem experimentally against the kernel's actual behavior.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the typing judgment used, exhibit the full derivation tree, and explicitly justify, for each equality of types encountered, why the kernel treats the two sides as definitionally equal — an unjustified appeal to "definitional equality" is the most common flaw in student write-ups on this topic.

### curry_howard (39.4s)

## Curry Howard

**Definition.** The Curry–Howard correspondence identifies propositions with types and proofs with terms. For a proposition $P$ (in some logic $\mathcal{L}$) and a type $\tau_P$ (in some type system $\mathcal{T}$), there is a translation such that
$$P \text{ is provable in } \mathcal{L} \iff \exists\, t : \tau_P \text{ in } \mathcal{T},$$
and moreover the *proof term* $t$ is not merely a witness of provability — it *is* the proof, in the sense that checking $t : \tau_P$ (typechecking) and checking a derivation of $P$ (proof-checking) are the same decision procedure run on isomorphic data. The one new primitive this section fixes is the **function type** $\tau_P \to \tau_Q$, under which implication corresponds to function type: $P \to Q \;\leftrightarrow\; \tau_P \to \tau_Q$. (Conjunction, disjunction, and quantification extend this correspondence via further type formers — products, sums, and dependent $\Pi$/$\Sigma$ — but those are deferred to the literature context below rather than introduced here.)

**Theorem (Curry–Howard for implicational intuitionistic logic and simply typed $\lambda$-calculus).** There is a bijection, up to $\beta\eta$-equivalence, between normal-form natural deduction derivations of $P$ in the implicational fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic and normal-form typed $\lambda$-terms of type $\tau_P$ in the simply typed $\lambda$-calculus, compatible with the $\to$-introduction/elimination rules on one side and $\lambda$-abstraction/application on the other.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on derivations. $\to$-introduction (discharging a hypothesis) corresponds exactly to $\lambda$-abstraction; $\to$-elimination (modus ponens) corresponds to application. The correspondence is checked to preserve normalization: $\beta$-reduction on terms mirrors normalization (cut-elimination) of derivations — this is the content of the *Curry–Howard–Tait* observation that cut-elimination in logic and evaluation in computation are the same rewriting system. $\square$

**Literature context.** The correspondence originates with Curry's observation on combinatory logic and Howard's 1969 (published 1980) note *"The Formulae-as-Types Notion of Construction,"* which extends the implicational fragment above to full propositional logic by adding product types for conjunction and sum types for disjunction. Martin-Löf's intuitionistic type theory further extends it to first-order (and higher) quantification via dependent $\Pi$ and $\Sigma$ types, and Girard's linear logic and System F extend it to polymorphism and resource-sensitive logics under his *proofs-as-programs* slogan. The correspondence underlies proof assistants such as Coq, Agda, and Lean, where "proving a theorem" and "writing a program of that type" are literally the same kernel-checked act. Open questions remain active: extending Curry–Howard to classical logic (via control operators / continuations, Griffin's $\texttt{call/cc} \leftrightarrow$ double-negation elimination), to concurrency (session types vs. linear logic, still not a full correspondence), and to higher-order/homotopical logics (univalent foundations, where Voevodsky's Curry–Howard–*Univalence* extension is an active research frontier).

**Suggested investigation.** Implement, in Lean or Agda, the implicational fragment above: define a small logic with only $\to$, a corresponding simply typed system with only function types, and an explicit bidirectional translation function on derivations/terms. Then instrument SPL's own kernel-check step (proposition + type as a composed node) and verify empirically that its verification of a derivation is literally the typechecking algorithm — i.e., that no separate "logic-checking" code path exists.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the implication-to-function-type translation explicitly, give the induction proof of the bijection (or cite Howard precisely rather than restating informally), and report concretely on where the SPL kernel's proof-checking and type-checking code paths converge or diverge, with at least one worked example showing a single artifact serving as both a proof term and a program.

### function_types (42.2s)

## Function Types

**Definition.** Fix a simple type system with base types $\iota_1,\dots,\iota_k$ and a single binary type constructor $\to$. The set of types $\mathrm{Ty}$ is the smallest set containing the base types and closed under: if $A,B\in\mathrm{Ty}$ then $A\to B\in\mathrm{Ty}$. This is the one new primitive of the section: the function type itself, whose introduction and elimination rules are two faces of a single construct rather than separate primitives. A term of type $A\to B$ is introduced by abstraction, $\lambda x{:}A.\,e$ where $e:B$ given $x:A$ in context, and eliminated by application: if $f:A\to B$ and $a:A$ then $f\,a:B$. The typing rules are

$$
\frac{\Gamma,x{:}A \vdash e:B}{\Gamma \vdash \lambda x{:}A.\,e : A\to B}\ (\to\text{-I})
\qquad
\frac{\Gamma\vdash f:A\to B \quad \Gamma\vdash a:A}{\Gamma\vdash f\,a:B}\ (\to\text{-E})
$$

together with the computation rule (\emph{β-reduction}) $(\lambda x{:}A.\,e)\,a \rightsquigarrow e[a/x]$, which is definitional to what it means for $\lambda x{:}A.\,e$ and $f\,a$ to inhabit the *same* type rather than two unrelated constructs.

**Theorem (uniqueness of typing / subject reduction).** In the simply typed calculus, if $\Gamma\vdash e:A\to B$ and $e \rightsquigarrow e'$ under β-reduction, then $\Gamma\vdash e':A\to B$; moreover every well-typed term has a unique type derivable from $\Gamma$.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the derivation of $\Gamma\vdash e:A\to B$. The only nontrivial case is $(\to\text{-E})$ applied to a redex $(\lambda x{:}A.\,e_1)\,a$: by inversion of $(\to\text{-I})$, $\Gamma,x{:}A\vdash e_1:B$, and a substitution lemma (proved by a separate induction on the structure of $e_1$) gives $\Gamma\vdash e_1[a/x]:B$. Uniqueness follows because each syntactic form has exactly one applicable rule, so the type is determined by structural recursion on $e$. $\blacksquare$

**Literature context.** This is the $\to$-fragment of the simply typed lambda calculus, going back to Church (1940) and Curry–Feys; the Curry–Howard correspondence reads $A\to B$ as material implication, $(\to\text{-I})$ as conditional proof, $(\to\text{-E})$ as modus ponens. Subject reduction and strong normalization for this fragment are classical (Tait 1967). A further identification rule, η-expansion ($f \rightsquigarrow \lambda x{:}A.\,f\,x$ when $f:A\to B$), is often added to express extensionality of functions; it is deliberately deferred here rather than treated as a second primitive, since it changes the equational theory without altering the core typing discipline. What remains genuinely open at the research level is calibrating expressiveness against complexity: the fragment with only $\to$ and base types has decidable, PSPACE-complete type inhabitation (Statman 1979), and extending function types with polymorphism (System F) or dependent types ($\Pi$-types, subsuming $\to$ as the non-dependent case) changes both the proof theory and the decidability landscape in ways still actively studied in type-theoretic semantics and proof assistants (Coq, Agda, Lean).

**Suggested investigation.** Implement the typing and β-reduction rules above for a toy calculus with one base type $\iota$ and function types built from it. Prove strong normalization for this restricted case directly (via a reducibility/logical-relations argument à la Tait), rather than citing it, and measure empirically: generate random well-typed terms of increasing size and record reduction-sequence lengths to (informally) probe the gap between worst-case bounds and typical behavior.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the grammar and typing rules precisely, present the reducibility argument in full (defining the reducibility candidates $\mathrm{RED}_A$ used for the induction), report the normalization-length data with the experimental setup (term-generation method, size metric), and discuss how the bound compares to Statman's complexity result and to the dependent-type generalization.

### proof (40.9s)

## Proof

**Definition.** Fix a type universe with a distinguished sort $\mathsf{Prop}$ of propositions, following the propositions-as-types interpretation. For a proposition $P : \mathsf{Prop}$, a *proof* of $P$ is a term $p : P$ — an inhabitant of the type $P$ constructed by the usual term-formation rules (variables, $\lambda$-abstraction, application, constructors of inductive types, and so on). The judgment "$P$ has a proof" is definitionally the judgment "$P$ is inhabited," i.e. $\exists p.\, p : P$, and *this existential is not itself further checked semantically* — it is witnessed syntactically by exhibiting $p$.

**Theorem (Decidability of proof-checking).** Type checking — in particular checking $p : P$ for a given closed term $p$ and proposition $P$ — is decidable, provided the underlying type theory has decidable definitional equality.

*Proof.* Type checking reduces to type *synthesis* plus equality checking: synthesize a type $Q$ for $p$ by structural recursion on $p$'s syntax (each formation rule fixes the type of its output in terms of the types of its subterms, so synthesis terminates on well-formed syntax trees), then decide $Q \equiv P$ by the theory's conversion algorithm (typically normalization followed by syntactic comparison, or an algorithmic equality relation shown sound and complete with respect to definitional equality). Both steps terminate because $p$ is a finite term and conversion is decidable by hypothesis (e.g. strong normalization plus confluence, as established for the Calculus of Constructions by Coquand–Huet and for Martin-Löf type theory under suitable restrictions). $\blacksquare$

This is exactly the sense in which "the kernel checks a proof like any other term": there is no separate proof-checking algorithm distinct from type checking — verifying $p:P$ *is* type checking, and its tractability rests on the same decidability of conversion that governs checking any $t : T$.

**Literature context.** This is the Curry–Howard–de Bruijn correspondence, realized computationally in Automath (de Bruijn, 1968), the Calculus of Constructions (Coquand–Huet 1988), and its descendants (Lean, Coq/Rocq, Agda). The decidability theorem above is classical for CoC and for $\Pi\Sigma$-type theories with $\beta\eta$-conversion. It becomes delicate as production kernels extend the core calculus — Lean's and Coq's kernels add features whose interaction with normalization and confluence is the subject of ongoing metatheoretic study rather than settled fact; whether a given extension preserves decidable conversion is, in general, an open question to be checked feature by feature rather than assumed.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a minimal kernel for a fragment of CoC (function types, $\Pi$, and a $\mathsf{Prop}$ sort) supporting only synthesis and $\beta$-conversion checking. Prove termination of your type-synthesis function by structural induction on term syntax, then prove soundness of your conversion check against a reference (denotational or normalization-based) definitional equality. Pick one feature discussed in the literature you consult (chosen by you, not prescribed here) that is known to complicate this metatheory, and identify precisely where your termination or confluence argument breaks or must be strengthened once that feature is added.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the term syntax and typing rules precisely, give the termination argument for synthesis as an explicit induction, state and prove (or cite and adapt) the decidability-of-conversion result used, and — for the chosen extension — isolate the exact rule interaction responsible for any loss of decidability, with a minimal counterexample if one is found.

### theorem_statements (64.3s)

## Theorem Statements

**Definition.** A *theorem statement* is a closed type $T$ in a type theory (e.g., the simply typed $\lambda$-calculus, or a logical calculus reinterpreted as a type system). A *proof* of $T$ is a term $p$ such that the judgment $p : T$ is derivable from the theory's typing rules. Under the Curry–Howard correspondence, propositions are types and proofs are programs. The primitive instance of this correspondence, and the only one this section develops in full, is implication: a proposition $A \to B$ is identified with the function type $A \to B$, so that a proof of $A \to B$ is nothing other than a function taking proofs of $A$ to proofs of $B$. Other connectives — conjunction, disjunction, quantification — admit analogous type-theoretic readings (product types, sum types, dependent products and sums), but these are extensions of the same idea rather than new primitives, and are deferred to the literature discussion below. A theorem is thus fully specified — not merely gestured at — by writing down its type; "proving" it is the act of *inhabiting* that type with a well-typed term.

**Theorem (Curry–Howard, implicational fragment).** For the simply typed $\lambda$-calculus and the implicational fragment of intuitionistic propositional logic (formulas built from atoms and $\to$ alone), a formula $A$ is provable in natural deduction if and only if the corresponding type $\ulcorner A \urcorner$ is inhabited, i.e., there exists a closed term $t$ with $\vdash t : \ulcorner A \urcorner$.

*Proof.* By induction on derivations. The rule $\to\!\text{-intro}$ — hypothetical reasoning that discharges an assumption $A$ to conclude $A \to B$ — corresponds exactly to $\lambda$-abstraction: from a term $t$ with $x : A \vdash t : B$, form $\lambda x^A.\, t : A \to B$. The rule $\to\!\text{-elim}$ (modus ponens) corresponds to function application: given $t : A \to B$ and $u : A$, the term $t\,u : B$ is derivable. Conversely, every closed well-typed term in this fragment erases to a valid natural-deduction derivation by replaying these two correspondences backward: an abstraction erases to a discharged assumption, an application erases to a modus ponens step. $\blacksquare$

**Literature context.** The correspondence originates with Curry's observation on combinatory logic and Howard's 1969 manuscript (circulated widely, published 1980), which extended it to full intuitionistic natural deduction and the $\lambda$-calculus, adding product types for conjunction and sum types for disjunction. Girard independently developed System F, linking second-order logic to polymorphic types, and Martin-Löf type theory supplies the dependent products and sums ($\Pi$, $\Sigma$) needed for quantifiers. The frontier lies further out: classical logic requires control operators (Griffin 1990), linear logic requires substructural typing (Girard 1987), and Homotopy Type Theory (2013) identifies proofs of equality with paths, raising open questions about the computational content of univalence. Proof assistants built on this correspondence — Coq/Rocq, Agda, Lean — make "theorem statement := proof" a literal, machine-checked syntactic unit, which is the operational meaning intended in this section's framing.

**Suggested investigation.** Formalize the implicational fragment in Lean or Coq. State three theorems as function types — $A \to A \to A$, currying $(A \times B \to C) \to (A \to B \to C)$ using the library's built-in product type, and a nontrivial tautology of your choosing built from $\to$ alone — and construct each proof term by hand before invoking any automation tactic. Then look up how the library encodes conjunction and disjunction, and attempt to extend one statement to include $\vee$: observe where classical principles (excluded middle) are needed but not derivable intuitionistically, and diagnose the double-negation-elimination failure.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should present each type signature *before* its inhabiting term, state explicitly which typing rule — $\to$-intro or $\to$-elim — licenses each step of the term's construction, and include the one case where construction fails without classical axioms, with a precise explanation of which rule is unavailable and why, tying the obstruction back to the completeness boundary of intuitionistic vs. classical logic.

### tactic_mode (44.6s)

## Tactic Mode

**Definition.** Fix a proof assistant with a formal language of terms and types (e.g. the Calculus of Inductive Constructions in Coq/Rocq, or a dependent type theory in Lean/Isabelle). A *goal* is a sequent $\Gamma \vdash \tau$: a context $\Gamma$ of typed hypotheses and a target proposition (type) $\tau$ to inhabit. A *tactic* $t$ is a partial function from goals to lists of goals, $t : \Gamma \vdash \tau \rightharpoonup [\Gamma_1 \vdash \tau_1, \ldots, \Gamma_n \vdash \tau_n]$, together with a *justification*: a term-building procedure that, given proof terms $p_i : \tau_i$ for each subgoal, produces a term $p : \tau$ under $\Gamma$. Tactic mode is the interactive execution of a sequence $t_1; t_2; \ldots; t_k$ against an evolving list of open goals, terminating when the list is empty; the corresponding proof term is assembled compositionally by the justifications, bottom-up.

**Theorem (Soundness of tactic composition).** If each $t_i$ is sound — i.e., whenever $t_i$ succeeds on $\Gamma \vdash \tau$ producing subgoals $\Gamma_j \vdash \tau_j$, its justification is a valid derivation rule of the underlying type theory — then any completed tactic script yields a well-typed closed term $p$ with $\Gamma \vdash p : \tau$, by structural induction on the tree of tactic applications: the base case is a goal closed by an axiom or hypothesis lookup (a leaf term), and the inductive step composes justifications along each branch, mirroring the way natural-deduction proofs compose inference rules into a derivation tree.

This is precisely the "Curry–Howard behind the curtain": tactic mode is a *goal-directed* (backward) search procedure that constructs a term whose *type* is the theorem, while remaining faithful to a small, independently type-checked kernel. The system's trust does not rest on the tactic engine — only on the kernel's re-verification of $p$.

**Literature and open problems.** The paradigm traces to LCF (Milner, 1972) and its ML-based tactic combinators; Coq's tactic language (Ltac, and its typed successor Ltac2, Pédrot et al. 2020) and Lean 4's tactic monad (`TacticM`) are direct descendants. Open questions of active research: (1) *tactic robustness* — proof scripts break under refactoring of definitions; premise-selection and proof-repair tools (e.g. Blaauwbroek et al.) attempt automatic repair; (2) *learning tactics from corpora* — GPT-f, PACT, LeanDojo, and ReProver train models to predict the next tactic given a goal state, framing tactic mode as a sequential decision process; (3) *completeness/decidability* of tactic-generated fragments versus decision procedures (e.g. `omega`, `ring`) remains delicate when tactics interact with metavariables and higher-order unification.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a minimal LCF-style kernel (propositional logic suffices) with the primitives `intro`, `apply`, `exact`, and the sequential combinator `;`. Prove a small theorem (e.g. $(A \to B) \to (B \to C) \to A \to C$) by hand-writing the tactic script, then instrument the engine to print the goal list after each step. As a single, self-contained extension, add one new combinator, `repeat`, defined so that `repeat t` applies $t$ until it fails and then stops without emitting a term for the failing attempt; verify by induction on the number of repetitions that this failure-then-stop behavior preserves soundness (no partial or ill-typed term is ever produced). Resist the temptation to add further combinators (`try`, `orelse`, backtracking search) in the same pass — each introduces its own failure semantics and soundness obligation, and conflating them obscures which combinator's proof is doing the work.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should exhibit: the formal grammar of goals and tactics used; the induction argument for soundness of the specific tactic set implemented, including the added `repeat` combinator's termination and non-emission-on-failure argument; the full goal-state trace for the worked example; and a discussion of where the implementation's tactic language diverges from Ltac/Lean's (e.g., absence of metavariable unification, absence of `orelse`-style branching) and what that costs in expressiveness.

### core_tactics (44.5s)

## Core Tactics

**Definition.** Fix a proof state $\Sigma = \langle \Gamma \vdash \tau \rangle$: a local context $\Gamma$ of hypotheses and a goal type $\tau$, in a type theory with dependent products, inductive families, and Curry–Howard term extraction (CIC for Coq, CIC-with-inductive-families for Lean/Agda). We introduce a single new primitive, `apply`, and derive the remaining classical tactics from it. `apply` takes a term $f$ (possibly containing holes $\vec{?m}$) of type $\Pi \vec{x}{:}\vec{A}.\,C$, unifies $\tau$ against $C[\vec{x} := \vec{?m}]$, and — if unification succeeds — closes $\Sigma$ and emits one new goal $\Gamma \vdash A_i[\vec{?m}]$ per unresolved hole $?m_i$. A tactic script $t_1;\dots;t_n$ threads these hole-assignments through the monad's bind ($\mathrm{Tac} = \mathrm{TacticM}$ in Lean 4); closing all goals yields $\Gamma \vdash M : \tau$.

The other four "core" tactics are *specializations* of `apply` to canonical terms, not independent primitives: `intro` is `apply` to the canonical $\lambda$-abstraction shape when $\tau = \Pi x{:}A.\,B$, splitting it into a context extension $\Gamma,x{:}A$ and residual goal $B$; `exact e` is `apply e` with zero holes; `constructor` is `apply c_j$ for each constructor $c_j : \Pi\vec{y}.\,D\,\vec{a}$ of the inductive $D$ headed by the goal, trying $j$ in turn; `cases h` is `apply` to the eliminator $D_{\mathrm{elim}}$ (Lean 4's `casesOn`/`brecOn`) instantiated at $h : D\,\vec{a}$, which replaces $\tau$ by one goal per constructor with $h$ substituted by the matching pattern.

**Theorem (soundness of tactic composition).** If `apply`'s unification step only ever produces well-typed instantiations — i.e., succeeds only when $\tau \equiv C[\vec{?m}]$ is kernel-checkable after hole resolution — then any script built from `apply` and its four derived forms produces, on total success, a well-typed term $M$. *Proof sketch:* by induction on script length $n$; the base case is `apply`'s definition, which routes through elaboration with metavariable unification rather than judgment-forging, so no tactic bypasses the kernel. Each derived tactic inherits soundness because it is `apply` applied to a term already well-typed in the ambient signature (a constructor, an eliminator, a $\lambda$-introduction scheme). Composite soundness thus reduces to kernel type-checking of the final $M$, per the de Bruijn criterion.

**Literature context.** `apply`'s unification is higher-order, restricted to Miller patterns for decidability (Miller 1991); the eliminator instance underlying `cases`/`constructor` is the one derived automatically for inductive families (Dybjer 1994; Coquand's inductive-recursive schemas). Open: optimal backtracking when `apply` leaves multiple metavariable orderings (the "unification vs. later goals" tension formalized via Lean 4's `synthetic opaque` metavariables), and whether `apply` plus `rewrite` is a complete basis for a well-defined fragment of dependent pattern-matching goals — known for propositional fragments, open for indexed families.

**Suggested investigation.** Build a toy tactic monad over a minimal core (Π, one inductive family) implementing only `apply`; derive `intro`, `exact`, `constructor`, `cases` as macros over it and prove the soundness theorem by induction on scripts. Then instrument a Coq/Lean corpus (mathlib) to measure what fraction of goal closures factor through bare `apply`-instances versus `simp`/`omega`/`ring`.

**Report-writing note.** State `apply`'s operational semantics as an inference rule on $\Sigma$, give the derivations of the other four tactics explicitly as instantiated `apply` calls, prove soundness in full, and report the empirical frequency histogram with discussion of whether it reflects search complexity or corpus style.

## Equality Rfl

**Definition.** In dependent type theory (Martin-Löf, Coq, Agda, Lean), equality is not a proposition external to the type theory but itself a type. For a type $A$ and terms $a, b : A$, the identity type $\mathrm{Eq}\,a\,b$ (written $a = b$ or $a =_A b$) is defined by a single constructor:
$$\mathrm{refl} : \forall (a : A),\ a = a.$$
The term $\mathrm{refl}$ — often abbreviated $\mathtt{rfl}$ in tactic languages — is the canonical proof that a term equals itself, produced not by inspection of syntax but by the *definitional* (judgmental) equality of the two sides after full reduction. Thus $\mathtt{rfl} : a = b$ typechecks exactly when $a$ and $b$ share a common normal form under the theory's reduction rules ($\beta$, $\iota$, $\delta$, and computation on primitive recursors).

**Theorem (decidability of rfl-provability for closed numerals).** For closed terms $m, n$ built from $\mathrm{Nat}$ constructors ($\mathrm{zero}$, $\mathrm{succ}$) and primitive recursion (e.g. $+$), the proposition $m = n$ is provable by $\mathtt{rfl}$ iff $m$ and $n$ reduce to the same numeral, and this is decidable.

*Proof.* Addition on $\mathrm{Nat}$ is defined by recursion on the second argument: $a + 0 := a$, $a + \mathrm{succ}(b) := \mathrm{succ}(a + b)$. Since $\mathrm{succ}$ and $\mathrm{zero}$ are constructors (no further reduction applies to them), every closed $\mathrm{Nat}$-term reduces, by strong normalization of the calculus (Girard–Tait), to a unique numeral $\mathrm{succ}^k(\mathrm{zero})$. Hence $2 + 2$ reduces: $2+2 \to 2 + \mathrm{succ}(1) \to \mathrm{succ}(2+1) \to \mathrm{succ}(\mathrm{succ}(2+0)) \to \mathrm{succ}(\mathrm{succ}(2)) = 4$. Since $2+2$ and $4$ share the normal form $\mathrm{succ}^4(\mathrm{zero})$, the term $\mathrm{refl}_4$ has type $2+2=4$ by the typing rule for $\mathrm{refl}$ applied at the shared normal form, and conversion (which allows typing up to definitional equality) transports this to a proof of $2+2=4$. Decidability follows because reduction to normal form is a terminating, syntax-directed procedure. $\blacksquare$

**Literature context.** This account is the *intensional* identity type of Martin-Löf (1975/1984). A foundational open question is what proofs of $a = b$ can exist beyond $\mathrm{refl}$ when $a, b$ are not judgmentally equal — the uniqueness-of-identity-proofs question, shown to fail in general by Hofmann and Streicher's groupoid model (1994), and reinterpreted geometrically by the univalent foundations program (Voevodsky et al., *HoTT Book*, 2013). We do not develop that machinery here; it is flagged only as the natural next reading once $\mathrm{refl}$ itself is understood. On the computational side, whether decidable rfl-checking scales to richer recursion schemes (large elimination, inductive-inductive types) remains an active question in kernels such as Lean 4 and Coq.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a small $\mathrm{Nat}$ kernel with $\beta/\iota$-reduction and a `check_rfl : Term → Term → Bool` function testing whether two terms share a normal form. Benchmark reduction-step count for $m+n$ versus $n+m$ (definitionally *not* equal by rfl alone, since $+$ recurses on the right argument) — this exposes why commutativity of addition requires induction, not rfl.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the reduction rules used, exhibit the explicit reduction sequence establishing $2+2 \equiv 4$, and explain — with a concrete counterexample such as $a+0$ vs. $0+a$ — why definitional equality is asymmetric in computational cost despite being propositionally symmetric, motivating the need for propositional (proof-carrying) equality beyond rfl.

## Math Education Games

**Definition.** A *proof assistant game* is built from ordinary objects the reader already knows — a finite poset $(W, \prec)$ of "worlds" (levels), a set $T$ of tactics with an unlock map $\tau: W \to \mathcal{P}(T)$, and a goal type $G_w$ attached to each world — plus exactly one genuinely new primitive: a *kernel* $K$, a type-checker for a fixed formal system (typically dependent type theory) that decides, in finite time, whether a purported proof term $p$ inhabits $G_w$. Write $\mathcal{G} = (W, \prec, T, \tau, K)$ for the resulting game. Progress is monotone — $w$ is completable only once every $w' \prec w$ is completed — and completion of $w$ is graded not by a human or heuristic but by $K(p, G_w) \in \{\texttt{accept}, \texttt{reject}\}$. The paradigm case is the Natural Number Game (Buzzard–Massot, built on Lean's kernel), which stages Peano arithmetic — $\text{add\_zero}$, $\text{succ\_inj}$, $\text{mul\_comm}$ — as a sequence of worlds culminating in a hand-built induction principle.

**Theorem (soundness transfer).** If $K$ is sound for the ambient type theory (a proof term is accepted only if it genuinely inhabits its type), then completion of $\mathcal{G}$ certifies that the player has *constructed*, not merely *stated*, a chain of theorems $T_{w_1}, \dots, T_{w_n}$ respecting $\prec$, each depending only on kernel-verified predecessors.

*Proof.* By induction on $\prec$. Base worlds have $G_w$ discharged by axioms or already-verified library lemmas, so acceptance is immediate. For non-minimal $w$, the tactic state exposed by $\tau$ can only invoke lemmas from $\{T_{w'} : w' \prec w\}$, each already $K$-accepted by inductive hypothesis; any $p$ built from these and accepted by $K$ is therefore itself sound by soundness of $K$ applied to the composite term. $\square$

This is the formal content behind the informal slogan "instant kernel feedback is the most patient tutor": the feedback loop is not an approximation of correctness (as a multiple-choice grader is) but a decision procedure for it, with zero false positives when $K$ is sound.

**Literature context.** This design descends from Lean's game engine (`lean4game`) and precedent efforts — Coq's `mathcomp` exercises, Isabelle's Isar-based teaching packs — but the *gamification* layer (worlds, unlockable tactics, narrative framing) is comparatively recent and understudied empirically. What is well established: type-theoretic kernels are small trusted cores (de Bruijn criterion), so $K$'s soundness is far more auditable than a human grader's. What is open: (i) no controlled study yet measures transfer from tactic-game fluency to unassisted informal proof-writing; (ii) the design space of $\tau$ (tactic unlock ordering) as a *curriculum-scheduling problem* is unformalized — it resembles knowledge-tracing in intelligent tutoring systems but has not been connected to that literature; (iii) whether game-based induction training reduces the well-documented novice difficulty with the induction step specifically (as opposed to general proof-writing) is unmeasured.

**Suggested investigation.** Pick a small induction-heavy fragment (e.g., list-reversal lemmas) and build a two-world game around it in Lean or Coq. Instrument $K$ to log attempt sequences (rejected proof terms, time-to-accept per world). Formalize $\tau$-ordering as a DAG-scheduling problem minimizing expected time-to-completion under a simple cognitive-load cost model, and compare the game's actual unlock order against your optimized schedule.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state precisely which type theory and kernel were used (with version), report acceptance/rejection logs as raw data (not summary statistics only), justify the cost model chosen for scheduling optimization, and explicitly separate the soundness claim (proved above, kernel-guaranteed) from the pedagogical-efficacy claim (empirical, requires the logged data to support).
