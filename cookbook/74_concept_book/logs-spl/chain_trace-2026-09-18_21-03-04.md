## Assassination 1914

Picture this: a young man, Gavrilo Princip, stands on a street corner in Sarajevo. He is not a general. He is not a king. He has a gun and a plan. When Archduke Franz Ferdinand's car takes a wrong turn and stops right in front of him, Princip fires — and an empire's heir falls. Why does one bullet matter so much? Because empires in 1914 were connected by promises: "If you are attacked, I will fight for you." This is called an alliance. Austria-Hungary was linked to Germany; Serbia was linked to Russia; Russia was linked to France. So one small, local act — a single shot — pulled on a thread that ran through every capital in Europe. Think of it like a row of dominoes standing very close together: you only need to tip the first one. This is the spark of the July Crisis, the moment before the fire.

**Now you try:** Draw five boxes in a row, one for each country: Serbia, Austria-Hungary, Germany, Russia, France. Connect boxes that are "allies" with a line. Now knock over the Serbia box — trace which lines make the next boxes fall too.

## War Declarations 1914

Imagine you and your best friend made a pact: "If anyone ever picks on you, I'll jump in and fight too." Sounds loyal, right? Now imagine *everyone* in your whole school made pacts like that — you with one friend, they with another, that friend with someone else — until the entire school was secretly linked by promises. Then one kid pushes another kid. Suddenly, because of all those pacts, the *whole school* is in a brawl, even kids who weren't mad at anyone!

That's what happened in 1914. Austria-Hungary was angry at Serbia and declared war. But Serbia had a friend, Russia, who had promised to help. Russia had a friend, France. France had a friend, Britain. Germany had promised to help Austria-Hungary. One by one, each country kept its promise — and within weeks, a quarrel between two countries pulled almost all of Europe into war.

$$
\text{one conflict} + \text{chain of alliances} \rightarrow \text{continent-wide war}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw five stick-figure friends holding hands in a circle, each promising to defend the next. What happens if just *one* of them gets into a fight?

## Schlieffen Plan 1914

Picture a chess player who plans every move for the next ten turns — but forgets that the other player also gets to move. That was Germany's problem in 1914. Their generals had a plan drawn up years earlier: rush west through Belgium, swing around Paris like a door closing, defeat France in about six weeks, then rush east to fight Russia before it was ready. It depended on everything happening exactly on schedule.

But armies marching through Belgium got tired. Belgium fought back harder than expected. France and Britain moved faster than expected. At the Marne River, just short of Paris, French and British troops stopped the German advance and pushed it back.

The lesson: a plan that only works if nothing goes wrong is a fragile plan. When soldiers dug trenches instead of retreating, "fast war" became years of stalemate.

**Now you try:** Think of a plan you made that needed everything to go perfectly. What happened when one small thing changed?
