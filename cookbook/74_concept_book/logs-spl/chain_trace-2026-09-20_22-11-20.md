## China 1945 Resumption Civil War

When Japan surrendered in August 1945, China's two rival power centers — Chiang Kai-shek's Kuomintang (KMT) and Mao Zedong's Chinese Communist Party (CCP) — had spent eight years in an uneasy wartime alliance held together only by the shared enemy. That enemy was gone. What remained was an unresolved question dating back to the 1920s: which party would govern China. Both sides distrusted the other's territorial gains from the Japanese collapse, and both raced to occupy former Japanese-held cities, especially in Manchuria, where the USSR had seized Japanese arms and infrastructure and quietly allowed CCP forces access to them.

American-brokered talks in late 1945 (the Marshall Mission) produced a nominal truce and coalition proposals, but neither side had any intention of sharing power. The KMT held the cities, the formal government seat, and U.S. support; the CCP held the countryside, peasant loyalty built through land-reform promises, and a disciplined guerrilla-trained army. By mid-1946, skirmishes escalated into open war.

The pattern that decided the outcome is worth tracing as a case study in how military and political factors compound each other rather than acting independently. The KMT initially held numerical and material superiority — more troops, U.S. equipment, and control of major cities — yet its position eroded steadily between 1947 and 1949. Three reinforcing failures explain this: hyperinflation devastated the currency and turned urban populations against the government; corruption and patronage weakened army morale and command; and rigid urban-garrison strategy let the CCP encircle and starve out KMT strongholds one by one, most decisively in the Huai-Hai campaign of late 1948, which destroyed the KMT's best remaining armies.

Apply this to a general problem: predicting the outcome of an internal conflict from asymmetric strengths. A useful exercise is to list, for either side in any civil conflict, which advantages are convertible into sustained control (loyal administration, functioning currency, popular legitimacy) versus which are merely momentary (a captured city, a foreign shipment of arms). The Chinese case shows that momentary military advantages collapse quickly when the underlying administrative and economic base is failing — a pattern also visible in other 20th-century civil wars. By late 1949, the CCP controlled the mainland; the KMT government retreated to Taiwan, and the People's Republic of China was proclaimed on October 1, 1949.
