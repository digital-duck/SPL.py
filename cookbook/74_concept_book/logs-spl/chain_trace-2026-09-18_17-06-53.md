### measurement (0.0s)

## Measurement

Measurement is the quantitative determination of a physical property, expressed as a numerical value paired with a unit. Every measurement takes one of two forms: a **direct measurement**, in which an instrument compares the property to a standard (using a ruler to find length, a balance to find mass), or a **derived measurement**, in which the value is calculated from other measured quantities using a known relationship. Density, for instance, is never read directly off a device — it is computed from measured mass and volume via $\rho = m/V$.

**Worked example.** Suppose you measure the mass of a metal block as $m = 47.6\ \text{g}$ using a balance, and its dimensions as $2.0\ \text{cm} \times 3.0\ \text{cm} \times 4.0\ \text{cm}$ using calipers. The volume is a derived quantity:

$$
V = 2.0 \times 3.0 \times 4.0 = 24\ \text{cm}^3
$$

The density is then a second derived quantity, built from the first:

$$
\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{47.6\ \text{g}}{24\ \text{cm}^3} \approx 1.98\ \text{g/cm}^3
$$

Comparing this to a reference table, you'd identify the metal as magnesium ($\rho \approx 1.74$–$1.98\ \text{g/cm}^3$ depending on alloy), illustrating how a chain of measurements supports an inference.

**Problem-solving application.** A crucial practical skill is tracking how uncertainty propagates through derived measurements. If each caliper reading carries an uncertainty of $\pm 0.1\ \text{cm}$, that uncertainty does not vanish when the dimensions are multiplied — it compounds. For a quantity $Q$ computed as a product or quotient of measured values, the relative uncertainties add:

$$
\frac{\delta Q}{Q} \approx \frac{\delta x}{x} + \frac{\delta y}{y} + \frac{\delta z}{z}
$$

Here each dimension has a relative uncertainty around $0.1/2.0 = 5\%$ to $0.1/4.0 = 2.5\%$, so the volume's relative uncertainty is roughly their sum — about $10\%$ — meaning the reported density should be written as $1.98 \pm 0.20\ \text{g/cm}^3$, not as a bare number. This is the discipline measurement demands: a value without a stated precision is scientifically incomplete, because it hides whether an apparent difference between two measurements is real or merely instrument noise. Recognizing when a derived result is trustworthy — and when it merely reflects accumulated measurement error — is a skill that recurs across every quantitative science.

### physical_quantity (0.0s)

## Physical Quantity

A physical quantity is any property of an object or system that can be assigned a numerical value together with a unit — obtained either by direct measurement (using an instrument) or by calculation from other measured quantities. Length, mass, time, and temperature are directly measured; density, velocity, and kinetic energy are calculated. What makes something a *physical* quantity, rather than just a number, is this pairing of magnitude and unit: "5" means nothing on its own, but "5 meters" specifies a measurable extent in space. Physical quantities fall into two categories: **base quantities** (length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity — the seven SI base units) and **derived quantities**, built from base ones through algebraic combination.

**Worked example.** Suppose you measure a rectangular plate's length as $L = 12.0\ \text{cm}$ and width as $W = 5.0\ \text{cm}$, each read directly from a ruler — these are measurements. The plate's area, however, is not measured directly; it is calculated:

$$A = L \times W = 12.0\ \text{cm} \times 5.0\ \text{cm} = 60.0\ \text{cm}^2$$

Area is a derived physical quantity: it has a numerical value (60.0), a unit ($\text{cm}^2$), and it was obtained by combining two measurements through a defined relationship. If you further weigh the plate and find its mass to be $m = 24.0\ \text{g}$, you can calculate area density, $\sigma = m/A = 0.40\ \text{g/cm}^2$ — a quantity derived from two others in turn.

**Problem-solving application.** When solving a physics or engineering problem, the first useful step is to identify which physical quantities are given (with their units), which are unknown, and what relationship (formula) connects them — then check that the units on both sides of any equation match, a technique called dimensional analysis. For instance, if a problem asks for kinetic energy, $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, recognizing that mass is in kilograms and velocity in meters per second tells you the result will carry units of $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, which is defined as the joule. Catching a units mismatch this way — before finishing a calculation — is one of the fastest ways to catch a wrong formula or a wrong conversion, and it is why every physical quantity, measured or derived, must always be reported with its unit attached.

### units (0.0s)

## Units

A unit is a standardized reference amount used to express the magnitude of a physical quantity — a number by itself, like "9.8," is meaningless until paired with a unit like $\text{m/s}^2$ that tells you what was measured and against what standard. Every physical quantity (length, mass, time, force, energy) requires a unit to be communicated unambiguously, and modern science relies on the International System of Units (SI), which defines seven base units: the meter (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, ampere (A) for electric current, kelvin (K) for temperature, mole (mol) for amount of substance, and candela (cd) for luminous intensity. All other units, called derived units, are built from these — for example, velocity is $\text{m/s}$, force (the newton, N) is $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$, and energy (the joule, J) is $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$.

Consider a worked example: a car accelerates at $2\ \text{m/s}^2$ for $5\ \text{s}$, and you want the distance traveled. Using $d = \frac{1}{2}at^2$, you get $d = \frac{1}{2}(2\ \text{m/s}^2)(5\ \text{s})^2 = 25\ \text{m}$. Notice that the units themselves multiply and cancel: $\text{m/s}^2 \times \text{s}^2 = \text{m}$, confirming the answer is a length. This is not a coincidence — it reflects the principle of dimensional consistency, which states that any physically valid equation must have matching units on both sides.

This principle turns into a powerful problem-solving tool called dimensional analysis: if you're unsure whether an equation is set up correctly, check whether the units on the left and right sides reduce to the same thing. For instance, if a formula for kinetic energy gave you units of $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$ instead of $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, you'd immediately know something was wrong, without needing to know the "correct" formula in advance. Dimensional analysis is also the basis for unit conversion: multiplying by conversion factors written as fractions equal to 1 (e.g., $\frac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}}$) lets you cancel unwanted units and produce the ones you need, a technique essential in physics, chemistry, and engineering whenever quantities measured in different systems must be combined.

### fundamental_units (0.0s)

## Fundamental Units

A fundamental unit is not derived from any other unit — it is defined directly by a physical procedure or a fixed physical constant. Every other measurement in science ultimately traces back to a small set of these base definitions. This text works with four: the meter ($\text{m}$, length), the kilogram ($\text{kg}$, mass), the second ($\text{s}$, time), and the ampere ($\text{A}$, electric current). Since 2019, all four have been redefined in terms of invariant constants of nature rather than physical artifacts: the second is fixed by a specific number of oscillations of a cesium-133 atom, the meter by the distance light travels in a fraction of a second, the kilogram by fixing the value of Planck's constant $h$, and the ampere by fixing the elementary charge $e$. This matters practically — a kilogram defined by a constant of nature is reproducible in any properly equipped lab, whereas the old reference cylinder in France could drift in mass over time.

**Worked example.** Suppose a physics problem gives you a force in newtons and asks you to verify the units are consistent with $F = ma$. A newton is *not* fundamental — it's derived: $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \text{s}^{-2}$. To check dimensional consistency, express every quantity in the problem in terms of $\text{kg}$, $\text{m}$, $\text{s}$, and $\text{A}$ only, then confirm both sides of the equation reduce to the same combination. If a claimed formula gives you $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \text{s}^{-2}$ on one side and $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} \cdot \text{s}^{-2}$ on the other, the formula is wrong — no numerical check needed.

**Problem-solving application.** Dimensional analysis using fundamental units is a fast error-check for any equation you derive or half-remember. Given a quantity like electric power, $P = IV$, decompose it: current $I$ is fundamental ($\text{A}$), and voltage $V$ derives to $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \text{s}^{-3} \cdot \text{A}^{-1}$. Multiplying gives $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \text{s}^{-3}$, which is indeed the fundamental-unit expansion of the watt. Before trusting a formula on an exam or in a lab report, reduce both sides to $\text{m}$, $\text{kg}$, $\text{s}$, $\text{A}$ — if they don't match, you've caught a mistake before it costs you a wrong answer.

### derived_units (11.2s)

## Derived Units

Not every physical quantity has its own base unit. Instead, most quantities are described using derived units — combinations of the seven fundamental SI units (meter, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela) formed through multiplication and division according to the physical definition of the quantity. Because a derived unit's algebraic structure directly reflects the physics it measures, tracking units through a calculation is not bookkeeping — it is a built-in check on whether an equation can possibly be correct.

Consider speed, defined as distance traveled divided by time elapsed. If distance is measured in meters and time in seconds, then speed inherits the unit $\text{m/s}$, or equivalently $\mathrm{m \cdot s^{-1}}$. This is a derived unit: it did not require a new definition, only the ratio of two existing ones. Acceleration extends this further — as the rate of change of velocity with respect to time, it carries the unit $\mathrm{m/s^2}$, or $\mathrm{m \cdot s^{-2}}$.

This structure enables a powerful problem-solving tool called dimensional analysis: checking that both sides of an equation reduce to the same combination of base units. For example, consider Newton's second law:

$$
F = ma
$$

Force is mass times acceleration, so its derived unit is $\mathrm{kg \cdot m \cdot s^{-2}}$. Rather than write this combination out repeatedly, we give it its own name — the newton, $\mathrm{N}$, defined by $1\ \mathrm{N} = 1\ \mathrm{kg \cdot m \cdot s^{-2}}$.

Dimensional analysis lets you catch errors before you ever check a numerical answer. Suppose a formula claims that kinetic energy is $E = \tfrac{1}{2}mv$. Checking units: mass ($\mathrm{kg}$) times velocity ($\mathrm{m/s}$) gives $\mathrm{kg \cdot m/s}$ — the unit of momentum, not energy ($\mathrm{J} = \mathrm{kg \cdot m^2 \cdot s^{-2}}$). The mismatch immediately signals that the formula is wrong; the correct relation, $E = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, produces $\mathrm{kg \cdot m^2/s^2} = \mathrm{J}$, matching correctly.

This same technique generalizes: whenever you derive a new physical relationship — in mechanics, electromagnetism, or chemistry — verifying that the units on both sides reduce to the same base-unit combination is a fast, reliable test of internal consistency, often revealing algebra mistakes long before any numbers are plugged in.
