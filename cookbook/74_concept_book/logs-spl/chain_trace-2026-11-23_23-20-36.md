### fluid_density (0.6s)

## Fluid Density

Fluid density, denoted $\rho$, is the mass per unit volume of the medium through which an object moves — air, water, or any other gas or liquid:

$$
\rho = \frac{m}{V}
$$

with SI units $\text{kg/m}^3$. Density is a property of the fluid itself, not of the moving object, and it is the variable that couples the surrounding medium to the strength of drag. Air at sea level has $\rho \approx 1.225\ \text{kg/m}^3$; water has $\rho \approx 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$ — nearly 800 times denser. This ratio explains why the same object falling through water decelerates far more abruptly than one falling through air.

Density enters the quadratic drag force directly:

$$
F_d = \tfrac{1}{2}\, \rho\, C_d\, A\, v^2
$$

where $C_d$ is the drag coefficient, $A$ is the cross-sectional area, and $v$ is the object's speed relative to the fluid. Because $F_d$ is *linear* in $\rho$, doubling the fluid's density doubles the drag force at any given speed — no exponent, no threshold effect, just direct proportionality.

**Worked example.** A sphere with $C_d = 0.5$, $A = 0.01\ \text{m}^2$, moving at $v = 20\ \text{m/s}$, first through air then through water:

- In air: $F_d = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times 0.5 \times 0.01 \times 20^2 \approx 1.23\ \text{N}$
- In water: $F_d = 0.5 \times 1000 \times 0.5 \times 0.01 \times 20^2 \approx 1000\ \text{N}$

The force in water is roughly 800 times larger — exactly the ratio of the two densities, since every other factor is unchanged. This is why terminal velocity in water is so much lower than in air for the same object.

**Problem-solving application.** When setting up a drag problem, always identify $\rho$ from the physical scenario before touching the rest of the formula: standard tables give $\rho_{\text{air}} \approx 1.225\ \text{kg/m}^3$ at sea level (decreasing with altitude), $\rho_{\text{water}} \approx 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$ for fresh water, and $\rho_{\text{seawater}} \approx 1025\ \text{kg/m}^3$. A common error is treating density as constant across a tall altitude range in atmospheric-reentry or skydiving problems — in those cases $\rho$ itself becomes a function of height, and the drag force equation must be solved as a differential equation rather than evaluated at a single point.

### conservation_of_energy (23.2s)

## Conservation Of Energy

**Definition.** In a closed system, with no energy entering or leaving, the total energy stays constant:

$$E_{\text{total}} = K + U + E_{\text{other}} = \text{constant}.$$

Energy changes form (kinetic $K$, potential $U$, thermal, and so on) but is never created or destroyed. When non-conservative forces such as friction act, the energy lost from the mechanical terms appears as thermal energy. If external work $W_{\text{ext}}$ is done on the system, the statement becomes

$$\Delta K + \Delta U + \Delta E_{\text{thermal}} = W_{\text{ext}}.$$

If $W_{\text{ext}}=0$ and no thermal energy is produced, mechanical energy $K+U$ is conserved by itself.

**Worked example.** A 2.0 kg ball is dropped from rest at height $h = 5.0\ \text{m}$. Find its speed just before impact, taking $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ and ignoring air resistance.

Take the ground as $U=0$. At the top, $K_i = 0$ and $U_i = mgh$. At the bottom, $U_f = 0$ and $K_f = \tfrac12 mv^2$. Conservation gives

$$mgh = \tfrac12 m v^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(5.0)} \approx 9.9\ \text{m/s}.$$

The mass cancels, so any object falls with the same speed. The energy method needs no knowledge of the time of fall or the forces along the way, which is why it is so efficient.

**Problem-solving application.** A block of mass $m = 1.5\ \text{kg}$ slides down a frictionless ramp from height $1.2\ \text{m}$, then crosses a rough horizontal patch of length $d = 2.0\ \text{m}$ with kinetic friction coefficient $\mu_k = 0.20$. What is its speed after the patch?

1. *Choose the system and states.* Initial: at rest at the top. Final: just after the patch.
2. *Account for non-conservative work.* Friction converts energy to heat: $E_{\text{thermal}} = \mu_k m g d$.
3. *Write the balance:*
$$mgh = \tfrac12 m v^2 + \mu_k m g d.$$
4. *Solve:* the mass cancels, so
$$v = \sqrt{2g(h-\mu_k d)} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(1.2-0.40)} \approx 4.0\ \text{m/s}.$$

**Extension: a fluid parcel.** The same bookkeeping applies to flowing fluid. Consider a small parcel of incompressible fluid of density $\rho$ moving along a streamline with no thermal losses. Per unit volume its energy is kinetic, $\tfrac12\rho v^2$, plus gravitational potential, $\rho g y$. If the parcel's speed or height changes with no external work and no heat, then $\tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho g y$ would be conserved, exactly as $K+U$ was for the ball. The one thing a flowing fluid adds is that neighboring fluid does work on the parcel through pressure, so the pressure $P$ must be included in the balance. This is the starting point for Bernoulli's equation, which you will derive in the next section.

**Strategy summary.** Define the system, pick a zero for potential energy, list all energy forms at the initial and final states, include any heat or external work, then solve the single balance equation.

### gravitational_potential_energy (12.6s)

## Gravitational Potential Energy

**Definition.** Gravitational potential energy (GPE) is the energy a system stores because of the position of a mass in a gravitational field. Near Earth's surface, where the gravitational field strength $g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ is nearly constant, an object of mass $m$ at height $h$ above a chosen reference level has

$$U_g = mgh$$

The units are joules (J). The formula follows from work: lifting an object at constant speed requires an upward force $F = mg$ through a distance $h$, so the work done against gravity is $W = Fh = mgh$. That work is stored as $U_g$.

Two features matter in practice:

1. **Only changes matter.** The zero of height is a free choice (floor, table, sea level). Physical results depend on $\Delta U_g = mg\,\Delta h$, which is the same whichever reference you pick.
2. **Path independence.** Gravity is a conservative force. $\Delta U_g$ depends only on the vertical change in height, not on the route taken. Carrying a box up a ramp or straight up a ladder to the same height stores the same energy.

For large distances, where $g$ is no longer constant, the general form is $U_g = -\dfrac{GMm}{r}$, with zero energy at infinite separation. Near the surface, $mgh$ is an excellent approximation of its changes.

**Worked example.** A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ book is lifted from the floor to a shelf $1.5\ \text{m}$ high. Taking the floor as $h = 0$:

$$\Delta U_g = (2.0)(9.8)(1.5) = 29.4\ \text{J}$$

If the origin were instead the table at $0.75\ \text{m}$, the book's energy on the shelf would be $(2.0)(9.8)(0.75) = 14.7\ \text{J}$. But its change in energy from the floor is still $29.4\ \text{J}$, because the book's starting energy relative to the table is $-14.7\ \text{J}$.

**Problem-solving application.** Combine GPE with conservation of mechanical energy. A $0.50\ \text{kg}$ ball is dropped from rest at $h = 20\ \text{m}$. Find its speed just before it hits the ground, ignoring air resistance.

Energy is conserved, so $U_i + K_i = U_f + K_f$:

$$mgh + 0 = 0 + \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$$

The mass cancels:

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(20)} \approx 19.8\ \text{m/s}$$

Notice the strategy: choose the reference level at the lowest point so $U_f = 0$, write energy at two moments, and solve for the unknown. The same method handles roller coasters, pendulums, and ramps. Only the vertical drop matters, not the shape of the track. If friction acts, subtract the energy it dissipates: $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + W_{\text{friction}}$.

**Check yourself.** If the ball were dropped from $40\ \text{m}$, would its speed double? No. Since $v \propto \sqrt{h}$, it increases by a factor of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$.

### incompressible_fluid (12.4s)

## Incompressible Fluid

An **incompressible fluid** is one whose density $\rho$ stays constant no matter how the pressure changes. Real liquids such as water are only approximately incompressible, but the model works very well for liquids at ordinary pressures, and for gases moving well below the speed of sound.

### Consequence: the continuity equation

Because density cannot change, a given volume of fluid cannot be squeezed into a smaller space. The same volume must therefore pass every point along a pipe in the same time. For a pipe with cross-sectional area $A$ and flow speed $v$, the **volume flow rate** is

$$Q = A v \quad (\text{m}^3/\text{s}).$$

For steady flow with no leaks or branches, $Q$ is the same at every cross-section:

$$A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2.$$

This is the **continuity equation**. It is mass conservation, $\rho A_1 v_1 = \rho A_2 v_2$, with the constant $\rho$ cancelled. A narrower pipe gives a faster flow, and a wider pipe gives a slower one.

In differential form, incompressibility means the velocity field $\mathbf{v}$ has zero divergence:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0.$$

Fluid is neither created nor destroyed at any point, and none piles up there.

### Worked example

Water flows through a pipe of diameter $8.0\ \text{cm}$ at $1.5\ \text{m/s}$. The pipe narrows to a diameter of $4.0\ \text{cm}$. Find the speed in the narrow section.

Area scales with the square of the diameter, so

$$\frac{A_1}{A_2} = \left(\frac{8.0}{4.0}\right)^2 = 4.$$

Then

$$v_2 = v_1 \frac{A_1}{A_2} = (1.5)(4) = 6.0\ \text{m/s}.$$

Halving the diameter quadruples the speed.

### Problem-solving application

Incompressibility also lets us link speed to pressure. Bernoulli's equation for steady, frictionless flow along a streamline is

$$P + \tfrac12 \rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant}.$$

Suppose the pipe above is horizontal, with pressure $P_1 = 200\ \text{kPa}$ in the wide section. Take $\rho = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and use $v_1 = 1.5$ and $v_2 = 6.0\ \text{m/s}$. With $h$ constant,

$$P_2 = P_1 + \tfrac12\rho\,(v_1^2 - v_2^2) = 200{,}000 + 500\,(2.25 - 36) \approx 183\ \text{kPa}.$$

The pressure drops by about $17\ \text{kPa}$ where the fluid speeds up.

**Strategy for these problems:**

1. Use continuity ($A_1v_1 = A_2v_2$) to find unknown speeds.
2. Use Bernoulli's equation to find pressures or heights.
3. Check the assumptions: steady flow, negligible viscosity, and constant $\rho$.

### Check your understanding

A garden hose of inner radius $9\ \text{mm}$ delivers water at $0.5\ \text{m/s}$. If you partly cover the opening so the exit has radius $3\ \text{mm}$, the exit speed is

$$v = 0.5 \left(\frac{9}{3}\right)^2 = 4.5\ \text{m/s}.$$

The tighter opening makes the stream faster because the volume flow rate $Q$ must stay the same.

### kinetic_energy (1.3s)

## Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses because it is moving. For an object of mass $m$ translating at speed $v$ (with $v \ll c$, so relativistic effects are negligible), the kinetic energy is

$$KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

This expression follows directly from the work-energy theorem: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, $W_{net} = \Delta KE$. Starting from Newton's second law, $F = ma$, and integrating force over displacement,

$$W = \int F\,dx = \int ma\,dx = \int m\frac{dv}{dt}\,dx = \int mv\,dv = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2$$

using the substitution $dx/dt = v$. This derivation shows that $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ is not an arbitrary definition — it is the quantity whose change exactly tracks the work done by a net force, which is why kinetic energy is so useful for solving motion problems without tracking time explicitly.

**Worked example.** A 1200 kg car accelerates from rest to 25 m/s. Its kinetic energy at that speed is

$$KE = \frac{1}{2}(1200\text{ kg})(25\text{ m/s})^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1200)(625) = 375{,}000\text{ J} = 375\text{ kJ}$$

By the work-energy theorem, this is exactly the net work the engine (net of friction and drag) must have delivered to reach that speed, regardless of how long the acceleration took or whether it was uniform.

**Problem-solving application.** Kinetic energy's real power is in problems where forces are known but time is not. Suppose the same 1200 kg car, moving at 25 m/s, brakes to a stop under a constant friction force of 6000 N. Rather than solving for acceleration and time separately, apply the work-energy theorem directly: the net work done by friction over stopping distance $d$ must equal the negative of the car's kinetic energy, $-Fd = 0 - KE_i$. Solving,

$$d = \frac{KE_i}{F} = \frac{375{,}000\text{ J}}{6000\text{ N}} = 62.5\text{ m}$$

This illustrates a key problem-solving strategy: whenever a force and a change in speed are known but the time of interaction is not, relate them through work and kinetic energy rather than through kinematics. Also note the quadratic dependence on $v$: doubling the initial speed would quadruple $KE_i$, and therefore quadruple the stopping distance for the same braking force — a fact central to vehicle safety engineering, since it explains why small increases in speed produce disproportionately larger stopping distances and collision severity.

### pressure (0.7s)

## Pressure

**Pressure** is the force per unit area acting perpendicular to a surface:

$$P = \frac{F_\perp}{A}$$

The SI unit is the pascal: $1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2$. Pressure is a scalar. It has no direction of its own, but the force it produces on a surface always acts perpendicular to that surface. Only the component of force perpendicular to the surface counts.

Pressure measures how concentrated a force is, which is why the same force can do very different things. A person pressing a thumbtack pushes with the same force on both ends. The point has a tiny area, so the pressure there is enormous.

### Worked example

A 60 kg person stands on one foot, and the contact area is $A = 0.020\ \text{m}^2$. Find the pressure on the floor.

The force is the person's weight:

$$F = mg = (60)(9.8) = 588\ \text{N}$$

$$P = \frac{588\ \text{N}}{0.020\ \text{m}^2} \approx 2.9\times10^{4}\ \text{Pa}$$

Now suppose the same person wears a stiletto heel with contact area $A = 1.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$, and all the weight rests on that heel:

$$P = \frac{588}{1.0\times10^{-4}} = 5.9\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}$$

The pressure is about 200 times larger, because the area is 200 times smaller. This is why stiletto heels dent wooden floors that bare feet don't mark.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** A pressure of $1.96\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ acts on a circular viewing window of radius $0.05\ \text{m}$ on a diver's equipment. Find the force on the window.

**Step 1: Area.** Use consistent SI units.

$$A = \pi r^2 = \pi(0.05)^2 = 7.85\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$$

**Step 2: Force.** Rearrange the definition to $F = PA$:

$$F = (1.96\times10^{5})(7.85\times10^{-3}) \approx 1.5\times10^{3}\ \text{N}$$

This is about 1500 N, roughly the weight of a 160 kg mass, pressing on a window the size of a small coaster. A strategy that works for most pressure problems:

1. Identify the force and the surface it acts on. Use only the component perpendicular to the surface.
2. Find the area in $\text{m}^2$. Convert from $\text{cm}^2$ by multiplying by $10^{-4}$.
3. Use $P = F/A$ to find pressure, or $F = PA$ to find force.
4. Check units: $\text{N}/\text{m}^2$ gives pascals.

Note how the two forms connect. If the same 1500 N were spread over a window ten times larger in area, the pressure would drop to one tenth. Comparing two situations by their area ratio, as in the stiletto example, is often faster than computing each pressure separately.

### bernoullis_equation (23.8s)

## Bernoulli's Equation

### Definition

Bernoulli's equation is a statement of conservation of energy for a fluid flowing without friction (inviscid), with constant density (incompressible), and in steady flow. Along any single streamline:

$$P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant}$$

Here $P$ is the static pressure (Pa), $\rho$ is the fluid density (kg/m³), $v$ is the flow speed (m/s), $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $h$ is the height above a chosen reference level. Each term has units of energy per unit volume (J/m³). They represent pressure energy, kinetic energy, and gravitational potential energy, respectively.

Between two points 1 and 2 on the same streamline:

$$P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 + \rho g h_1 = P_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 + \rho g h_2 .$$

The equation follows from the work–energy theorem applied to a small parcel of fluid. The net work done by pressure forces and gravity equals the change in the parcel's kinetic energy.

### Worked example

Water ($\rho = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$) flows through a horizontal pipe. At point 1 the pipe has cross-sectional area $A_1 = 20\ \text{cm}^2$, with $v_1 = 2.0\ \text{m/s}$ and $P_1 = 150\ \text{kPa}$. At point 2 the pipe narrows to $A_2 = 10\ \text{cm}^2$. Find $P_2$.

Because the water is incompressible, the volume passing each point per second is the same. This gives $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$, which we use to find the speed at point 2.

1. **Speed at point 2:** $v_2 = v_1 A_1/A_2 = 2.0 \times 2 = 4.0\ \text{m/s}$.
2. **Bernoulli (with $h_1 = h_2$):** the height terms cancel, so
$$P_2 = P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,(v_1^2 - v_2^2) = 150\,000 + \tfrac{1}{2}(1000)(4 - 16) = 144\,000\ \text{Pa}.$$

The pressure drops by 6 kPa where the fluid speeds up. Faster flow corresponds to lower pressure when height is unchanged.

### Problem-solving application

**Torricelli's law.** A large open tank of water has a small hole a depth $d$ below the surface. How fast does water exit?

Choose point 1 at the surface and point 2 at the hole. Both are open to the atmosphere, so $P_1 = P_2 = P_{\text{atm}}$. The surface is large, so $v_1 \approx 0$. Setting $h_1 - h_2 = d$:

$$\rho g d = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_2 = \sqrt{2gd}.$$

This is the speed of an object in free fall through height $d$, which is what energy conservation predicts. For $d = 1.25\ \text{m}$, $v_2 = \sqrt{2(9.8)(1.25)} \approx 4.9\ \text{m/s}$.

**General strategy:**

1. Pick two points on one streamline, with one point where most quantities are known.
2. Choose a reference height so that one $h$ is zero.
3. If the speeds are both unknown, relate them using the equal-volume-per-second condition from the worked example.
4. Apply Bernoulli's equation and solve for the unknown.

**Limitations.** Viscous friction, compressibility (high-speed gas flow), turbulence, and pumps or turbines that add or remove energy all violate the assumptions. In those cases the equation does not apply without correction.

### bernoullis_principle (24.9s)

## Bernoulli's Principle

**Definition.** Bernoulli's principle expresses conservation of energy for a steady, incompressible, non-viscous fluid flowing along a streamline. At constant height, the sum of pressure and kinetic energy per unit volume is the same at every point:

$$P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 = P_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2$$

Here $P$ is the pressure (Pa), $\rho$ is the fluid density (kg/m³), and $v$ is the speed (m/s). Where the fluid moves faster, the pressure is lower. Where it moves slower, the pressure is higher.

The term $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2$ is kinetic energy per unit volume. Since the total is fixed, any gain in kinetic energy must be paid for by a loss in pressure. The equation applies only if the flow is steady, the fluid is incompressible, viscosity is negligible, and both points lie on the same streamline.

To find the speeds, we use the continuity relation $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$ that you already know. A fluid speeds up where the pipe narrows.

**Worked example.** Water ($\rho = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$) flows through a horizontal pipe. In the wide section the cross-sectional area is $A_1 = 20\ \text{cm}^2$, the speed is $v_1 = 2.0\ \text{m/s}$, and the pressure is $P_1 = 150\ \text{kPa}$. The pipe narrows to $A_2 = 10\ \text{cm}^2$. Find $P_2$.

1. Use continuity to find the speed in the narrow section:
$$v_2 = \frac{A_1}{A_2}v_1 = 2(2.0) = 4.0\ \text{m/s}$$

2. Rearrange Bernoulli's equation for $P_2$:
$$P_2 = P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\left(v_1^2 - v_2^2\right)$$

3. Substitute:
$$P_2 = 150\,000 + \tfrac{1}{2}(1000)(4 - 16) = 150\,000 - 6000 = 144\,000\ \text{Pa}$$

The pressure drops by 6 kPa in the narrow section, where the water moves faster.

**Problem-solving application.** Bernoulli's equation also works in reverse. If you measure the pressure difference $\Delta P = P_1 - P_2$ between the two sections, you can find the flow speed. Substituting $v_2 = (A_1/A_2)v_1$ gives

$$\Delta P = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2\left[\left(\frac{A_1}{A_2}\right)^2 - 1\right]
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta P}{\rho\left[(A_1/A_2)^2 - 1\right]}}$$

The volumetric flow rate is then $Q = A_1 v_1$. Check this with the numbers above: $\Delta P = 6000\ \text{Pa}$ and $A_1/A_2 = 2$ give $v_1 = \sqrt{12000/(1000\cdot 3)} = 2.0\ \text{m/s}$. This matches the given speed. Then $Q = (20\times10^{-4})(2.0) = 4.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$, or 4 L/s.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Convert all units to SI (Pa, m², m/s) before substituting.
- Do not apply the equation across a pump, a turbulent region, or a long pipe where friction matters.
- The equation as written holds only at constant height. If the height changes, it cannot be used as is.

### aerodynamic_lift (14.2s)

## Aerodynamic Lift

**Definition.** Aerodynamic lift is the net upward force on a wing produced when air flows around it so that the pressure on the lower surface exceeds the pressure on the upper surface. The airfoil's shape and its angle of attack make the air above move faster than the air below. By Bernoulli's principle, for steady, incompressible, nearly frictionless flow along a streamline,

$$p + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 = \text{constant},$$

so higher speed $v$ means lower static pressure $p$. Here $\rho$ is air density.

A caution: the "equal transit time" explanation is wrong. Air above the wing does not need to rejoin the air below at the trailing edge, and in practice it arrives earlier. Bernoulli's equation relates pressure to speed correctly, but the wing's curvature and angle of attack, which turn the flow downward, are what set up the speed difference. By Newton's third law, the downward-turned air pushes the wing up. The two descriptions are consistent.

**Engineering form.** Engineers summarize the effect with the lift equation

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\, v^2\, S\, C_L,$$

where $S$ is the wing area and $C_L$ is the dimensionless lift coefficient. $C_L$ depends on airfoil shape and angle of attack. Lift grows with the square of airspeed.

**Worked example.** A small plane has $S = 16\ \text{m}^2$ and $C_L = 1.2$. It flies at $v = 50\ \text{m/s}$ at sea level, where $\rho = 1.225\ \text{kg/m}^3$.

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2}(1.225)(50)^2(16)(1.2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1.225)(2500)(19.2) = 29{,}400\ \text{N}$$

Using $W = mg$ with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, this lift supports a mass of about $29{,}400/9.8 = 3000\ \text{kg}$.

**Bernoulli check.** Suppose air flows at $v_{\text{lower}} = 48\ \text{m/s}$ under the wing and $v_{\text{upper}} = 52\ \text{m/s}$ over it. The pressure difference is

$$\Delta p = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,(v_{\text{upper}}^2 - v_{\text{lower}}^2)\ \text{(magnitude)} = \tfrac{1}{2}(1.225)(2704 - 2304) = 245\ \text{Pa}.$$

Multiplying by the wing area gives $245 \times 16 \approx 3{,}900\ \text{N}$. This is far less than the 29,400 N above, because I chose the speeds arbitrarily. Real wings have much larger speed differences over the upper surface. The point is the method: pressure difference times area gives force.

**Problem-solving application.** Find the minimum takeoff speed for an aircraft of mass $m = 1200\ \text{kg}$, with $S = 16\ \text{m}^2$ and maximum $C_L = 1.6$.

Lift must at least equal weight: $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 S C_L = mg$. Solving for $v$ gives

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho S C_L}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1200)(9.8)}{(1.225)(16)(1.6)}} = \sqrt{\frac{23{,}520}{31.36}} \approx 27.4\ \text{m/s}.$$

This is about 99 km/h. Two consequences follow. At higher altitude $\rho$ is smaller, so the required speed rises. A heavier load also raises the required speed, in proportion to $\sqrt{m}$.

**Takeaway.** Lift comes from a pressure difference created by the shape and tilt of the wing. Bernoulli's principle links that difference to the airflow speeds. The equation $L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 S C_L$ lets you predict how much lift a wing generates and how fast it must fly.
