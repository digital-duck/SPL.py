## Physiology

Physiology is the study of how living systems — from single cells to organ systems to whole organisms — perform the functions that keep them alive at baseline: transport, exchange, signaling, and mechanical work, all coordinated to hold internal conditions within survivable and functionally optimal bounds. Within a concept-book organized around escalating "radicals" of biomedical reasoning, physiology occupies the ground floor: it defines what "healthy" looks like operationally, before pathology (deviation), pharmacology (intervention), or pathophysiology (mechanism of breakdown) can be meaningfully described. Every downstream concept is a perturbation of, or a return to, some physiological baseline established here.

The core mechanism unifying physiological function is homeostasis: the maintenance of a controlled variable — core temperature, blood pH, plasma glucose, arterial pressure, extracellular potassium — within a narrow operating range around a set point, despite continuous internal and external disturbance. This is achieved through negative-feedback loops with three canonical components: a sensor (e.g., baroreceptors in the carotid sinus, osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus), an integrating center (often the hypothalamus, brainstem, or a local paracrine circuit), and an effector (smooth muscle, gland, or organ) that returns the variable toward set point. Positive feedback loops exist but are rarer and self-limiting by design (e.g., the coagulation cascade, oxytocin-driven labor), since unchecked positive feedback is inherently destabilizing. Normal ranges themselves are not fixed constants but statistically derived intervals — typically the central 95% of values in a reference population — a point often underemphasized in clinical teaching: a "normal" lab value is a population-based confidence interval, not a physiological law, and set points can shift adaptively (e.g., fever resetting the hypothalamic thermostat, chronic hypoxia resetting erythropoietin drive).

The literature here is mature at the level of individual loops (cardiovascular baroreflex, renal renin-angiotensin-aldosterone regulation, respiratory chemoreceptor control of ventilation) but genuinely open at the level of systems integration: how do dozens of interacting feedback loops with different time constants (seconds for baroreflex, hours for renal fluid balance, days for erythropoiesis) avoid destructive interference, and how does "allostasis" — anticipatory adjustment of set points ahead of predicted demand — extend classical homeostatic theory? Chronobiology and network physiology (e.g., work on physiologic networks by Ivanov and colleagues) represent active fronts.

A concrete investigation: select one feedback loop (e.g., baroreflex control of heart rate) and build a quantitative model — even a simple first-order differential equation relating sensor error to effector response — then validate it against real or published time-series data (e.g., heart-rate variability under a Valsalva maneuver or postural change). Alternatively, conduct a case study comparing normal-range definitions across two reference populations (e.g., pediatric vs. adult, or two countries' lab standards) and analyze the physiological and statistical basis for the discrepancy.

A write-up should establish: (1) the specific controlled variable and its accepted normal range, with citation to the reference population used; (2) the sensor–integrator–effector loop diagrammed explicitly, including loop gain and response latency where known; (3) at least one condition (physiological stress, not disease) under which the set point shifts, and the evidence for that shift; (4) an explicit statement of what remains unmeasured or contested in the current literature about that loop's integration with other systems.

## Symptom

A symptom is a departure from a patient's baseline physiological or functional state that the patient perceives and reports — pain, fatigue, dyspnea, nausea, dizziness — as distinct from a *sign*, which a clinician observes or measures (a fever, a murmur, an elevated white count). The distinction matters because a symptom is inherently a first-person report: it is filtered through the patient's language, attention, cultural framing, and threshold for concern before it ever reaches a chart. In control-theoretic terms, a symptom is the analog of a sensor's deviation from set point — the raw error signal $e(t) = y_{\text{observed}}(t) - y_{\text{setpoint}}$ in a feedback loop — except here the "sensor" is a person's interoceptive and cognitive apparatus, which is noisy, non-uniformly calibrated across individuals, and only partially observable to anyone outside the patient.

**Mechanism.** Symptoms arise through several distinct physiological pathways that a clinician (or investigator) must distinguish, because they carry different diagnostic weight. Nociceptive pain arises from activation of peripheral nociceptors by tissue damage or inflammatory mediators (prostaglandins, bradykinin) and is transmitted via A-delta and C fibers to the dorsal horn and thence to cortex. Dyspnea arises from mismatch between ventilatory demand and the mechanical or chemical capacity to meet it, integrated by brainstem and cortical afferents from chemoreceptors, mechanoreceptors, and respiratory muscle spindles — a genuinely multifactorial percept, not a single-pathway signal. Fatigue is even less well localized, implicating central (cortical/limbic) and peripheral (muscular, metabolic, inflammatory-cytokine) contributors whose relative weighting is often undetermined for a given patient. In each case, the symptom is the output of an integration process that combines a genuine physiological perturbation with substantial top-down modulation (attention, mood, expectation, prior experience) — which is precisely why symptom severity correlates only loosely with objective pathology across many conditions (e.g., the weak correlation between radiographic osteoarthritis severity and reported knee pain).

**Literature context.** Psychometric instruments (visual analog scales, the Brief Pain Inventory, PROMIS measures) attempt to convert the subjective report into a quantifiable, comparable signal, but validation remains an open methodological problem: test-retest reliability, cross-cultural equivalence, and susceptibility to response-shift bias (patients recalibrate their internal scale over the course of illness) are all active concerns in outcomes research. A parallel and growing literature treats symptoms as time series generated by wearable and ecological momentary assessment (EMA) data, raising the engineering-adjacent question of how to denoise, detrend, and detect clinically meaningful change in a self-reported signal that has no fixed calibration across subjects or time.

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable project: obtain or construct a longitudinal symptom-diary dataset (e.g., daily pain or fatigue scores from a chronic-condition cohort, several public PRO datasets exist) and treat it explicitly as a noisy control-loop error signal. Fit a state-space or Kalman-filter model to separate a latent physiological trend from reporting noise, and compare filtered estimates against any concurrent objective sign (activity counts, inflammatory markers) available in the same dataset.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state explicitly which symptom-elicitation instrument was used and its known reliability/validity properties, report the model's assumptions about noise structure and justify them against the instrument's psychometric literature, and — critically — characterize where the latent-trend estimate diverges from the objective sign, since that divergence is itself the scientifically interesting quantity, not an error to be minimized away.
