### position (0.0s)

## Position

Position specifies where an object is located at a given instant, measured relative to a chosen reference frame — a coordinate system with an origin, a set of axes, and a unit of measurement. Because position is always relative, the number describing it depends entirely on the reference point you pick. A car might be "at mile marker 50" relative to a highway, or "3 meters from the wall" relative to a room. In one dimension, position is a single signed number, $x$, often measured in meters, where the sign indicates direction from the origin (positive to the right, negative to the left, by convention). In two or three dimensions, position requires more than one number to pin down — a pair $(x, y)$ or a triple $(x, y, z)$ — but the one-dimensional case captures the core idea and is what the rest of this section uses.

**Worked example.** Suppose a runner starts a race and a stopwatch begins at $t = 0$. Define the reference frame with the starting line as the origin and the positive direction pointing down the track. At $t = 2\text{ s}$, the runner is at $x = 10\text{ m}$; at $t = 4\text{ s}$, she is at $x = 22\text{ m}$. These two values are positions — snapshots of location at specific times. Note what position does *not* tell you directly: the total distance traveled (if she weaved sideways, distance could exceed the straight-line displacement) or her speed. Position is a description of "where," not "how far traveled" or "how fast."

**Problem-solving application.** Choosing the reference frame wisely is often the key move in solving a mechanics problem. Consider two blocks connected by a string over a pulley, one hanging and one on a table. Defining a consistent positive direction for each block (e.g., "down" as positive for the hanging block, matching the direction of its motion) lets the relationship linking their positions become a simple algebraic statement rather than a sign-error minefield: as one block's position increases, the other's decreases by the same amount, since the string length is fixed. When solving multi-object or multi-stage problems, always state your reference frame explicitly before writing equations: origin, positive direction, and units. This single habit eliminates a large fraction of errors in kinematics problems, since velocity and acceleration are both defined as rates of change of position, and an inconsistent frame propagates errors through every subsequent calculation.

### time (0.0s)

## Time

Time is the interval over which change occurs. It is one of the seven SI base quantities, measured in seconds (s), and unlike length or mass, it cannot be directly compared side by side — you cannot lay two durations next to each other the way you lay two rulers next to each other. Instead, time is measured indirectly, by counting repetitions of a process assumed to repeat identically: a pendulum swing, a heartbeat, a vibration. Since 1967, the SI second has been defined by counting exactly 9,192,631,770 oscillations of radiation emitted by a cesium-133 atom transitioning between two hyperfine energy states. This atomic standard replaced earlier definitions based on Earth's rotation, which turned out to vary slightly and unpredictably.

**Worked example.** Suppose a cesium clock reports that a signal has completed $4{,}596{,}315{,}885$ oscillations. How many seconds have elapsed? Since one second corresponds to $9{,}192{,}631{,}770$ oscillations,
$$
t = \frac{4{,}596{,}315{,}885}{9{,}192{,}631{,}770} \text{ s} \approx 0.5000\text{ s}.
$$
This illustrates the core idea: a time measurement is always a ratio — the number of elapsed cycles divided by the defined cycle rate of the reference standard.

**Problem-solving application.** Timekeeping systems must be calibrated against this standard, and calibration errors compound. GPS satellites, for instance, carry onboard atomic clocks, and receivers on Earth compute position by timing how long radio signals take to arrive from multiple satellites: distance equals the signal's travel time multiplied by the speed of light. Because the speed of light is so large, a clock error of just one microsecond ($10^{-6}$ s) translates into a position error of about 300 meters. This is why GPS satellite clocks are corrected not only for such drift but also for relativistic time dilation caused by their speed and altitude — without correction, GPS positioning would degrade by several kilometers per day.

When solving problems involving time, always check the units and the reference frame first: is the duration given in seconds, and is it measured against the same standard as any rate or speed used alongside it? Mismatched time bases — for example, mixing a locally measured clock with an uncorrected satellite clock — are a common source of error in real-world systems that depend on precise timing, from financial trading networks to power-grid synchronization.

### force (0.0s)

## Force

Force is any interaction that, when unbalanced, changes an object's state of motion — its velocity. This is the definitional core of Newton's second law: force is not what keeps things moving, but what changes how they move. A force is a vector, carrying both magnitude and direction, and multiple forces acting on an object combine by vector addition into a single net force, $\vec{F}_{net} = \sum \vec{F}_i$.

Newton's second law makes this relationship precise:

$$
\vec{F}_{net} = m\vec{a}
$$

Here $m$ is the object's mass — its resistance to acceleration — and $\vec{a}$ is the resulting acceleration. This is a genuine dynamical law, not a definition dressed up as one: it asserts that acceleration is caused by and proportional to net force, and inversely proportional to mass. Units follow directly: force is measured in newtons, where $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$.

**Worked example.** A 2 kg block sits on a frictionless table. Two forces act on it: 6 N to the right and 4 N to the left. The net force is $\vec{F}_{net} = 6\ \text{N} - 4\ \text{N} = 2\ \text{N}$ to the right. By Newton's second law, the acceleration is

$$
a = \frac{F_{net}}{m} = \frac{2\ \text{N}}{2\ \text{kg}} = 1\ \text{m/s}^2
$$

If the block starts at rest, after 3 seconds it reaches $v = at = 3\ \text{m/s}$, still moving to the right.

**Problem-solving application.** The standard method for analyzing any dynamics problem is the free-body diagram: isolate the object, draw every force acting on it (gravity, normal force, tension, friction, applied push or pull), resolve each into components along convenient axes, sum the components to get $\vec{F}_{net}$, and apply $\vec{F}_{net} = m\vec{a}$ separately along each axis. For instance, consider a block on a frictionless ramp tilted at angle $\theta$. Gravity pulls straight down with magnitude $mg$, and the normal force pushes perpendicular to the ramp's surface, exactly canceling the part of gravity pushing into the ramp. What remains is the part of gravity pulling the block down along the slope, which works out to $mg\sin\theta$; dividing by mass gives the block's acceleration down the ramp, $a = g\sin\theta$. This decomposition — identify every interaction, sum as vectors, divide by mass — is the dynamical floor beneath essentially all of classical mechanics, from orbits to collisions to oscillations.

### velocity (0.0s)

## Velocity

Position tells you *where* something is; velocity tells you *how fast and in what direction* that position is changing. Formally, velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time:

$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt}$$

This is the first quantity "derived" from position, in the literal calculus sense of taking a derivative. Because position $x(t)$ can be a vector (specifying direction as well as magnitude in two or three dimensions), velocity inherits that vector character — it is not just a speed, but a speed with a direction. Speed is the magnitude $|v|$, discarding directional information; velocity keeps it.

**Worked example.** Suppose a particle moves along a line according to $x(t) = 2t^3 - 9t^2 + 12t$ (meters, with $t$ in seconds). To find velocity, differentiate:

$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = 6t^2 - 18t + 12$$

At $t = 1$: $v(1) = 6 - 18 + 12 = 0$ m/s. The particle is momentarily at rest. At $t = 3$: $v(3) = 54 - 54 + 12 = 12$ m/s, moving in the positive direction. Notice that velocity can be zero even while the particle is in motion overall — it's an instantaneous quantity, valid at that exact moment, not an average over some interval.

**Problem-solving application.** A common task is distinguishing average velocity from instantaneous velocity. Average velocity over $[t_1, t_2]$ is the slope of the secant line through $(t_1, x(t_1))$ and $(t_2, x(t_2))$:

$$\bar{v} = \frac{x(t_2) - x(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}$$

Instantaneous velocity, by contrast, is the slope of the tangent line — the limit of that same expression as $t_2 \to t_1$:

$$v(t_1) = \lim_{t_2 \to t_1} \frac{x(t_2) - x(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}$$

This limit definition is exactly the definition of the derivative, which is why velocity is the canonical first example used to motivate calculus: it converts the everyday question "how fast?" into a precise mathematical object. Once you have $v(t)$, you can solve for when a particle is moving forward versus backward (sign of $v$), when it's momentarily at rest ($v = 0$), and — by integrating — recover displacement from a known velocity function, since $x(t) = x(0) + \int_0^t v(\tau)\,d\tau$. Velocity is also the quantity that must next be differentiated to obtain acceleration, continuing the chain of kinematic derivatives.

### mass (0.0s)

## Mass

Mass is a body's resistance to a change in its state of motion — a quantitative measure of inertia. This is a genuinely different idea from anything in kinematics, which only describes position, velocity, and acceleration without asking what causes or resists them. Two objects can trace identical trajectories, yet if you try to redirect them with equal force, the more massive one deflects less. Mass is not "how much stuff" in some vague sense; it is defined operationally by how much a given force fails to change an object's velocity.

This relationship is captured by Newton's second law:

$$
F = ma \quad \Longrightarrow \quad m = \frac{F}{a}
$$

Mass is the constant of proportionality between force and acceleration. Rearranged, it becomes an operational definition: apply a known force, measure the resulting acceleration, and their ratio is the mass. This is why mass is measured in kilograms, a base SI unit, rather than derived from length and time alone — it is an independent physical quantity, not a geometric one.

**Worked example.** A 0.50 kg cart on a frictionless track is pushed with a constant force of 2.0 N. What is its acceleration?

$$
a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{2.0\ \text{N}}{0.50\ \text{kg}} = 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2
$$

Now compare a second cart of mass 2.0 kg pushed with the same 2.0 N force: it accelerates at only $1.0\ \text{m/s}^2$. Same force, same time interval, but the more massive cart's velocity changes four times more slowly — direct evidence of greater inertial resistance.

**Problem-solving application.** Mass lets you predict motion from force, or infer force from observed motion, in any setting where kinematics alone is silent — for instance, why a fully loaded truck needs a much longer stopping distance than an empty one under the same braking force, or why an astronaut in orbit still has to strain to push a massive equipment module into place even though it is weightless. In each case, mass is what governs how hard it is to start, stop, or redirect an object, regardless of friction or apparent weightlessness.

### constraints (0.0s)

## Constraints

A constraint is an equation or inequality that restricts which configurations of a system are physically allowed, reducing the number of independent coordinates needed to describe it. If a pendulum bob has position $(x, y)$ in a plane, it does not roam freely over two dimensions — it is tied to a pivot by a rod of fixed length $L$, so its position must always satisfy

$$
x^2 + y^2 = L^2
$$

This single equation converts what looks like a two-degree-of-freedom problem into a one-degree-of-freedom problem: the angle $\theta$ from vertical fully determines the position, via $x = L\sin\theta$, $y = -L\cos\theta$. The constraint has not eliminated motion — it has carved out the *circle* on which motion happens, out of the full plane of positions that would otherwise be possible.

**Worked example.** Consider a bead threaded on a rigid circular wire of radius $R$ centered at the origin. Without the wire, the bead's position $(x,y)$ needs two coordinates. The wire imposes $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$, a *holonomic* constraint (expressible purely as a relation among coordinates, with no dependence on velocity). Because it is holonomic, we can substitute it directly to reduce the coordinate count: parametrize by the single angle $\phi$, with $x = R\cos\phi$, $y = R\sin\phi$. Any physics problem involving this bead — computing its speed, its kinetic energy, or the forces needed to keep it on the wire — can now be worked entirely in terms of $\phi$ and $\dot\phi}$, a strictly simpler problem than tracking $x$ and $y$ separately while enforcing the circle equation at every step.

**Problem-solving application.** The practical payoff of identifying constraints is dimensional reduction: a system with $N$ unconstrained coordinates and $k$ independent constraint equations has only $N - k$ true degrees of freedom, and choosing coordinates that automatically satisfy the constraints (like $\theta$ for the pendulum, or $\phi$ for the bead) eliminates the need to carry constraint forces — like the rod's tension or the wire's normal force — through the rest of the calculation. When you encounter a mechanics problem, the first move is always to count constraints and pick coordinates matched to them, since equations of motion written in the reduced coordinates are almost always shorter and easier to solve than the same physics written in raw Cartesian coordinates.

### work (0.0s)

## Work

Work is the transfer of energy that occurs when a force acts on an object as it moves. In its simplest form,

$$
W = F \cdot d
$$

where $F$ is the magnitude of the force applied in the direction of motion, and $d$ is the displacement of the object. Work is measured in joules (J), where $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$.

A critical subtlety: only the component of force *parallel* to the displacement does work. If the force acts at an angle $\theta$ to the direction of motion, the formula generalizes to

$$
W = F d \cos\theta
$$

This is why carrying a heavy box across a level floor does zero physics-work on the box, even though it feels exhausting — the force you exert (upward, against gravity) is perpendicular to your horizontal displacement, so $\cos 90^\circ = 0$.

**Worked example.** A worker pushes a 40 kg crate across a warehouse floor with a horizontal force of 150 N, moving it 8 meters. Friction is negligible here, so all the force contributes to motion:

$$
W = (150\ \text{N})(8\ \text{m}) = 1200\ \text{J}
$$

Now suppose instead the worker pulls the crate with a rope angled 30° above horizontal, using the same 150 N force over the same 8 m distance:

$$
W = (150)(8)\cos(30^\circ) \approx (150)(8)(0.866) \approx 1039\ \text{J}
$$

Less work is done for the same force and distance, because part of the effort is "wasted" lifting rather than moving the crate forward.

**Problem-solving application.** Work is the bridge that connects force (a vector, describing *interaction*) to energy (a scalar, describing *capacity to change a system*). This is what makes the work–energy theorem so useful: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy,

$$
W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_i^2
$$

This lets you solve motion problems without tracking acceleration over time — if you know the forces and distances involved, you can jump directly to the resulting speed. For example, to find how fast that 40 kg crate is moving after the 1200 J push (starting from rest), solve $1200 = \tfrac{1}{2}(40)v^2$ for $v \approx 7.75\ \text{m/s}$ — no kinematics equations required.

### configuration_space (0.0s)

## Configuration Space

A mechanical system's **configuration space**, denoted $\mathcal{Q}$, is the set of all positions consistent with its constraints — the manifold on which the system's dynamics unfold. Each point $q \in \mathcal{Q}$ specifies the system's full geometric arrangement at an instant, independent of how fast anything is moving. Constraints reduce the raw space of coordinates down to $\mathcal{Q}$: a bead free in three dimensions has configuration space $\mathbb{R}^3$, but if it is constrained to a wire bent into a circle, its configuration space collapses to the circle $S^1$, parametrized by a single angle $\theta$. The dimension of $\mathcal{Q}$ equals the number of degrees of freedom — the minimum number of independent coordinates needed to pin down the configuration.

**Worked example.** A simple pendulum — a mass on a rigid rod pivoted at a fixed point — can swing anywhere in the vertical plane, but the rod length is fixed. That single constraint reduces two Cartesian coordinates $(x, y)$ to one: the angle $\theta$ from vertical. So $\mathcal{Q} = S^1$, a circle. Now attach a second pendulum to the end of the first — a double pendulum. Each rod contributes its own angle, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, and each angle independently ranges over a full circle. The configuration space is the Cartesian product $\mathcal{Q} = S^1 \times S^1 = T^2$, a torus. This is a genuinely different geometric object from the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ you might naively picture for two angles: on a torus, "going around" in $\theta_1$ eventually returns you to where you started, and the two angles wrap independently, so the space is curved and compact.

**Why this matters for problem-solving.** Identifying $\mathcal{Q}$ correctly is the first and most consequential step in solving any constrained-motion problem. It tells you how many independent coordinates you actually need — for the double pendulum, exactly two, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, never four — and it tells you their natural range and topology, not just their instantaneous values. Choosing $\theta$ instead of $(x, y)$ for the simple pendulum automatically builds the rod-length constraint into the geometry itself, so no separate bookkeeping for that constraint is ever needed once $\mathcal{Q}$ is chosen correctly. Misidentifying the topology of $\mathcal{Q}$ — treating the double pendulum's torus as a flat plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ — is a common and costly error: it obscures the fact that $\theta_1 = 0$ and $\theta_1 = 2\pi$ describe the identical configuration, and it can lead you to expect the system's motion to settle into a fixed region rather than wrap around periodically. Before writing down any equations of motion for a constrained system, the practical first step is always the same: count the degrees of freedom, choose coordinates that respect them, and identify what shape $\mathcal{Q}$ actually is.

### potential_energy (0.0s)

## Potential Energy

Potential energy, $V(x)$, is energy stored in the configuration of a system — the arrangement of an object relative to a force field, such as height in a gravitational field or displacement in a stretched spring. It is defined so that a conservative force is recovered by differentiating $V(x)$ with respect to position:

$$
F(x) = -\frac{dV}{dx}
$$

The negative sign encodes a physical fact: a conservative force always pushes a system toward *lower* potential energy. A ball rolls downhill because gravity pulls it toward decreasing $V$; a stretched spring snaps back because the restoring force points toward decreasing elastic $V$. Since force is the derivative of $V$, potential energy is only ever defined up to an additive constant — physically meaningful quantities are differences $\Delta V$, not absolute values.

**Worked example.** Near Earth's surface, gravity is approximately constant, $F = -mg$ (taking upward as positive $x$). Integrating $F = -dV/dx$ gives $V(x) = mgx + C$; choosing $C = 0$ so that $V(0) = 0$ yields the familiar $V(x) = mgx$. For a spring obeying Hooke's law, $F(x) = -kx$, integration gives $V(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. Notice both derivations follow the same logic: given a force law, integrate $-F(x)$ to recover $V(x)$.

**Problem-solving application.** Potential energy becomes most useful when paired with kinetic energy, $K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, through conservation of mechanical energy: for systems where only conservative forces act, the total $E = K + V$ stays constant. This turns a differential-equation problem (solving Newton's second law explicitly for position as a function of time) into an algebraic one. Example: a block on a frictionless track starts at rest at height $h$. At the top, $E_i = mgh$ (since $K=0$); at the bottom, $E_f = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ (since $V=0$). Setting $E_i = E_f$ gives $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ directly — no need to solve the track's equation of motion, regardless of its shape. This is the general strategy: whenever forces are conservative, write $V(x)$ once, then use $E = K + V$ to bypass integrating the motion equations for each new geometry or force profile.

### generalized_coordinates (0.0s)

## Generalized Coordinates

A system of $N$ particles moving freely in three dimensions needs $3N$ numbers to specify its configuration. But real systems are rarely free — a bead is confined to a wire, a pendulum bob swings at fixed distance from its pivot, two atoms in a rigid molecule stay a fixed distance apart. Each such constraint removes one degree of freedom. If there are $k$ independent constraints, only $n = 3N - k$ independent numbers are needed to pin down the configuration. **Generalized coordinates** $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n$ are any set of $n$ independent variables that fully specify the configuration of a constrained system — chosen not because they are Cartesian, but because they match the geometry of the constraints.

The defining freedom is that generalized coordinates need not be lengths at all. They can be angles, arc lengths, areas — whatever varies independently as the system moves. What makes a choice of coordinates "generalized" is only that (1) each $q_i$ can vary independently, and (2) knowing all $q_i$ determines the Cartesian position of every particle in the system through relations of the form $x_j = x_j(q_1, \dots, q_n, t)$.

**Worked example.** A simple pendulum — a bob of mass $m$ on a rigid rod of length $\ell$, swinging in a vertical plane — has a bob position $(x, y)$ that would naively need two Cartesian coordinates. But the constraint $x^2 + y^2 = \ell^2$ ties $x$ and $y$ together, leaving only one independent quantity. The natural generalized coordinate is the angle $\theta$ from vertical:

$$
x = \ell \sin\theta, \qquad y = -\ell \cos\theta.
$$

One variable, $\theta$, replaces two Cartesian variables shackled by one constraint equation — exactly $3N - k = 2 - 1 = 1$ degree of freedom.

**Why this matters for problem-solving.** The payoff of choosing $\theta$ instead of $(x,y)$ is not philosophical — it is computational. The rod exerts a tension force on the bob that keeps it at fixed distance $\ell$ from the pivot, but this force does no work along the direction $\theta$ moves the bob, so it never appears when the equations of motion are written in terms of $\theta$. Writing Newton's second law in $x$ and $y$ requires solving for the unknown tension force explicitly; writing it in $\theta$ eliminates that unknown automatically. This is the practical reason generalized coordinates are the entry point to Lagrangian mechanics: picking coordinates that respect the constraints turns a coupled, force-laden problem into a single, clean differential equation for $\theta(t)$.

### kinetic_energy (0.0s)

## Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses because it is moving. Defined as

$$T = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

it depends on two quantities: mass $m$ (kg), a measure of how much matter resists changes in motion, and velocity $v$ (m/s), the speed at which that matter travels. Kinetic energy is measured in joules (J), where $1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$. Notice the quadratic dependence on $v$: doubling an object's speed does not double its kinetic energy — it quadruples it. This nonlinearity is not a mathematical curiosity; it comes directly from the work-energy theorem, which states that the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, $W_{net} = \Delta T$. Deriving $T = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ from Newton's second law ($F = ma$) via $W = \int F\,dx = \int ma\,dx = \int mv\,dv = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ shows that kinetic energy is not an arbitrary definition but a consequence of the relationship between force, displacement, and acceleration.

**Worked example.** A 1200 kg car accelerates from rest to 25 m/s (about 90 km/h). Its kinetic energy is

$$T = \frac{1}{2}(1200)(25)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1200)(625) = 375{,}000\ \text{J} = 375\ \text{kJ}$$

If the car instead reaches 50 m/s — double the speed — its kinetic energy becomes $\frac{1}{2}(1200)(2500) = 1{,}500{,}000\ \text{J}$, four times as much, not two. This is why doubling a vehicle's speed more than doubles stopping distance: all that extra energy must be dissipated by braking friction over a longer distance.

**Problem-solving application.** Kinetic energy calculations let you solve for unknowns you cannot measure directly. Suppose a 0.5 kg ball has 40 J of kinetic energy; solving $40 = \frac{1}{2}(0.5)v^2$ gives $v^2 = 160$, so $v \approx 12.6\ \text{m/s}$. The same work-energy relationship used above, $W_{net} = \Delta T$, also lets you find stopping distances: if a braking force $F$ acts over distance $d$, the work it does is $W = Fd$, and setting this equal to the car's kinetic energy gives $Fd = T$, so $d = T/F$. This single relationship — energy in, distance or force out — underlies collision analysis, braking system design, and projectile problems throughout physics and engineering.

### acceleration (20.2s)

## Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes with respect to time. Because velocity is itself a rate — the derivative of position — acceleration is a second derivative:

$$a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$$

Velocity tells you how fast position is changing; acceleration tells you how fast that rate of change is changing. Both are vector quantities, so acceleration captures not only speeding up or slowing down but also changes in direction. An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because its velocity vector is continuously rotating even though its magnitude never changes.

**Worked example.** Suppose a particle's position is given by $x(t) = 2t^3 - 9t^2 + 12t$ (meters, with $t$ in seconds). Its velocity is $v(t) = x'(t) = 6t^2 - 18t + 12$, and its acceleration is $a(t) = v'(t) = 12t - 18$. At $t = 1$ s, $a(1) = -6 \text{ m/s}^2$: the particle is decelerating in the positive direction. Acceleration is zero when $12t - 18 = 0$, i.e., $t = 1.5$ s — the instant velocity is momentarily at an extremum (here, a local minimum, since $a$ changes from negative to positive).

**Problem-solving application.** Once you have an expression for acceleration, integrating it once recovers velocity, and integrating again recovers position, provided you know the initial conditions $v_0$ and $x_0$. This lets you work backward from acceleration to full motion. Take the case of constant acceleration, as with an object falling under gravity alone, $a = -g \approx -9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$: integrating once gives $v(t) = v_0 - gt$, and integrating again gives $x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ — the familiar equations of free fall, derived rather than memorized. When acceleration is not constant, as in the polynomial example above, the same integration logic still applies, but you must carry out the antiderivatives symbolically or numerically rather than plugging into a fixed formula. Recognizing whether $a$ is constant or a function of $t$ is the key first step in solving any motion problem: constant acceleration lets you jump straight to the free-fall-style formulas, while time-varying acceleration requires integrating the specific expression given before you can say anything about velocity or position at a later time.

### lagrangian (0.0s)

## Lagrangian

Once you can write kinetic energy $T$ and potential energy $V$ in terms of generalized coordinates $q$, the **Lagrangian** collapses the whole dynamical problem into a single scalar function:

$$
L(q, \dot{q}, t) = T(q, \dot{q}) - V(q)
$$

This is not merely bookkeeping. $L$ is the object that Hamilton's principle says nature extremizes: among all paths $q(t)$ connecting fixed endpoints, the true motion makes the action $S = \int L \, dt$ stationary. Extremizing $S$ via the calculus of variations yields the **Euler-Lagrange equation**,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = 0,
$$

one equation for each generalized coordinate $q_i$. This is the theorem that makes the Lagrangian indispensable: it converts a geometric statement (stationary action) into the differential equations of motion, without ever invoking forces or vector components explicitly.

**Worked example.** For a simple pendulum of length $\ell$ and mass $m$, using angle $\theta$ as the generalized coordinate:

$$
T = \tfrac{1}{2} m \ell^2 \dot{\theta}^2, \qquad V = -mg\ell\cos\theta
$$

$$
L = \tfrac{1}{2} m \ell^2 \dot{\theta}^2 + mg\ell\cos\theta
$$

Compute $\partial L/\partial \dot{\theta} = m\ell^2\dot{\theta}$, so $\frac{d}{dt}(\partial L/\partial \dot\theta) = m\ell^2\ddot\theta$. Also $\partial L/\partial\theta = -mg\ell\sin\theta$. The Euler-Lagrange equation gives

$$
m\ell^2\ddot\theta + mg\ell\sin\theta = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta,
$$

the familiar pendulum equation, obtained without ever drawing a free-body diagram or resolving tension into components.

**Why this matters for problem-solving.** The Euler-Lagrange machinery is mechanical: write $T$ and $V$ correctly in your chosen $q_i$, differentiate, and the equations of motion fall out — even for systems (double pendulums, beads on rotating wires, coupled oscillators) where Newtonian force analysis becomes a nightmare of constraint forces and coordinate transformations. Constraint forces that do no work (like tension in a rigid rod) never appear at all, because they were eliminated the moment you chose $q$. This is the concrete payoff of building the energy ledger first: $L$ turns energy bookkeeping into a differential-equation generator.

### least_action (0.0s)

## Least Action

Newton's second law tells you how a particle moves, instant by instant, given the forces acting on it. The principle of least action tells you something more sweeping: out of every conceivable path a system could take between a fixed starting configuration and a fixed ending configuration, the one actually realized in nature is the one that makes a single number — the **action** $S$ — stationary. Formally,

$$
S[q(t)] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L\big(q(t), \dot q(t), t\big)\, dt,
$$

where $L = T - V$ is the Lagrangian (kinetic minus potential energy) and $q(t)$ is a trial trajectory connecting fixed endpoints $q(t_1)$ and $q(t_2)$. Among all such trial paths, the true physical trajectory is the one for which $S$ is stationary — unchanged to first order under any small variation $\delta q(t)$ that vanishes at the endpoints. This is a variational principle: physics restated not as "solve a differential equation forward in time" but as "find the extremal path among all paths."

The condition $\delta S = 0$ is not a postulate pulled from nowhere — it is derivable, using the calculus of variations, and it produces exactly the Euler–Lagrange equation, $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0$. Applied to a falling mass with $L = \tfrac{1}{2}m\dot y^2 - mgy$, this reduces to $m\ddot y = -mg$ — Newton's law recovered exactly, but arrived at through a global condition on the whole path rather than a local, instant-by-instant force balance.

The payoff of this reformulation is that it generalizes far beyond mechanics. In optics, Fermat's principle of least time is the same stationary-path idea applied to light: light traveling between two points takes the path of least (or stationary) travel time, and this single condition reproduces both the law of reflection and Snell's law of refraction. In field theory, the action becomes a functional of fields rather than particle coordinates, and stationarity again yields the governing field equations — Maxwell's equations for electromagnetism, Einstein's for gravity — through the same $\delta S = 0$ condition already established above. Least action is thus not a computational shortcut but the deepest common structure linking classical mechanics, optics, and field theory, all built from the single stationary-path idea introduced here.

### newtons_second_law (22.5s)

## Newtons Second Law

Newton's second law states that the net force acting on an object equals the product of its mass and its acceleration:

$$
\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\vec{a}
$$

This equation is the bridge between two branches of mechanics. *Kinematics* describes motion — position, velocity, acceleration — without asking what causes it. *Dynamics* asks why objects accelerate, in terms of force and mass. Newton's second law is the equation that converts one into the other: give it the forces acting on an object (dynamics) and it returns the resulting acceleration (kinematics), or vice versa.

The vector nature of the law matters. $\vec{F}_{\text{net}}$ is the sum of all forces acting on the object — you must add forces as vectors, accounting for direction, before applying the equation. Mass $m$ is a scalar measuring an object's resistance to acceleration (inertia): the same force produces less acceleration on a more massive object, which is why the equation is often solved as $\vec{a} = \vec{F}_{\text{net}}/m$.

**Worked example.** A 5 kg box sits on a frictionless floor. Two horizontal forces act on it: 12 N to the east and 4 N to the west. Find the acceleration.

First find the net force by combining vectors along the same axis: $F_{\text{net}} = 12\,\text{N} - 4\,\text{N} = 8\,\text{N}$ eastward. Then apply the second law:

$$
a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{8\,\text{N}}{5\,\text{kg}} = 1.6\ \text{m/s}^2 \text{ eastward}
$$

**Problem-solving application.** The law becomes most powerful in multi-force problems, where the strategy is always: (1) isolate the object with a *free-body diagram* — a sketch showing only the object and every force acting on it, drawn as arrows — (2) sum forces along convenient axes, decomposing any forces that aren't already aligned with those axes, (3) set that sum equal to $ma$, and (4) solve for the unknown, which might be acceleration, an unknown force, or mass itself. For instance, a 10 kg crate is pulled by a rope at 30° above horizontal with tension 50 N, while friction opposes the motion with a force of 15 N. The free-body diagram shows two forces along the direction of motion: the horizontal component of tension, $T\cos(30°) = 43.3\,\text{N}$, and friction acting backward. Summing along that axis gives $F_{\text{net}} = 43.3\,\text{N} - 15\,\text{N} = 28.3\,\text{N}$, so $a = 28.3\,\text{N}/10\,\text{kg} = 2.83\ \text{m/s}^2$. This four-step procedure — diagram, sum, set equal to $ma$, solve — is the same one used for any force problem, no matter how many forces are involved.

### euler_lagrange_equations (0.0s)

## Euler Lagrange Equations

Given a Lagrangian $L(q, \dot{q}, t)$, the physically realized path is the one that makes the action stationary. Turning that variational statement into a solvable equation of motion is the job of the Euler-Lagrange equation:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0
$$

This holds for each generalized coordinate $q_i$ independently, so a system with $n$ degrees of freedom produces $n$ coupled second-order differential equations — one per coordinate, no extra bookkeeping required for constraints or curvilinear axes.

**Derivation sketch.** Consider a perturbed path $q(t) + \epsilon \eta(t)$, where $\eta$ vanishes at the fixed endpoints. The action $S = \int L\, dt$ is stationary at the true path, so $\frac{dS}{d\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0} = 0$. Expanding and integrating the $\dot{\eta}$ term by parts (the boundary term drops because $\eta(t_1) = \eta(t_2) = 0$) leaves
$$
\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q} - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}}\right] \eta(t)\, dt = 0.
$$
Since $\eta$ is arbitrary, the bracketed quantity must vanish everywhere — this is the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations, and it hands us the equation of motion directly.

**Worked example: a mass on a spring.** With $L = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx^2$, compute $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} = m\dot{x}$, so $\frac{d}{dt}(m\dot{x}) = m\ddot{x}$. And $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = -kx$. The Euler-Lagrange equation gives $m\ddot{x} = -kx$ — Newton's second law, recovered without ever writing down a force vector.

**Why coordinates don't matter.** The real payoff appears for a pendulum: using arc length would tangle you in constraint forces, but with $\theta$ as the generalized coordinate, $L = \frac{1}{2}m\ell^2\dot{\theta}^2 - mg\ell(1-\cos\theta)$ plugs straight into the same formula to give $\ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta$, with the constraint (fixed rod length) built in automatically. This is the practical power of the Euler-Lagrange equation: pick coordinates that fit the geometry of the problem, write down kinetic minus potential energy, and differentiate — the equations of motion follow mechanically, even for systems where Newtonian free-body diagrams become unwieldy.

### harmonic_oscillator (16.3s)

## Harmonic Oscillator

Newton's second law, $F = ma$, becomes genuinely predictive only once you specify the force. For a mass attached to a spring, Hooke's law gives $F = -kx$: the restoring force is proportional to displacement $x$ from equilibrium and always points back toward it. Combining the two yields the defining equation of the simple harmonic oscillator:

$$
m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx
$$

This is a second-order linear differential equation, and its structure — not just its solution — is worth understanding. Dividing by $m$ and defining $\omega^2 = k/m$ gives $x'' = -\omega^2 x$: the acceleration is always proportional to displacement but opposite in sign. Any function whose second derivative returns a negative multiple of itself is a sine or cosine, so the general solution is

$$
x(t) = A\cos(\omega t) + B\sin(\omega t)
$$

where $A$ and $B$ are set by initial position and velocity, and $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ is the angular frequency, fixed entirely by the physical parameters of the system — not by how far you initially pull the mass.

**Worked example.** A 0.5 kg mass on a spring with $k = 8\ \text{N/m}$ is pulled 0.2 m from equilibrium and released from rest. Then $\omega = \sqrt{8/0.5} = 4\ \text{rad/s}$. Zero initial velocity forces $B = 0$ (since $x'(0) = B\omega = 0$), and $x(0) = A = 0.2$. So $x(t) = 0.2\cos(4t)$ meters, oscillating with period $T = 2\pi/\omega \approx 1.57\ \text{s}$, independent of amplitude — a hallmark of linear restoring forces.

**Why this matters beyond springs.** Near any stable equilibrium, the restoring force is approximately proportional to displacement — this is the same linear relationship as Hooke's law, just with a different proportionality constant determined by the shape of the system's potential energy near that equilibrium. This is why pendulums, molecular bonds, and circuit currents all reduce to $x'' = -\omega^2 x$ for small displacements: the harmonic oscillator isn't one system among many — it's the universal first approximation to *any* system perturbed slightly from stable equilibrium.

### normal_modes (0.0s)

## Normal Modes

A system of coupled oscillators — masses connected by springs, atoms in a molecule, pendulums linked by a rod — generally moves in a tangled way: each mass oscillates at a mixture of frequencies, and displacing one part sets all the others into motion. A **normal mode** is a special pattern of motion in which every part of the system oscillates at the *same* single frequency and in a fixed phase relationship, so the whole system moves as if it were one simple harmonic oscillator. Any complicated motion of the coupled system can be written as a superposition of these normal modes.

The equations of motion for small oscillations about equilibrium take the matrix form $M\ddot{\mathbf{x}} + K\mathbf{x} = 0$, where $\mathbf{x}$ is the vector of displacements, $M$ is the mass matrix, and $K$ is the stiffness matrix. Seeking a solution where all coordinates oscillate together, $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}\,e^{i\omega t}$, turns the differential equation into an algebraic eigenvalue problem:

$$
K\mathbf{v} = \omega^2 M \mathbf{v}.
$$

Each eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_k$ is the mode shape, describing the relative amplitude and direction each mass moves; the corresponding eigenvalue $\omega_k^2$ gives the mode's frequency. This is why normal modes and eigenpairs are the same idea: diagonalizing $K$ in the basis of $M$-orthogonal eigenvectors decouples the equations, turning $N$ tangled oscillators into $N$ independent simple harmonic oscillators, one per mode.

**Worked example.** Two equal masses $m$ connect to walls and to each other by identical springs of constant $k$. The mass and stiffness matrices give eigenvalues $\omega_1^2 = k/m$ (symmetric mode, both masses move in phase, center spring unstretched) and $\omega_2^2 = 3k/m$ (antisymmetric mode, masses move oppositely, center spring stretched twice as much). Any initial displacement of the two masses can be decomposed into these two mode shapes, each evolving independently at its own frequency.

**Problem-solving application.** To analyze an unfamiliar coupled system — three pendulums, a molecule's vibrations, a bridge's sway modes — build $M$ and $K$ from Newton's second law or energy methods, solve the generalized eigenvalue problem numerically, and interpret each eigenvector as a resonance pattern. This approach underlies vibration analysis in engineering and the prediction of molecular vibrational spectra in chemistry.

### molecular_vibrations (12.0s)

## Molecular Vibrations

A molecule with $N$ atoms has $3N$ total degrees of freedom, but 3 correspond to translation of the whole molecule and 3 (or 2, for a linear molecule) to rigid rotation. The remaining $3N-6$ (or $3N-5$) degrees of freedom describe internal motion — the atoms oscillating relative to one another. Near its equilibrium geometry, the potential energy surface is well approximated by a quadratic (harmonic) expansion, and the dynamics reduce to a classic eigenvalue problem in mass-weighted coordinates. Writing the displacement of each atom from equilibrium as $q_i$, Newton's equations give

$$
M\ddot{q} = -Kq,
$$

where $M$ is the diagonal mass matrix and $K$ is the Hessian of the potential energy (the matrix of second derivatives with respect to displacement). Substituting a mass-weighted coordinate $Q = M^{1/2}q$ turns this into $\ddot{Q} = -\tilde{K}Q$ with $\tilde{K} = M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}$, a symmetric matrix. Its eigenvectors are the **normal modes** — collective, independent oscillation patterns — and its eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ give the vibrational angular frequencies via $\omega_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}$, so each mode behaves exactly like an independent harmonic oscillator with frequency $\omega_k$.

**Worked example.** Water ($N=3$, nonlinear) has $3(3)-6 = 3$ vibrational modes: a symmetric stretch, an antisymmetric stretch, and a bend. Diagonalizing $\tilde{K}$ (computed from quantum chemistry, e.g., density functional theory) yields three eigenvalues, converted to frequencies of roughly 3650, 3750, and 1595 cm$^{-1}$ — values that match water's experimental infrared absorption peaks almost exactly, because IR light is absorbed precisely when its photon energy matches a $\Delta E = \hbar\omega_k$ vibrational transition.

**Problem-solving application.** This is quantum chemistry's daily eigenvalue exercise: build $K$ from an electronic structure calculation, diagonalize the mass-weighted Hessian, and read the eigenvalues as an IR spectrum before ever running an experiment. It also flags errors — a negative eigenvalue ($\lambda_k < 0$, imaginary frequency) means the structure sits at a saddle point, not a true minimum, signaling that the geometry optimization hasn't converged to a stable molecule. Software packages (Gaussian, Psi4, ORCA) automate exactly this pipeline: geometry $\to$ Hessian $\to$ eigenvalue decomposition $\to$ predicted spectrum.
