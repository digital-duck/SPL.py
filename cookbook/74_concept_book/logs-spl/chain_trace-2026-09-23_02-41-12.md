## Coefficient Of Kinetic Friction

When two surfaces slide against each other, the friction force resisting that motion is not fixed — it scales with how hard the surfaces are pressed together. The proportionality constant that links them is the coefficient of kinetic friction, $\mu_k$, defined by

$$
f_k = \mu_k N
$$

where $f_k$ is the kinetic friction force (opposing the relative sliding, acting along the contact surface) and $N$ is the normal force between the surfaces. Because $\mu_k$ is a ratio of two forces, it is dimensionless — a pure number describing how "grippy" or "slippery" a particular pair of materials is once they are already sliding. It is always found empirically, by measuring $f_k$ and $N$ for real surfaces, never derived from first principles. Crucially, $\mu_k < \mu_s$ (the coefficient of static friction) for essentially every material pair: it takes more force to start an object sliding than to keep it sliding, which is why a stalled crate suddenly "gives" and accelerates once it breaks free.

**Worked example.** A 20 kg wooden crate slides across a concrete floor with $\mu_k = 0.30$. The normal force equals the crate's weight, $N = mg = (20\ \text{kg})(9.8\ \text{m/s}^2) = 196\ \text{N}$. The kinetic friction force is then

$$
f_k = \mu_k N = (0.30)(196\ \text{N}) = 58.8\ \text{N}.
$$

This force acts opposite to the crate's velocity regardless of speed — kinetic friction depends only on $N$ and $\mu_k$, not on how fast the object is moving.

**Problem-solving application.** Kinetic friction is what lets you solve for deceleration and stopping distance once motion has begun. Suppose a 1000 kg car skids to a stop with $\mu_k = 0.70$ between tires and dry pavement. The friction force is $f_k = \mu_k mg = (0.70)(1000)(9.8) = 6860\ \text{N}$, giving a deceleration $a = f_k/m = 6.86\ \text{m/s}^2$. From there, the kinematic relation $v^2 = v_0^2 - 2ad$ yields the stopping distance for any initial speed $v_0$: for instance, from $v_0 = 20\ \text{m/s}$, the car stops in $d = v_0^2/(2a) \approx 29.2\ \text{m}$. Notice that friction alone supplies the deceleration here — no other force acts to slow the car — so once $\mu_k$ and $N$ are known, the rest of the problem is ordinary kinematics.
