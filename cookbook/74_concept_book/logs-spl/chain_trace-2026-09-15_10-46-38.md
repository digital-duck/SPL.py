### 木 (5.9s)

## 木

Picture a tree standing alone in a field: a straight trunk, with branches spreading out to the left and right. That's exactly what 木 looks like — a simple drawing of a tree, trunk and branches and all.

Now here's the surprising part. This little tree-brick doesn't always do the same job. Sometimes it's hired for its *meaning* — "tree" or "wood." Put two trees side by side and you get 林, a forest. Add a tree next to a resting person and you get 休, someone leaning against a tree trunk to rest. In both cases, 木 is telling you: something to do with trees or wood is happening here.

But sometimes 木 is hired for its *sound* instead — like an actor cast just for their voice. In 沐, meaning "to bathe," the water radical carries the meaning, while 木 simply lends its pronunciation, "mu."

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree,\ trunk+branches} \rightarrow \text{meaning role (林, 休)} \ \ \text{or} \ \ \text{sound role (沐)}
$$

Now you try: next time you spot 木 in a new character, ask — is it painting a picture, or just singing a note?

### 林 (5.4s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing alone in a yard. Now imagine two trees standing close together — not just "trees," but something new: a little patch of woods, thick and shady.

That's the magic trick this character plays. Chinese doesn't always invent a brand-new symbol for a bigger idea. Sometimes it just repeats what you already know! One tree, 木, means "tree." Put two of them side by side, and you get 林 — "woods," a small forest.

Why does this feel right? Think about how you'd draw "a lot of trees" if you only had one tree-symbol to work with. You'd draw it twice! That's exactly what happened here — doubling a symbol to mean "more of it" or "a group of it."

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林 \; (\text{woods})
$$

**Now you try:** If 木 is one tree, and 林 is two trees making woods, can you guess what THREE trees together might mean? (Hint: think even bigger — a whole forest!)

### 森 (7.9s)

## 森

Imagine one tree standing alone — that's 木. Now imagine two trees close together, like friends leaning in — that's 林, a small grove. But what if trees keep going and going, so many you can't count them, so thick the sunlight barely reaches the ground? That's a forest, and in Chinese, we write it as 森 (sēn) — three trees stacked together.

Notice the pattern: one tree isn't enough to feel "forest," two trees feel like a cozy grove, but three trees feels like it never ends. The character grows brick by brick, and each brick adds a feeling of *more* — more shade, more trees, more wildness. The number of trees drawn is literally the size of the idea.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森\ (forest)
$$

Now you try: if one 木 is a tree and 林 is a small woods, what do you think a character with *four* trees might mean? Draw it and guess!
