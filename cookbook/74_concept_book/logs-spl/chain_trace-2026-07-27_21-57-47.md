## Minimum Cut

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E)$ be a directed graph with a source vertex $s \in V$, a sink vertex $t \in V$, and a capacity function $c : E \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$. An **$s$–$t$ cut** is a partition $(S, T)$ of $V$ such that $s \in S$ and $t \in T$. The **capacity** of the cut is

$$
c(S, T) = \sum_{\substack{u \in S,\, v \in T \\ (u,v) \in E}} c(u, v).
$$

A **minimum cut** is an $s$–$t$ cut whose capacity is as small as possible over all such partitions. Intuitively, it identifies the "bottleneck" set of edges whose removal disconnects $s$ from $t$ at minimum cost.

**Worked Example.** Consider the graph with vertices $\{s, a, b, t\}$ and directed edges:

$$
s \to a\ (c=3),\quad s \to b\ (c=2),\quad a \to t\ (c=2),\quad b \to t\ (c=3),\quad a \to b\ (c=1).
$$

Evaluate the cut $S = \{s, a\}$, $T = \{b, t\}$. The forward edges crossing the cut are $s \to b$ and $a \to t$ and $a \to b$, giving capacity $2 + 2 + 1 = 5$. Now try $S = \{s\}$, $T = \{a, b, t\}$: forward edges are $s \to a$ and $s \to b$, capacity $3 + 2 = 5$. The cut $S = \{s, b\}$, $T = \{a, t\}$ yields only $s \to a$ and $b \to t$, capacity $3 + 3 = 6$. The minimum cut in this graph has capacity $5$, achieved by the partition $(\{s\}, \{a, b, t\})$.

**Key Theorem (Max-Flow Min-Cut).** For any network $(G, s, t, c)$,

$$
\max_{f} |f| \;=\; \min_{(S,T)} c(S, T),
$$

where the maximum is taken over all feasible $s$–$t$ flows $f$ and the minimum over all $s$–$t$ cuts. This theorem, due to Ford and Fulkerson (1956), guarantees that the value of a maximum flow equals the capacity of a minimum cut. A minimum cut can therefore be recovered from any max-flow solution by performing a reachability search from $s$ in the residual graph.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import networkx as nx

G = nx.DiGraph()
G.add_edge('s', 'a', capacity=3)
G.add_edge('s', 'b', capacity=2)
G.add_edge('a', 't', capacity=2)
G.add_edge('b', 't', capacity=3)
G.add_edge('a', 'b', capacity=1)

cut_value, (S, T) = nx.minimum_cut(G, 's', 't')
print(f"Min-cut capacity: {cut_value}")   # 5
print(f"S = {S}, T = {T}")
```

Running this cell confirms the minimum cut capacity of $5$ and identifies the reachable set $S$ in the residual graph after saturation of the max-flow.

## Project Selection

**Definition.** The *project selection problem* is a combinatorial optimization problem in which a decision-maker must choose a subset $S$ of projects from a universe $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}$ so as to maximize total value subject to resource constraints. Formally, let $v_i \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ denote the value of project $p_i$, let $\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^m$ be its vector of resource requirements across $m$ resource types, and let $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^m$ be the budget vector. Introducing binary decision variables $x_i \in \{0,1\}$, the problem is:

$$\maximize \sum_{i=1}^{n} v_i x_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{w}_i x_i \leq \mathbf{b}, \quad x_i \in \{0,1\}.$$

This is the **multi-dimensional 0–1 knapsack problem**, a canonical NP-hard integer program. When $m=1$, it reduces to the classical knapsack problem.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a research office has a budget of $B = 10$ units and evaluates four projects:

| Project | Value $v_i$ | Cost $w_i$ |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| $p_1$   | 6           | 4          |
| $p_2$   | 5           | 3          |
| $p_3$   | 4           | 3          |
| $p_4$   | 3           | 2          |

The greedy heuristic ranks by value-to-cost ratio: $p_1 \to 1.5$, $p_2 \to 1.67$, $p_3 \to 1.33$, $p_4 \to 1.5$. Greedily selecting $p_2, p_1, p_4$ consumes $3+4+2=9$ units and yields value $14$. Exhaustive search confirms $\{p_2, p_3, p_4\}$ costs $8$ units and yields $12$, while $\{p_1, p_2, p_4\}$ achieves $14$—the optimum here.

**Theorem (FPTAS for 0–1 Knapsack).** For the single-resource 0–1 knapsack problem, there exists a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme (FPTAS): for any $\varepsilon > 0$, a solution of value at least $(1-\varepsilon)\,\text{OPT}$ can be found in time $O\!\left(n^2 / \varepsilon\right)$ via dynamic programming on scaled values $\tilde{v}_i = \lfloor v_i / \theta \rfloor$ with scaling factor $\theta = \varepsilon \max_i v_i / n$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational
from itertools import combinations

projects = [("p1", 6, 4), ("p2", 5, 3), ("p3", 4, 3), ("p4", 3, 2)]
B = 10

best_val, best_set = 0, []
for r in range(1, len(projects) + 1):
    for combo in combinations(projects, r):
        cost = sum(c for _, _, c in combo)
        val  = sum(v for _, v, _ in combo)
        if cost <= B and val > best_val:
            best_val, best_set = val, [name for name, _, _ in combo]

print(f"Optimal projects: {best_set}, Total value: {best_val}")
```
