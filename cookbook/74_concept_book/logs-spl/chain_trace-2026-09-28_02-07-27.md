## Seven Years War 1756

The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) was the first conflict fought on a genuinely global scale, with battles in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and the Philippines. Winston Churchill later called it "the first world war." Two rival coalitions faced off: Britain and Prussia against France, Austria, Russia, and Spain. The war combined a European struggle over Prussian expansion with a colonial struggle between Britain and France over trade routes, sugar islands, fur territory, and Indian textile wealth. In North America the same war is known as the French and Indian War (1754–1763), and it began there two years before fighting formally opened in Europe.

Consider the war's outcome through the 1763 Treaty of Paris. France ceded all of Canada and its territory east of the Mississippi to Britain, along with most of its Indian trading posts (retaining only a handful of coastal stations). Spain, France's ally, ceded Florida to Britain but received Louisiana from France as compensation. Britain emerged holding the largest colonial empire in the world and effective commercial and political control over Bengal and much of India through the East India Company, whose armies had crushed French-backed rivals at Plassey (1757) and Wandiwash (1760).

The deeper significance lies in the treasuries left behind. Britain's war debt roughly doubled to about £133 million, prompting the taxation of American colonies (Stamp Act, 1765; Townshend Acts, 1767) that fed directly into the American Revolution. France's debt was proportionally worse relative to its smaller and less efficient tax base: historians estimate the war left the French crown over a billion livres in debt, a burden the monarchy never resolved. Successive finance ministers — Turgot, Necker, Calonne — failed to reform a tax system that exempted the nobility and clergy, and by 1789 the crown's inability to service this debt forced Louis XVI to summon the Estates-General, the opening act of the French Revolution.

To apply this causally: trace a single decision — Britain's 1765 Stamp Act — back to its funding source (postwar debt service) and forward to its consequence (colonial boycotts, the Sons of Liberty, and ultimately 1776). The same debt-to-crisis logic, applied to France's ledger instead of Britain's, explains why a war won decisively by Britain became the financial trigger for revolution in the country that lost it.
