### assassination_1914 (23.6s)

## Assassination 1914

Imagine a single spark landing on a pile of dry wood that has been stacking up for years. On its own, one spark is nothing — but if the wood is dry enough, the whole pile goes up in flames. That is what happened on June 28, 1914, in Sarajevo.

Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, was riding through the city in an open car. A young Bosnian Serb nationalist named Gavrilo Princip shot and killed him and his wife, Sophie. Princip belonged to a group that wanted Bosnia to break away from Austria-Hungary and join Serbia.

Why did one shooting nearly burn down all of Europe? Because the "wood" was already dry: alliances bound country to country like a chain of promises, and empires distrusted each other deeply. Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and issued a harsh ultimatum. Within five weeks, that single act pulled Germany, Russia, France, and Britain into what became World War I — a war that would kill over 15 million people.

**Now you try:** Think of a small argument between two friends that pulled in many other people because everyone had already taken sides. What made it spread so fast?

### war_declarations_1914 (11.4s)

## War Declarations 1914

Imagine dominoes standing in a giant circle, each one tied by string to several others. Push one domino, and it doesn't just fall — it yanks the strings and pulls down dominoes all around the circle, even ones far away that never touched the first one.

That's what happened in the summer of 1914. On July 28, Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia to punish it for the assassination. But European countries had made promises — treaties — to defend each other. Russia had promised to protect Serbia, so it began preparing its army. Germany had promised to back Austria-Hungary, so it declared war on Russia on August 1, then on France on August 3. Because Germany's plan to attack France meant marching through Belgium, Britain — which had promised to protect Belgium — declared war on Germany on August 4.

In just one week, five great powers were at war. No single country wanted this whole war — each only kept its own promise. But the promises were tangled together like string, so pulling one meant pulling them all.

**Now you try:** if Country A promises to help Country B, and Country B promises to help Country C, what happens to Country A if someone attacks Country C?

### schlieffen_plan_1914 (8.7s)

## Schlieffen Plan 1914

Imagine a boxer who plans every punch of a fight years in advance, certain his opponent will stand still. That was Germany's problem in 1914. Its generals, fearing a two-front war against France and Russia, had a plan drawn up years earlier by Alfred von Schlieffen: swing a huge army through neutral Belgium, race around Paris, and crush France in about six weeks — before slow-mobilizing Russia could even finish gathering its troops in the east.

The plan looked perfect on paper. In reality, invading Belgium dragged Britain into the war to defend Belgian neutrality, Belgian and French resistance slowed the German advance, and exhausted German soldiers, marching over 20 miles a day, outran their own supply lines. In September 1914, French and British forces stopped the Germans at the Marne River, just short of Paris. The "quick knockout" failed. Both sides dug trenches instead, and a war expected to last weeks stretched into more than four years.

Now you try: think of a plan you once made assuming everything would go exactly as expected. What actually happened when reality didn't cooperate?

### trench_warfare_1915 (8.9s)

## Trench Warfare 1915

Imagine two kids racing to build the tallest sandcastle wall, each one digging faster whenever the other digs. Neither wins — they just end up facing two walls with a ditch between them. That's the Western Front in 1915.

After the Schlieffen Plan failed to deliver a quick German victory, both sides dug in — literally. Soldiers carved trenches stretching nearly 475 miles, from the Belgian coast to the Swiss border. Machine guns could fire 400–600 rounds a minute, and artillery could shred anyone crossing the open ground between the trenches, called "no man's land." Attacking meant running straight into that fire. At battles like Neuve Chapelle and Second Ypres in 1915, tens of thousands of soldiers were killed or wounded while barely moving the front line a few hundred yards.

This wasn't bad luck — it was a new kind of war. Defense had become far stronger than offense, so armies that used to win by charging forward now won by staying put and surviving.

Now you try: if digging in made attacking so costly, why do you think generals kept ordering attacks anyway in 1915?

### gallipoli_1915 (10.7s)

## Gallipoli 1915

Imagine trying to open a locked back door because the front door is jammed. In 1915, the Western Front in France was stuck — two armies dug into trenches, neither able to move. So Britain and France looked for another way to help their ally Russia and knock a weaker enemy, the Ottoman Empire, out of the war. Their plan: land at Gallipoli, a narrow peninsula guarding the sea route to the Ottoman capital, Constantinople.

It seemed like a shortcut. But the "back door" was itself guarded fiercely. Ottoman soldiers, led partly by a young officer named Mustafa Kemal, held the high cliffs above the beaches. Allied troops — including many soldiers from Australia and New Zealand — landed on April 25, 1915, and found themselves trapped on narrow strips of sand, unable to advance for months.

By the time the Allies gave up and evacuated in January 1916, roughly 250,000 Ottoman and 250,000 Allied soldiers had been killed or wounded combined. The "shortcut" had failed completely — proving that even a clever new plan can fail if the enemy defends just as hard as on the old front.

**Now you try:** If your front door is stuck, is a side door automatically an easier way in? What would you need to check first?

### verdun_somme_1916 (9.7s)

## Verdun Somme 1916

Imagine two kids in a tug-of-war, pulling with all their strength for a whole year — and the rope doesn't move an inch. That's Verdun and the Somme in 1916.

At Verdun, the German army attacked a French fortress town, hoping to "bleed France white." The battle lasted ten months. When it finally ended, both sides together had suffered roughly 700,000 casualties — and the front line had barely shifted. A few miles later that same year, British and French troops attacked German lines along the Somme River. On the very first day, July 1, 1916, the British army lost about 20,000 men killed — the deadliest single day in its history. By the battle's end in November, over a million men were killed or wounded, for a few miles of gained ground.

These battles taught commanders a hard lesson: machine guns, artillery, and trenches made old-style charging attacks incredibly costly, while defense had become far stronger than attack.

Now you try: If a battle lasted ten months and cost 700,000 casualties but moved the front line only a few miles, what does that tell you about how the weapons of 1916 compared to the weapons of earlier wars?

### us_entry_1917 (8.7s)

## Us Entry 1917

Imagine you promised your neighbor you'd stay out of their fight — until one day you find a secret note where they plot to help your rival attack your own backyard. That's close to what happened to the United States in early 1917.

For years, President Woodrow Wilson had kept America neutral, even after a German submarine sank the passenger ship Lusitania in 1915, killing 128 Americans. But in January 1917, Germany announced it would sink *any* ship — even American ones — heading toward Britain, to starve the Allies into surrender. Then British codebreakers intercepted the Zimmermann Telegram: a secret German message asking Mexico to attack the United States if war came, promising Mexico back its lost territory in return.

That was the final straw. On April 6, 1917, Congress declared war on Germany. America was far from the trenches, but it brought something the exhausted Allies desperately needed: millions of fresh soldiers and a booming industrial economy, tipping the balance after years of stalemate at places like Verdun and the Somme.

Now you try: if you found a note like the Zimmermann Telegram, would it change your mind about staying "neutral"? Why?

### russian_revolution_1917 (7.9s)

## Russian Revolution 1917

Imagine a country at war for three years, its soldiers starving in the trenches, its cities out of bread, and its Tsar still living in a palace. That was Russia in early 1917. By November of that year, workers, soldiers, and peasants had had enough — and a small, disciplined party called the Bolsheviks, led by Vladimir Lenin, seized power in Petrograd almost overnight.

The Bolsheviks had promised the exhausted Russian people "Peace, Land, and Bread." To keep the first promise, they had to get out of the war immediately — no matter the cost. In March 1918, they signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany, surrendering huge territories including Ukraine and Poland just to stop the fighting.

Here is why this mattered far beyond Russia: Germany no longer needed to fight on two fronts. Hundreds of thousands of German troops in the east were now free to entrain west, reinforcing the war against Britain and France just as the United States was still gathering its own army.

Now you try: If you were a German general in spring 1918, why would Russia's exit feel like a golden, but narrow, window of opportunity?

### hundred_days_1918 (9.2s)

## Hundred Days 1918

Imagine a tug-of-war team that has just used every ounce of strength for one last mighty pull — and missed. That's what happened to Germany in spring 1918. Its huge offensive gained ground but exhausted its soldiers, its supplies, and its hope. Then, starting on August 8, 1918, the Allies pulled back — hard, and without stopping.

British General Henry Rawlinson called that first day "the black day of the German Army": tanks, airplanes, and fresh American troops smashed through lines the Germans thought were solid. From then on, Allied forces kept attacking, week after week, giving German soldiers no time to recover. It wasn't one dramatic battle — it was a hundred days of relentless pressure, each day chipping away a little more.

By November, German commanders told their leaders the war could not be won. Germany signed an armistice on November 11, 1918, ending the fighting.

Now you try: Why might a hundred days of steady pushing succeed where one giant offensive failed? Think about what happens to a team's energy when there's no rest between plays.

### armistice_1918 (8.2s)

## Armistice 1918

Imagine a clock in a train carriage in a forest near Compiègne, France. At 5:00 a.m. on November 11, 1918, German and Allied officials sign a document around a table inside that railway car. The paper does not say "Germany loses." It says: stop shooting, in six hours. Why wait six hours? Because armies scattered across hundreds of miles needed time to get the news — no phones for every soldier, just messengers and telegraph wires racing the clock.

At 11:00 a.m. — the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month — the guns fall silent. After four years, roughly 9 million soldiers dead, and empires (German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, Russian) collapsing, the fighting simply stops. But an armistice is only a ceasefire, a promise to quit fighting while leaders negotiate — not a final peace treaty. That formal peace, the Treaty of Versailles, would not be signed until nearly a year later, in June 1919.

**Now you try**: If a ceasefire stops the fighting but doesn't decide the peace terms, what questions do you think still needed answering between November 1918 and June 1919?
