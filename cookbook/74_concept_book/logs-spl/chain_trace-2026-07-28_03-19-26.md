### difference_quotient (0.0s)

## Difference Quotient

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval containing $x$ and $x + h$, where $h \neq 0$. The *difference quotient* of $f$ at $x$ with increment $h$ is the expression

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.$$

Geometrically, this quantity equals the slope of the secant line passing through the points $\bigl(x,\, f(x)\bigr)$ and $\bigl(x+h,\, f(x+h)\bigr)$ on the graph of $f$, measuring the average rate of change of $f$ over the interval $[x,\, x+h]$ (or $[x+h,\, x]$ when $h < 0$). Both of those ideas — secant slope and average rate of change — are assumed familiar; what is new here is the single algebraic expression that unifies them.

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$. Compute the difference quotient and simplify.

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h.$$

The factor $h$ cancels for all $h \neq 0$, leaving the expression $2x + h$, which is defined everywhere in the plane except along the line $h = 0$. Observe that as $h$ decreases toward zero, the value $2x + h$ approaches $2x$: the secant slopes converge, and their common limit is what calculus will later call the derivative. That step — passing to the limit — belongs to the next section; here the goal is simply to see how cleanly the difference quotient simplifies.

**Key Theorem** *(Existence of the Limit).* If the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ converges to a finite value $L$ as $h \to 0$ from both sides, then $L$ is unique and equals the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $\bigl(x, f(x)\bigr)$.

*Proof sketch.* Uniqueness follows directly from the uniqueness of limits: if two values $L_1$ and $L_2$ both satisfy the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ criterion for the same limit, then $|L_1 - L_2| < 2\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$, forcing $L_1 = L_2$. The geometric interpretation — that the limiting secant slope equals the tangent slope — follows because the tangent line is defined as precisely that limit.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, simplify, limit

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**2

dq = (f.subs(x, x + h) - f) / h
dq_simplified = simplify(dq)
print("Difference quotient:", dq_simplified)   # 2*x + h
print("Limit as h -> 0:",  limit(dq_simplified, h, 0))  # 2*x
```

Running this cell confirms the algebraic cancellation and previews the limiting value, grounding the formal definition in concrete symbolic computation.

### limit (0.0s)

## Limit

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on a punctured neighbourhood of a point $a \in \mathbb{R}$. We say that the *limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $a$* equals $L$, written

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L,$$

if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that

$$0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon.$$

The condition $0 < |x - a|$ is essential: the value $f(a)$ itself is irrelevant, or may not even exist. The definition formalises the intuition that $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by choosing $x$ sufficiently close (but not equal) to $a$.

---

**Worked example.** We verify $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2}(3x - 1) = 5$ directly from the definition.

Given $\varepsilon > 0$, we need $|3x - 1 - 5| < \varepsilon$, i.e., $3|x - 2| < \varepsilon$. Choose $\delta = \varepsilon/3$. Then

$$0 < |x - 2| < \delta \implies |f(x) - 5| = 3|x - 2| < 3\delta = \varepsilon. \quad \checkmark$$

The choice $\delta = \varepsilon/3$ was found by working backwards from the desired inequality — a standard technique in $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ proofs.

---

**Key theorem (Limit laws).** If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M$, then:

$$\lim_{x \to a}[f(x) + g(x)] = L + M, \qquad \lim_{x \to a}[f(x) \cdot g(x)] = L \cdot M,$$

and, provided $M \neq 0$,

$$\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}.$$

These laws reduce the verification of composite limits to simpler ones, eliminating the need for a fresh $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ argument each time.

---

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit, sin, oo

x = symbols('x')

# Fundamental trigonometric limit
print(limit(sin(x) / x, x, 0))          # 1

# Limit at infinity
print(limit((3*x**2 + 1) / (x**2 - 4), x, oo))  # 3

# One-sided limits
print(limit(abs(x) / x, x, 0, '+'))     # 1
print(limit(abs(x) / x, x, 0, '-'))     # -1
```

The last two calls illustrate that a two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits agree, connecting the symbolic machinery to the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition.

### derivative_at_a_point (0.0s)

## Derivative At A Point

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval containing $a$. The *derivative of $f$ at the point $a$*, denoted $f'(a)$, is defined as the limit

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists and is finite. When it does, $f$ is said to be *differentiable at $a$*. Geometrically, $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$.

---

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$. We compute $f'(3)$ directly from the definition.

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 3^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h}.$$

Factoring the numerator:

$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h(6 + h)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(6 + h) = 6.$$

Thus $f'(3) = 6$, meaning the tangent line to the parabola at $x = 3$ has slope $6$. The tangent line itself is $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$, or equivalently $y = 6x - 9$.

---

**Key Theorem** *(Differentiability implies continuity).* If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $f(a+h) - f(a) = \dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor converges to $f'(a)$ and the second to $0$, so the product tends to $0$. Hence $\lim_{h\to 0} f(a+h) = f(a)$. $\square$

The converse fails: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there, since the left- and right-hand limits of the difference quotient are $-1$ and $1$, respectively.

---

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**2
a = 3

# Derivative via limit definition
deriv_def = limit((f.subs(x, a + h) - f.subs(x, a)) / h, h, 0)

print(deriv_def)   # prints 6
```

### amount_of_change_formula (0.0s)

## Amount Of Change Formula

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function defined on an interval $[a, b]$. The *amount of change* of $f$ over $[a, b]$ is the net difference between the output at the right endpoint and the output at the left endpoint:

$$\Delta f = f(b) - f(a).$$

This quantity is signed: if $f(b) > f(a)$, the function has increased by $\Delta f$; if $f(b) < f(a)$, the function has decreased by $|\Delta f|$. The amount of change must be distinguished from the *rate* of change, which divides $\Delta f$ by the elapsed input $\Delta x = b - a$. The amount of change captures the absolute displacement in the output value, independent of how rapidly or slowly that displacement occurred.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a particle's position (in metres) is given by $s(t) = 3t^2 - 2t + 1$, where $t$ is measured in seconds. Find the amount of change in position from $t = 1$ to $t = 4$.

Compute each endpoint:

$$s(1) = 3(1)^2 - 2(1) + 1 = 2, \qquad s(4) = 3(16) - 8 + 1 = 41.$$

Therefore:

$$\Delta s = s(4) - s(1) = 41 - 2 = 39 \text{ m}.$$

The particle moved a net $39$ metres in the positive direction over the three-second interval, regardless of any back-and-forth motion it may have undergone in between.

**Key Theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2).** If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ (so that $F' = f$), then:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = F(b) - F(a) = \Delta F.$$

This theorem reveals that the definite integral computes precisely the amount of change in an antiderivative. Integration and the amount-of-change formula are therefore two sides of the same coin: the integral accumulates infinitesimal contributions $f(x)\,dx$ to produce the total change $\Delta F$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, integrate, simplify

t = symbols('t')
s = 3*t**2 - 2*t + 1

delta_s = s.subs(t, 4) - s.subs(t, 1)
print("Amount of change:", delta_s)          # 39

# Verify via integration of velocity
v = s.diff(t)
integral_result = integrate(v, (t, 1, 4))
print("Via FTC:", integral_result)            # 39
```

## Marginal Functions

**Definition.** Let $C(q)$ denote the total cost of producing $q$ units of a good, where $q \geq 0$ and $C$ is differentiable on its domain. The *marginal cost function* is defined as

$$MC(q) = C'(q) = \lim_{\Delta q \to 0} \frac{C(q + \Delta q) - C(q)}{\Delta q}.$$

More generally, for any differentiable economic quantity $f(q)$ — revenue, profit, utility — the corresponding *marginal function* is its derivative $f'(q)$. Economically, $MC(q)$ approximates the additional cost incurred when output increases by one infinitesimal unit beyond $q$; for discrete production, $C(q+1) - C(q)$ serves as the practical approximation.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a firm's total cost function is

$$C(q) = 0.5q^3 - 4q^2 + 20q + 50,$$

where the constant $50$ represents fixed costs. Differentiating term by term:

$$MC(q) = C'(q) = 1.5q^2 - 8q + 20.$$

At $q = 4$: $MC(4) = 1.5(16) - 8(4) + 20 = 24 - 32 + 20 = 12.$ This means producing the fourth unit (approximately) costs an additional \$12. Notice that $MC$ is a parabola opening upward with minimum at $q^* = \tfrac{8}{3} \approx 2.67$, reflecting the typical U-shape of short-run marginal cost driven by diminishing returns.

**Key Theorem (Profit Maximisation Condition).** Let $R(q)$ denote total revenue and $\pi(q) = R(q) - C(q)$ total profit. If $\pi$ attains an interior maximum at $q^*$, then

$$MR(q^*) = MC(q^*),$$

where $MR(q) = R'(q)$ is marginal revenue. *Proof sketch.* At an interior maximum, $\pi'(q^*) = 0$, which gives $R'(q^*) - C'(q^*) = 0$, i.e.\ $MR = MC$. The second-order condition $\pi''(q^*) < 0$ confirms a maximum. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, latex

q = symbols('q', positive=True)
C = 0.5*q**3 - 4*q**2 + 20*q + 50
R = 30*q                          # competitive firm: price = 30

MC = diff(C, q)
MR = diff(R, q)
q_star = solve(MR - MC, q)

print("MC =", latex(MC))
print("Profit-maximising q:", q_star)
```

Running this cell yields $MC = 1.5q^2 - 8q + 20$ and the optimal quantity satisfying $MR = MC$ under the given revenue schedule.
