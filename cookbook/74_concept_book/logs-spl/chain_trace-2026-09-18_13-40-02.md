## 日

**Definition.** 日 (rì) is a pictograph denoting "sun" and, by extension, "day." Its earliest oracle-bone forms render the sun as a circle with a central dot or short horizontal stroke, marking a sunspot or simply distinguishing the sign from an empty circle (a distinction the script needed, since undifferentiated circles were graphically ambiguous with other signs). As the script moved from bone and bronze to brush and ink, the round outline was squared into the rectangular frame familiar in modern print and handwriting — a change driven by the tool, not the referent: a brush moving in straight strokes on a flat surface naturally regularizes curves into angles, a process called reification of the pictograph, or more properly here, a shift from iconic to conventionalized graphic form.

**Mechanism.** 日 functions in the modern script chiefly as a semantic radical (part of Kangxi radical 72), meaning it recurs as the meaning-bearing component in phono-semantic compounds involving light, time, or weather. In such compounds it typically supplies the semantic domain while a second component supplies (or once supplied) the pronunciation:

$$\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun/light}} + \underbrace{青}_{\text{qīng, phonetic}} = 晴 \;(\text{qíng, ``clear/sunny weather''})$$

$$\underbrace{日}_{\text{time}} + \underbrace{寺}_{\text{sì, phonetic (drifted)}} = 時/时 \;(\text{shí, ``time/hour''})$$

The character also serves as a bound or free morpheme in compounds denoting temporal units: 日 + 本 → 日本 ("origin of the sun," Japan); 明 = 日 + 月 ("sun + moon" → "bright"), an ideographic rather than phono-semantic pairing.

**Literature context.** The reading of 日's earliest forms as strictly pictographic has been qualified by paleographers (Qiu Xigui's *Chinese Writing* is the standard synthesis): the internal mark's function — sunspot representation versus a disambiguating stroke against near-identical signs like 曰 (yuē, "to say") — remains debated, and the graphic history of squaring is documented but not fully explained mechanically (tool constraint vs. deliberate standardization under Qin/Han script reform). Comparative work on how radical 日 seeds semantic fields (time, weather, brightness) across the compound lexicon is active in computational approaches to character decomposition for OCR and pedagogy.

## 晶

**Definition.** 晶 (jīng) denotes brilliance, sparkle, crystalline clarity — the quality of light scattered or refracted by a faceted, transparent, or polished surface. It survives in modern Mandarin almost exclusively as a bound morpheme, most visibly in 水晶 (shuǐjīng, "rock crystal" / "quartz," literally "water-brilliance") and in 晶体 (jīngtǐ, "crystal" in the technical, crystallographic sense) and 晶片 (jīngpiàn, "chip/wafer," a 20th-century semiconductor-industry coinage). The character is composed of three instances of 日 (rì, "sun") stacked in a pyramid — one above, two below:

$$\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} = 晶 \;(\text{sparkle, crystal brilliance})$$

**Mechanism.** This is an instance of the "tripled graph" (三疊形, *sāndiéxíng*) principle found across the script: a single pictograph is repeated three times to intensify or abstract its base meaning, rather than to count three literal instances of the referent. In the case of 晶, three suns do not denote three literal suns but the *superlative brightness* associated with the sun — an early graphic metaphor for radiance transferred from the celestial body to any brilliant, light-scattering surface. Historically, 晶 is itself the etymon from which 星 (xīng, "star") derives: the oracle-bone and bronze forms show 晶 written above 生 (a phonetic), and the "three suns" component was later reduced to a single 日 in 星, leaving 晶 to specialize for brightness/crystal while 星 specialized for "star." This bifurcation is a documented case of graphic economy — simplification of a semantically loaded repeated component once two characters need to be distinguished by function.

**Literature context.** The tripling pattern is treated systematically in studies of Chinese logographic compounding (e.g. Qiu Xigui's *Chinese Writing*) as a subclass of huiyi (會意, "associative compound") formation, but the *semantic function* of repetition — intensification of a quality versus abstraction of a collective versus literal plurality — is not uniformly theorized across the full set of tripled graphs in the script; 晶 itself is generally classed as the intensification type, since the repeated sun encodes degree of brightness rather than a count of suns. Comparable typological cases exist in other logographic and iconic writing traditions (e.g. Sumerian cuneiform sign doubling for plurality), inviting cross-script comparison of when repetition of a pictographic unit encodes count versus degree.

**Suggested investigation.** Catalog the full inventory of tripled-graph characters in a standard character-frequency corpus, classify each by whether the tripling encodes (a) literal plurality, (b) spatial/collective abstraction, or (c) qualitative intensification — using 晶's intensification pattern as the reference case — and test whether semantic category correlates with the referent's ontological type (mass-noun referents trending toward abstraction or intensification, count-noun referents trending toward plurality). A secondary study could trace the 晶/星 graphic divergence through paleographic corpora (oracle bone, bronze, seal script) to date the point at which the "three suns" component was reduced to one in 星.
