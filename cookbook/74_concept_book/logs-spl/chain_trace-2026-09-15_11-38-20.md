### 子 (7.0s)

## 子

Imagine a tiny baby wrapped up snug in a blanket, arms tucked in, head peeking out at the top. That's exactly what the character 子 is drawing — a picture of a swaddled infant, wiggling but held tight.

Look at the shape: a rounded top for the head, and two crossing strokes below like little wrapped arms and a wrapped body. It feels right because it *looks* like a bundle — cozy, small, and just beginning.

But 子 grew up to do more than mean "child." Chinese speakers noticed that many small, round, or "offspring-like" things could borrow this friendly little character as a helper ending. Attach 子 to a noun, and it often just says "here's a solid little thing" — like adding a gentle label of *thing-ness*:

$$
\underbrace{椅}_{chair} + \underbrace{子}_{noun\text{-}ending} = 椅子 \ (chair)
$$

$$
\underbrace{桌}_{table} + \underbrace{子}_{noun\text{-}ending} = 桌子 \ (table)
$$

**Now you try:** The character 帽 means "hat." What do you think 帽子 means?

### 女 (6.1s)

## 女

Imagine you're drawing a picture of your grandmother sitting quietly, folding her hands, kneeling on a mat the way people did thousands of years ago in old China. Someone looked at that peaceful, graceful pose and turned it into a picture-word: 女. Look closely — you can almost see the curved arms and the bent knee, can't you?

This little picture doesn't just mean "woman." It became something even bigger: a building block. Ancient writers noticed that so many important ideas — mother, sister, marriage, peace, a good person — all connect back to women and family. So they started using 女 like a stamp, a signal inside other characters that says "this word is about family or relationships."

$$
\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman, kneeling}} \; \xrightarrow{+\text{other pieces}} \; \text{many family words}
$$

Now you try: draw 女 yourself — a curve for the arms, a line for the kneeling leg. Can you imagine what character might appear if you added a roof above her, like she's inside a house?

### 宀 (5.9s)

## 宀

Imagine you're tucking a tiny toy house under a blanket to keep it safe and warm. That little peaked shape draped over the top — that's exactly what 宀 looks like: a roof stretching from one wall to the other, sheltering whatever sits beneath it.

Why does this feel right? Because a roof is the first thing that makes a house *feel* like a house — not the walls, not the floor, but that cozy cover overhead. Chinese characters borrow this same feeling: whenever you see 宀 sitting on top of another piece, you know something is being sheltered, settled, or kept safe inside.

Think of 宀 as a promise: "whatever is under me is protected." A pig under this roof becomes a *home* (家). A woman under this roof becomes *peaceful* (安). The roof doesn't just sit there — it changes the meaning of what's beneath it.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{\text{something}}_{\text{sheltered}} = \text{a settled, housed idea}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw a roof shape over a stick figure. Doesn't it already look like a tiny home?

### 好 (5.8s)

## 好

Imagine peeking into a home three thousand years ago. What would tell you, at a glance, that everything was well? Not gold, not a full pantry — but a mother sitting with her child. That single picture became a word.

The character 好 places two people side by side: a woman on the left, a child on the right. Ancient scribes didn't need to describe happiness — they just drew the scene that *felt* like happiness. A parent and a little one, together, safe. That's goodness.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \underbrace{好}_{\text{good}}$$

Over time, 好 grew to mean "good" in almost any sense — good food, good weather, good friends — but it never lost that original warmth. Every time someone writes 好, they're quietly echoing that ancient scene of care.

**Now you try:** Look at 好 and picture the two halves as two people standing together. Can you think of another pair — like two friends, or a sun and a flower — that together would also mean "good" to you?

### 字 (6.5s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny house with a roof, and under that roof, a baby. That's the story hiding inside this character: a child (子) sheltered under a roof (宀). Long ago, this picture came to mean any character at all — because that's exactly what characters are: little ideas that get "born" and then "raised" inside the writing system, protected and passed down like a family.

Why does this feel right? Think about how you learned your very first words. Someone sheltered you, taught you, and slowly you grew. Chinese characters work the same way — new ones are built by combining older pieces, the way a child grows up learning from what came before.

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字 \;(\text{character, to give birth to})
$$

This is such a fitting image that the whole science of studying how characters connect to each other is even named after it: ZiNets, or "字 networks" — houses full of characters, all related like family.

Now you try: think of one word you know that "grew" from two smaller words put together. What shelter and what child make it up?

### vocabulary_compounds (6.2s)

## Vocabulary Compounds

Imagine you already built a LEGO tree and a LEGO person. Now what if you snapped the tree brick next to *another* tree brick to build a forest? That's exactly what Chinese words do — they snap whole characters together to build something new.

You already saw that one character can be built from smaller pieces, like radicals. But here's the surprising part: characters themselves are just pieces too! Two trees become a forest. "Bright" plus "day" becomes "tomorrow." "Good" plus "person" becomes "a good person." The same snapping trick happens one level higher up.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \quad(forest)
$$

$$
\underbrace{明}_{bright} + \underbrace{天}_{day} = 明天 \quad(tomorrow)
$$

$$
\underbrace{好}_{good} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = 好人 \quad(a\ good\ person)
$$

Once a character is finished, it doesn't stop being useful — it becomes a brick for the next, bigger idea.

**Now you try**: Take 安 (peace) and 全 (whole/complete). What word do you think they build together? Hint: it means "safe."
