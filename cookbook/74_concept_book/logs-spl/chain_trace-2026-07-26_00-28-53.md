### rate_of_change (16.0s)

## Rate Of Change

**Definition.** Let $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. The *average rate of change* of $f$ over an interval $[a, b]$ is the ratio

$$\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.$$

The *instantaneous rate of change* of $f$ at a point $x = a$ is the limit of this ratio as the interval shrinks to zero:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists. When it does, $f$ is said to be *differentiable* at $a$, and $f'(a)$ is called the derivative of $f$ at $a$. Geometrically, $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a,\, f(a))$.

**Worked Example.** Consider $f(x) = x^2$. Compute the instantaneous rate of change at $x = 3$.

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (6 + h) = 6.$$

Thus the tangent line at $(3, 9)$ has slope $6$ and equation $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$, i.e., $y = 6x - 9$.

**Key Theorem** *(Power Rule).* For any $n \in \mathbb{R}$, if $f(x) = x^n$, then

$$f'(x) = n\,x^{n-1}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply the binomial theorem to $(x+h)^n$, expand, divide by $h$, and take $h \to 0$. All terms containing a positive power of $h$ vanish, leaving $n x^{n-1}$. $\square$

The power rule, together with linearity of the derivative, reduces differentiation of any polynomial to an entirely algebraic procedure.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')
f = x**2
f_prime = sp.diff(f, x)          # Power rule applied symbolically
slope_at_3 = f_prime.subs(x, 3)

print("f'(x) =", f_prime)        # 2*x
print("f'(3) =", slope_at_3)     # 6

# Visualise the tangent line
tangent = slope_at_3 * (x - 3) + f.subs(x, 3)
print("Tangent line: y =", sp.expand(tangent))  # 6*x - 9
```

Running this cell confirms the analytic result and illustrates how symbolic computation can verify hand calculations before proceeding to more complex functions.

### limit (0.0s)

## Limit

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on a punctured neighbourhood of a point $a \in \mathbb{R}$. We say that the *limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $a$* equals $L$, written

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L,$$

if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that

$$0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon.$$

The condition $0 < |x - a|$ is essential: the value $f(a)$ itself is irrelevant, or may not even exist. The definition formalises the intuition that $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by choosing $x$ sufficiently close (but not equal) to $a$.

---

**Worked example.** We verify $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2}(3x - 1) = 5$ directly from the definition.

Given $\varepsilon > 0$, we need $|3x - 1 - 5| < \varepsilon$, i.e., $3|x - 2| < \varepsilon$. Choose $\delta = \varepsilon/3$. Then

$$0 < |x - 2| < \delta \implies |f(x) - 5| = 3|x - 2| < 3\delta = \varepsilon. \quad \checkmark$$

The choice $\delta = \varepsilon/3$ was found by working backwards from the desired inequality — a standard technique in $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ proofs.

---

**Key theorem (Limit laws).** If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M$, then:

$$\lim_{x \to a}[f(x) + g(x)] = L + M, \qquad \lim_{x \to a}[f(x) \cdot g(x)] = L \cdot M,$$

and, provided $M \neq 0$,

$$\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}.$$

These laws reduce the verification of composite limits to simpler ones, eliminating the need for a fresh $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ argument each time.

---

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit, sin, oo

x = symbols('x')

# Fundamental trigonometric limit
print(limit(sin(x) / x, x, 0))          # 1

# Limit at infinity
print(limit((3*x**2 + 1) / (x**2 - 4), x, oo))  # 3

# One-sided limits
print(limit(abs(x) / x, x, 0, '+'))     # 1
print(limit(abs(x) / x, x, 0, '-'))     # -1
```

The last two calls illustrate that a two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits agree, connecting the symbolic machinery to the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition.

### secant_line (15.0s)

## Secant Line

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function defined on an interval containing two distinct points $x_1$ and $x_2$. The *secant line* through $(x_1, f(x_1))$ and $(x_2, f(x_2))$ is the unique straight line passing through both points. Its slope is given by the *difference quotient*

$$m_{\sec} = \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1}.$$

Writing $x_2 = x_1 + h$ for some $h \neq 0$, this becomes

$$m_{\sec} = \frac{f(x_1 + h) - f(x_1)}{h},$$

a form that will prove central when $h \to 0$ in the definition of the derivative.

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$ and take $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 3$.

$$m_{\sec} = \frac{f(3) - f(1)}{3 - 1} = \frac{9 - 1}{2} = 4.$$

The equation of the secant line follows from the point-slope form using $(1, 1)$:

$$y - 1 = 4(x - 1) \implies y = 4x - 3.$$

Geometrically, this line cuts the parabola at two points and represents the *average rate of change* of $f$ over $[1, 3]$. As $x_2 \to x_1$ (equivalently, $h \to 0$), the secant line rotates about $(x_1, f(x_1))$ and, when the limit exists, converges to the tangent line at that point.

**Key Theorem (Secant–Tangent Limit).** If $f$ is differentiable at $x_1$, then

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_1 + h) - f(x_1)}{h} = f'(x_1),$$

and the slope of the secant line converges to the slope of the tangent line. This limit is precisely the definition of the derivative, making the secant line the finite approximation whose limit yields instantaneous rate of change.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, simplify

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**2

difference_quotient = simplify((f.subs(x, x + h) - f) / h)
print("Difference quotient:", difference_quotient)   # 2*x + h

tangent_slope = difference_quotient.subs(h, 0)
print("Tangent slope at x:", tangent_slope)          # 2*x
```

Running this cell confirms that the difference quotient $2x + h$ reduces to $f'(x) = 2x$ as $h \to 0$, illustrating the secant-to-tangent transition symbolically.

### tangent_line (13.3s)

## Tangent Line

**Definition.** Let $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function defined on an open interval $I$, and let $a \in I$. The *tangent line* to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$ is the unique line passing through $(a, f(a))$ whose slope equals the derivative of $f$ at $a$, provided that derivative exists. Formally, the tangent line has equation

$$
L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a),
$$

where $f'(a) = \displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$. Geometrically, $L$ is the limiting position of the secant line through $(a, f(a))$ and $(a+h, f(a+h))$ as $h \to 0$; the tangent line captures the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at $a$.

**Worked Example.** Find the tangent line to $f(x) = x^3 - 2x$ at $a = 1$.

*Step 1 — evaluate $f$ at $a$:* $f(1) = 1 - 2 = -1$, so the point of tangency is $(1, -1)$.

*Step 2 — compute $f'(x)$:* Differentiating term by term gives $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2$.

*Step 3 — evaluate the slope:* $f'(1) = 3(1)^2 - 2 = 1$.

*Step 4 — write the equation:* $L(x) = -1 + 1 \cdot (x - 1) = x - 2$.

One may verify: $L(1) = -1 = f(1)$ and the slope $1$ matches $f'(1)$. Near $x = 1$, the error $f(x) - L(x) = x^3 - 3x + 2 = (x-1)^2(x+2)$ vanishes to second order, confirming $L$ is a first-order approximation.

**Theorem (Tangent as Best Linear Approximation).** If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$ satisfies

$$
\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - L(x)}{x - a} = 0.
$$

No other line through $(a, f(a))$ has this property. This characterises the tangent line as the unique *first-order* local linearisation of $f$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, lambdify, plot

x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 2*x
a = 1
slope = diff(f, x).subs(x, a)
L = f.subs(x, a) + slope * (x - a)
plot(f, L, (x, -1, 3), legend=True,
     label=['f(x)', 'Tangent at x=1'])
```

### derivative (14.4s)

## Derivative

**Definition.** Let $f : (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function and let $x_0 \in (a, b)$. The *derivative* of $f$ at $x_0$ is defined as the limit

$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists and is finite. When the limit exists, $f$ is said to be *differentiable* at $x_0$. The quantity $\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}$ is the *difference quotient*, representing the slope of the secant line through $(x_0, f(x_0))$ and $(x_0+h, f(x_0+h))$; the derivative is the slope of the tangent line obtained as $h \to 0$.

**Worked Example.** Compute $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^3$.

Applying the definition directly:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h}.$$

Expanding $(x+h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3$ gives

$$\frac{(x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3) - x^3}{h} = 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2.$$

Taking $h \to 0$ yields $f'(x) = 3x^2$, confirming the power rule $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$ for $n = 3$.

**Key Theorem (Differentiability Implies Continuity).** If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0) = \frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor converges to $f'(x_0)$ and the second factor converges to $0$, so $\lim_{h \to 0}[f(x_0+h) - f(x_0)] = f'(x_0) \cdot 0 = 0$, establishing continuity. The converse fails: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $x = 0$. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, limit, Function, simplify

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**3

# Derivative via limit definition
dq = (f.subs(x, x + h) - f) / h
deriv_limit = limit(dq, h, 0)
print("Limit definition:", deriv_limit)   # 3*x**2

# Derivative via diff
deriv_diff = diff(f, x)
print("diff():", deriv_diff)              # 3*x**2

assert simplify(deriv_limit - deriv_diff) == 0
```

Both methods confirm $\frac{d}{dx} x^3 = 3x^2$, and `simplify` verifies that the two expressions are identical.

### differential_calculus (12.9s)

## Differential Calculus

**Definition.** Let $f : (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval. The *derivative* of $f$ at a point $x_0 \in (a, b)$ is defined as the limit

$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists and is finite. When $f'(x_0)$ exists, we say $f$ is *differentiable* at $x_0$. Geometrically, $f'(x_0)$ equals the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(x_0, f(x_0))$. Analytically, it measures the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ with respect to $x$.

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 2x$. We compute $f'(1)$ directly from the definition:

$$f'(1) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(1+h)^3 - 2(1+h) - (1^3 - 2 \cdot 1)}{h}.$$

Expanding the numerator: $(1 + 3h + 3h^2 + h^3) - (2 + 2h) - (-1) = h + 3h^2 + h^3$. Dividing by $h$ and taking $h \to 0$ yields $f'(1) = 1$. The tangent line at $(1, -1)$ therefore has equation $y = x - 2$.

**Key Theorem (Differentiability Implies Continuity).** If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0) = \dfrac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor converges to $f'(x_0)$ and the second to $0$, so the product tends to $0$, giving $\lim_{h\to 0} f(x_0 + h) = f(x_0)$. $\square$

The converse fails: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there, since the left- and right-hand limits of the difference quotient are $-1$ and $+1$, respectively.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')
f = x**3 - 2*x

df   = sp.diff(f, x)          # symbolic derivative: 3x² - 2
val  = df.subs(x, 1)          # evaluate at x=1: 1
line = val*(x - 1) + f.subs(x, 1)   # tangent line: x - 2

print("f'(x) =", df)
print("f'(1) =", val)
print("tangent at x=1:", line)
```
