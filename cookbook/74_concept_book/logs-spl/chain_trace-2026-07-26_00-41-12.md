### derivative (0.0s)

## Derivative

**Definition.** Let $f : (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function and let $x_0 \in (a, b)$. The *derivative* of $f$ at $x_0$ is defined as the limit

$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists and is finite. When the limit exists, $f$ is said to be *differentiable* at $x_0$. The quantity $\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}$ is the *difference quotient*, representing the slope of the secant line through $(x_0, f(x_0))$ and $(x_0+h, f(x_0+h))$; the derivative is the slope of the tangent line obtained as $h \to 0$.

**Worked Example.** Compute $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^3$.

Applying the definition directly:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h}.$$

Expanding $(x+h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3$ gives

$$\frac{(x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3) - x^3}{h} = 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2.$$

Taking $h \to 0$ yields $f'(x) = 3x^2$, confirming the power rule $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$ for $n = 3$.

**Key Theorem (Differentiability Implies Continuity).** If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0) = \frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor converges to $f'(x_0)$ and the second factor converges to $0$, so $\lim_{h \to 0}[f(x_0+h) - f(x_0)] = f'(x_0) \cdot 0 = 0$, establishing continuity. The converse fails: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $x = 0$. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, limit, Function, simplify

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**3

# Derivative via limit definition
dq = (f.subs(x, x + h) - f) / h
deriv_limit = limit(dq, h, 0)
print("Limit definition:", deriv_limit)   # 3*x**2

# Derivative via diff
deriv_diff = diff(f, x)
print("diff():", deriv_diff)              # 3*x**2

assert simplify(deriv_limit - deriv_diff) == 0
```

Both methods confirm $\frac{d}{dx} x^3 = 3x^2$, and `simplify` verifies that the two expressions are identical.

### absolute_extrema (0.0s)

## Absolute Extrema

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function defined on a domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. A value $f(c)$ is called the *absolute maximum* of $f$ on $D$ if $f(c) \geq f(x)$ for every $x \in D$. Similarly, $f(c)$ is the *absolute minimum* of $f$ on $D$ if $f(c) \leq f(x)$ for every $x \in D$. Together, absolute maxima and minima are called *absolute extrema* (or *global extrema*). Unlike local extrema, which describe behavior in a neighborhood of a point, absolute extrema make a claim about the entire domain.

The theoretical guarantee for their existence rests on the following result.

**Extreme Value Theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on a closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $[a, b]$.

The proof proceeds by showing that a continuous function on a compact set is bounded (using the Heine–Borel theorem) and then that the supremum of its range is achieved at some point $c \in [a,b]$. The hypotheses are essential: on an open interval, $f(x) = x$ has no maximum; on a closed interval, a function with a jump discontinuity may fail to attain its supremum.

**Finding Absolute Extrema — Closed Interval Method.** For $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$:

1. Compute $f'(x)$ and solve $f'(x) = 0$ to find all *critical points* $c_i \in (a,b)$.
2. Evaluate $f$ at each critical point and at the endpoints $a$ and $b$.
3. The largest value in step 2 is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum.

**Worked Example.** Find the absolute extrema of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 2$ on $[-2, 2]$.

Differentiating gives $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$, so the critical points are $x = -1$ and $x = 1$. Evaluating:

$$f(-2) = -8 + 6 + 2 = 0, \quad f(-1) = -1 + 3 + 2 = 4, \quad f(1) = 1 - 3 + 2 = 0, \quad f(2) = 8 - 6 + 2 = 4.$$

The absolute maximum is $4$, attained at $x = -1$ and $x = 2$; the absolute minimum is $0$, attained at $x = -2$ and $x = 1$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, Rational
x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x + 2
crit = solve(diff(f, x), x)
candidates = crit + [-2, 2]
values = {pt: f.subs(x, pt) for pt in candidates}
print("Candidate values:", values)
print("Absolute max:", max(values, key=values.get), "→", max(values.values()))
print("Absolute min:", min(values, key=values.get), "→", min(values.values()))
```

### critical_point (0.0s)

## Critical Point

**Definition.** Let $f : D \subseteq \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a differentiable function on an open interval $D$. A point $c \in D$ is called a *critical point* of $f$ if

$$f'(c) = 0.$$

More generally, if $f$ is only defined on a closed interval $[a, b]$, the endpoints $a$ and $b$ are also considered critical points in the search for extrema, even when $f'$ exists there. The value $f(c)$ is called the *critical value* corresponding to $c$. Critical points are candidates for local maxima, local minima, or saddle points (inflection points with zero slope); the first and second derivative tests distinguish among these cases.

**Worked Example.** Consider $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$ on $\mathbb{R}$. To locate the critical points, compute

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1).$$

Setting $f'(x) = 0$ yields $x = 1$ and $x = -1$. Evaluating: $f(-1) = (-1)^3 - 3(-1) + 1 = 3$ and $f(1) = 1 - 3 + 1 = -1$. The second derivative is $f''(x) = 6x$. Since $f''(-1) = -6 < 0$, the point $x = -1$ is a local maximum; since $f''(1) = 6 > 0$, the point $x = 1$ is a local minimum.

**Key Theorem (Fermat's Theorem).** *If $f$ is differentiable on an open interval and $f$ attains a local extremum at $c$, then $f'(c) = 0$.*

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $f$ has a local maximum at $c$. For small $h > 0$, the difference quotient satisfies

$$\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \leq 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{f(c-h)-f(c)}{-h} \geq 0.$$

Taking $h \to 0^+$ and $h \to 0^-$ respectively forces $f'(c) \leq 0$ and $f'(c) \geq 0$, hence $f'(c) = 0$. The local minimum case is analogous. $\blacksquare$

Note that the converse fails: $f(x) = x^3$ satisfies $f'(0) = 0$, yet $x = 0$ is neither a maximum nor a minimum.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x + 1

f_prime = sp.diff(f, x)
critical_pts = sp.solve(f_prime, x)

for c in critical_pts:
    f2 = sp.diff(f_prime, x).subs(x, c)
    nature = "local max" if f2 < 0 else "local min" if f2 > 0 else "inconclusive"
    print(f"x = {c}, f(x) = {f.subs(x, c)}, f''(x) = {f2} → {nature}")
```

### local_extrema (0.0s)

## Local Extrema

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval $I$, and let $c \in I$. We say $f$ has a **local maximum** at $c$ if there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $f(c) \geq f(x)$ for all $x \in (c - \delta, c + \delta)$. Symmetrically, $f$ has a **local minimum** at $c$ if $f(c) \leq f(x)$ for all $x$ in some such neighbourhood. Both are called **local extrema**. A point $c$ at which $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ is undefined is called a **critical point**; every local extremum at an interior point is a critical point, though the converse fails.

**Worked Example.** Consider $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on $\mathbb{R}$. Differentiating gives $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$, so the critical points are $x = -1$ and $x = 1$. Evaluating: $f(-1) = -(-1) + 3 = 2$ and $f(1) = 1 - 3 = -2$. Because $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $x = -1$, there is a local maximum of $2$ there; because $f'$ changes from negative to positive at $x = 1$, there is a local minimum of $-2$ there. Note that neither is a global extremum, since $f(x) \to \pm\infty$ as $x \to \pm\infty$.

**Theorem (Second Derivative Test).** Let $f$ be twice differentiable at a critical point $c$ where $f'(c) = 0$.
- If $f''(c) > 0$, then $f$ has a local minimum at $c$.
- If $f''(c) < 0$, then $f$ has a local maximum at $c$.
- If $f''(c) = 0$, the test is inconclusive.

*Proof sketch.* By Taylor's theorem, $f(c + h) = f(c) + f'(c)h + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)h^2 + O(h^3)$. Since $f'(c) = 0$, the sign of $f(c+h) - f(c)$ near $h = 0$ is determined by $\tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)h^2$, which is positive when $f''(c) > 0$ and negative when $f''(c) < 0$. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, solveset, S
x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x
fp  = diff(f, x)
fpp = diff(fp, x)
crits = solve(fp, x)
for c in crits:
    print(f"x={c}: f={f.subs(x,c)}, f''={fpp.subs(x,c)} → "
          f"{'min' if fpp.subs(x,c)>0 else 'max'}")
```

### extreme_value_theorem (0.0s)

## Extreme Value Theorem

**Definition.** Let $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous function on a closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$. The *Extreme Value Theorem* (EVT) states that $f$ attains both its absolute maximum and absolute minimum on $[a, b]$; that is, there exist points $c, d \in [a, b]$ such that

$$f(d) \leq f(x) \leq f(c) \quad \text{for all } x \in [a, b].$$

Two hypotheses are indispensable: *continuity* of $f$ and *compactness* of the domain. Dropping either destroys the conclusion. The function $f(x) = x$ on the open interval $(0, 1)$ is continuous yet attains neither bound; the function $f(x) = 1/x$ on $[1, 2]$ is continuous and does attain both extremes, but $f(x) = 1/x$ on $(0, 1]$ (discontinuous at 0) is unbounded above.

**Worked Example.** Find the absolute extrema of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on $[-2, 2]$.

*Step 1 — Interior critical points.* Compute $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$, so $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = \pm 1$.

*Step 2 — Boundary values.* Evaluate $f$ at the two endpoints and the two critical points:

| $x$ | $f(x)$ |
|-----|--------|
| $-2$ | $(-8)+6 = -2$ |
| $-1$ | $(-1)+3 = 2$ |
| $1$ | $1 - 3 = -2$ |
| $2$ | $8 - 6 = 2$ |

*Conclusion.* The absolute maximum is $f(-1) = f(2) = 2$ and the absolute minimum is $f(-2) = f(1) = -2$.

**Key Theorem (EVT, formal statement).** Every continuous function on a compact set $K \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is bounded and attains its bounds. The proof relies on two facts from real analysis: (i) the continuous image of a compact set is compact, and (ii) every compact subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is closed and bounded, hence contains its supremum and infimum.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, Rational

x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x
a, b = -2, 2

crit = solve(diff(f, x), x)
candidates = [c for c in crit if a <= c <= b] + [a, b]
values = {c: f.subs(x, c) for c in candidates}

print("Candidates and values:", values)
print("Maximum:", max(values, key=values.get), "=", max(values.values()))
print("Minimum:", min(values, key=values.get), "=", min(values.values()))
```

### fermats_theorem (0.0s)

## Fermats Theorem

**Definition.** Let $p$ be a prime and let $a$ be an integer with $\gcd(a, p) = 1$. *Fermat's Little Theorem* states that

$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}.$$

Equivalently, for *any* integer $a$ (including multiples of $p$), we have $a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}$. The hypothesis $\gcd(a,p)=1$ is essential in the first form: if $p \mid a$ then $a^{p-1}$ need not reduce to $1$ modulo $p$.

**Worked Example.** Take $p = 7$ and $a = 3$. We wish to verify that $3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$.

Compute successive powers modulo $7$:

$$3^1 \equiv 3,\quad 3^2 \equiv 2,\quad 3^3 \equiv 6,\quad 3^4 \equiv 4,\quad 3^5 \equiv 5,\quad 3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}.$$

The cycle closes exactly at exponent $p-1 = 6$, confirming the theorem. As a practical payoff, suppose we need $3^{100} \pmod{7}$. Write $100 = 6 \cdot 16 + 4$; then

$$3^{100} = \bigl(3^6\bigr)^{16} \cdot 3^4 \equiv 1^{16} \cdot 4 \equiv 4 \pmod{7}.$$

**Key Theorem (Proof Sketch).** Consider the set $S = \{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\}$. Multiplying every element by $a$ modulo $p$ permutes $S$ (since $p$ is prime and $\gcd(a,p)=1$, the map $x \mapsto ax \bmod p$ is a bijection on $S$). Therefore

$$\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(ak) \equiv \prod_{k=1}^{p-1} k \pmod{p},$$

which gives $a^{p-1}(p-1)! \equiv (p-1)! \pmod{p}$. Because $\gcd\bigl((p-1)!,\, p\bigr)=1$, we cancel $(p-1)!$ to obtain $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. $\blacksquare$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import isprime, mod_inverse

p, a = 7, 3
assert isprime(p) and p % a != 0          # preconditions

# Verify Fermat's Little Theorem directly
print(pow(a, p - 1, p))                   # should print 1

# Fast modular exponentiation via the theorem
exponent = 100
reduced  = exponent % (p - 1)             # 100 mod 6 = 4
result   = pow(a, reduced, p)
print(result)                             # should print 4

# Tabulate a^(p-1) mod p for a in 1..p-1
print([pow(a, p - 1, p) for a in range(1, p)])  # all ones
```

Running this cell should print `1`, `4`, and a list of six ones, numerically confirming each claim above.

### limits_at_infinity (0.0s)

## Limits At Infinity

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function defined on some interval $(a, \infty)$. We say that the *limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches infinity* is $L$, written

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L,$$

if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $M > 0$ such that whenever $x > M$, we have $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. Geometrically, this means the graph of $f$ approaches the horizontal line $y = L$ as $x$ grows without bound; such a line is called a **horizontal asymptote**. An analogous definition holds for $x \to -\infty$.

---

**Worked Example.** Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5x + 1}{2x^2 + 7}$.

Divide every term in the numerator and denominator by $x^2$, the highest power appearing:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3 - \tfrac{5}{x} + \tfrac{1}{x^2}}{2 + \tfrac{7}{x^2}}.$$

As $x \to \infty$, each term of the form $\tfrac{c}{x^k}$ (with $k \geq 1$) tends to $0$, leaving

$$\frac{3 - 0 + 0}{2 + 0} = \frac{3}{2}.$$

Hence the function has the horizontal asymptote $y = \tfrac{3}{2}$ in both directions.

---

**Key Theorem (Rational Function Limits at Infinity).** Let $f(x) = p(x)/q(x)$ where $p$ and $q$ are polynomials of degrees $m$ and $n$, respectively, and $q(x) \neq 0$ for all sufficiently large $x$.

- If $m < n$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = 0$.
- If $m = n$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \frac{a_m}{b_n}$, the ratio of leading coefficients.
- If $m > n$, then $|f(x)| \to \infty$ and no finite limit exists.

*Proof sketch.* Factor $x^n$ from $q(x)$ and $x^m$ from $p(x)$; the remaining factors each approach their leading coefficients, and $x^{m-n} \to 0$, $1$, or $\pm\infty$ according to the sign of $m - n$.

---

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit, oo, Rational

x = symbols('x')
f = (3*x**2 - 5*x + 1) / (2*x**2 + 7)

print(limit(f, x,  oo))   # Expected: 3/2
print(limit(f, x, -oo))   # Expected: 3/2

g = (x**3 + 1) / (x**2 - 4)
print(limit(g, x, oo))    # Expected: oo  (degree 3 > degree 2)
```

### rolles_theorem (13.7s)

## Rolle's Theorem

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function satisfying three hypotheses on a closed interval $[a, b]$:

1. $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$,
2. $f$ is differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$,
3. $f(a) = f(b)$.

Then there exists at least one point $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f'(c) = 0$.

Geometrically, if a smooth curve starts and ends at the same height, it must have at least one horizontal tangent somewhere between the endpoints. This is the essence of Rolle's Theorem: equal endpoint values force a critical point to exist in the interior.

**Proof sketch.** Since $f$ is continuous on the compact set $[a, b]$, the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that $f$ attains both a maximum $M$ and a minimum $m$ on $[a, b]$. If $M = m$, then $f$ is constant and $f'(c) = 0$ for every $c \in (a, b)$. Otherwise, at least one extremum is achieved at some interior point $c \in (a, b)$. At such a point, differentiability forces $f'(c) = 0$, because a non-zero derivative would allow a local perturbation to exceed the extremal value — a contradiction.

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on $[-\sqrt{3},\, \sqrt{3}]$.

Check the hypotheses: $f$ is a polynomial, hence continuous and differentiable everywhere. At the endpoints,
$$f(-\sqrt{3}) = (-\sqrt{3})^3 - 3(-\sqrt{3}) = -3\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{3} = 0 = f(\sqrt{3}).$$
All three conditions hold. Differentiating, $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$. Setting $f'(c) = 0$ gives $c^2 = 1$, so $c = \pm 1$. Both values lie in $(-\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{3})$, confirming two points with horizontal tangents.

**Key theorem (formal statement).** Rolle's Theorem is a special case of the Mean Value Theorem. Conversely, the Mean Value Theorem follows from Rolle's by applying the latter to the auxiliary function $g(x) = f(x) - \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x - a)$.

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import *
x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x
a, b = -sqrt(3), sqrt(3)
print("f(a) =", f.subs(x, a), "  f(b) =", f.subs(x, b))
critical = solve(diff(f, x), x)
interior = [c for c in critical if a < c < b]
print("Interior critical points:", interior)
```

Running this cell confirms $f(a) = f(b) = 0$ and recovers $c \in \{-1, 1\}$, matching the hand calculation above.

### mean_value_theorem (14.3s)

## Mean Value Theorem

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function that is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$. The **Mean Value Theorem (MVT)** states that there exists at least one point $c \in (a, b)$ such that

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.$$

Geometrically, the theorem guarantees the existence of a point at which the tangent line to the graph of $f$ is parallel to the secant line connecting $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$. The quantity $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ is precisely the average rate of change of $f$ over $[a, b]$, so the MVT asserts that an instantaneous rate must equal the average rate at some interior point.

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on $[0, 2]$. We verify the hypotheses: $f$ is a polynomial, hence continuous on $[0, 2]$ and differentiable on $(0, 2)$. Compute the average rate of change:

$$\frac{f(2) - f(0)}{2 - 0} = \frac{(8 - 6) - 0}{2} = 1.$$

Setting $f'(c) = 1$ with $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$ gives $3c^2 - 3 = 1$, so $c^2 = \tfrac{4}{3}$ and $c = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.155$. Since $c \in (0, 2)$, the MVT is satisfied.

**Key Theorem (Proof Sketch).** The MVT follows from **Rolle's Theorem**: if $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $g(a) = g(b)$, then $g'(c) = 0$ for some $c \in (a,b)$. Define the auxiliary function

$$g(x) = f(x) - \left[f(a) + \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)\right].$$

Then $g(a) = g(b) = 0$ and $g$ inherits the continuity and differentiability of $f$. By Rolle's Theorem, $g'(c) = 0$ for some $c \in (a,b)$, which yields $f'(c) = \dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. $\blacksquare$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, Rational, sqrt

x, c = symbols('x c')
f = x**3 - 3*x
a, b = 0, 2

avg_rate = (f.subs(x, b) - f.subs(x, a)) / (b - a)
fprime = diff(f, x)
mvt_points = solve(fprime - avg_rate, x)
interior = [p for p in mvt_points if a < p < b]
print("Average rate:", avg_rate)       # 1
print("MVT point(s):", interior)       # [2/sqrt(3)]
```

### concavity (12.1s)

## Concavity

**Definition.** Let $f$ be twice differentiable on an open interval $I$. We say $f$ is **concave up** on $I$ if $f''(x) > 0$ for all $x \in I$, and **concave down** on $I$ if $f''(x) < 0$ for all $x \in I$. Geometrically, $f$ is concave up when its graph lies above every tangent line, and concave down when it lies below every tangent line. A point $c$ where $f''$ changes sign is called an **inflection point** of $f$.

More formally, $f$ is concave up on $I$ if and only if $f'$ is strictly increasing on $I$. This reformulation connects the second derivative to the rate of change of slope, which is the geometrically intuitive picture: a curve bending upward like a bowl has a slope that steadily increases from left to right.

**Worked example.** Consider $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 2$. Compute:
$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x, \qquad f''(x) = 6x - 6 = 6(x - 1).$$
Setting $f''(x) = 0$ gives $x = 1$. Since $f''(x) < 0$ for $x < 1$ and $f''(x) > 0$ for $x > 1$, the function is concave down on $(-\infty, 1)$ and concave up on $(1, \infty)$. The point $(1,\, f(1)) = (1, 0)$ is an inflection point.

**Key theorem (Second Derivative Test).** Let $f$ be twice differentiable and suppose $f'(c) = 0$.
- If $f''(c) > 0$, then $f$ has a **local minimum** at $c$.
- If $f''(c) < 0$, then $f$ has a **local maximum** at $c$.
- If $f''(c) = 0$, the test is inconclusive.

*Proof sketch.* If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0$, then $f'$ is increasing through zero at $c$, so $f'$ changes from negative to positive there — the characterizing condition for a local minimum. The maximum case is symmetric. $\square$

**Lab cell.**
```python
import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x**2 + 2

f2 = sp.diff(f, x, 2)
inflection_pts = sp.solve(f2, x)

concave_up   = sp.solve(f2 > 0, x)
concave_down = sp.solve(f2 < 0, x)

print("f''(x)       =", f2)
print("Inflection x =", inflection_pts)
print("Concave up   :", concave_up)
print("Concave down :", concave_down)
```

### end_behavior (15.4s)

## End Behavior

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function whose domain contains an unbounded interval. The *end behavior* of $f$ describes the values that $f(x)$ approaches as $x \to +\infty$ or as $x \to -\infty$. Formally, we say $f$ has *right end behavior limit* $L \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty, +\infty\}$ if

$$\lim_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = L,$$

and analogously for the left end. When these limits are finite, the horizontal line $y = L$ is called a *horizontal asymptote*. When the limit is infinite, the function is said to *diverge* in that direction, and the sign of divergence (positive or negative) constitutes the end behavior.

---

**Worked Example.** Consider the rational function

$$f(x) = \frac{3x^2 - 5x + 1}{x^2 + 4}.$$

To determine end behavior, divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ present, namely $x^2$:

$$f(x) = \frac{3 - \tfrac{5}{x} + \tfrac{1}{x^2}}{1 + \tfrac{4}{x^2}}.$$

As $x \to \pm\infty$, every term containing a power of $x$ in the denominator vanishes, yielding

$$\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \frac{3 - 0 + 0}{1 + 0} = 3.$$

Hence $y = 3$ is a horizontal asymptote on both ends. The function approaches this line from below on the left and from above on the right, as can be verified by examining the sign of $f(x) - 3 = \tfrac{-5x - 11}{x^2 + 4}$.

---

**Key Theorem** *(End Behavior of Polynomials).* Let $p(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0$ with $a_n \neq 0$. Then

$$\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} p(x) = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} a_n x^n.$$

That is, end behavior is governed entirely by the leading term. If $n$ is even, both ends share the same infinite direction (both $+\infty$ if $a_n > 0$, both $-\infty$ if $a_n < 0$). If $n$ is odd, the ends diverge in opposite directions.

---

```python
right_end = limit(f, x,  oo)
left_end  = limit(f, x, -oo)

print("Right end behavior:", right_end)   # 3
print("Left end behavior: ", left_end)    # 3
```

Running this cell confirms the analytic result: both limits equal $3$, validating $y = 3$ as a two-sided horizontal asymptote.

### mvt_corollaries (20.9s)

## Mvt Corollaries

**Definition.** The Mean Value Theorem (MVT) guarantees that if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then there exists $c \in (a,b)$ such that $f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. Several important results follow directly from this single fact, each narrowing or extending its conclusion under additional hypotheses.

**Corollary 1 (Zero Derivative Implies Constant).** If $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x \in (a,b)$, then $f$ is constant on $(a,b)$.

*Proof sketch.* Pick any $x_1, x_2 \in (a,b)$ with $x_1 < x_2$. By the MVT, there exists $c \in (x_1,x_2)$ such that $f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) = 0$. Hence $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. $\square$

**Corollary 2 (Equal Derivatives Imply Functions Differ by a Constant).** If $f'(x) = g'(x)$ for all $x \in (a,b)$, then $f(x) - g(x) = C$ for some constant $C$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply Corollary 1 to $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$, which satisfies $h'(x) = 0$. $\square$

This corollary is the theoretical backbone of the indefinite integral: antiderivatives are unique up to an additive constant.

**Corollary 3 (Monotonicity Test).** If $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (a,b)$, then $f$ is strictly increasing on $(a,b)$; if $f'(x) < 0$, then $f$ is strictly decreasing.

*Proof sketch.* For $x_1 < x_2$ in $(a,b)$, the MVT gives $f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1)$. Since $x_2 - x_1 > 0$ and $f'(c) > 0$, we conclude $f(x_2) > f(x_1)$. $\square$

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on $(-\infty, \infty)$. Then $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$. Corollary 3 tells us $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty,-1)$ and $(1,\infty)$ (where $f' > 0$) and decreasing on $(-1,1)$ (where $f' < 0$).

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, oo, Interval, S

x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x
fp = diff(f, x)
critical = solve(fp, x)          # [-1, 1]
print("f'(x) =", fp)
print("Critical points:", critical)
# Check sign on (1, oo)
print("f'(2) =", fp.subs(x, 2)) # 9 > 0 → increasing
```

Together, these three corollaries transform the MVT from an existence statement into a practical toolkit for determining function behavior without plotting a single point.

### first_derivative_test (16.1s)

## First Derivative Test

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function continuous on an open interval $I$ containing a critical point $c$, where $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist. The *First Derivative Test* classifies $c$ as follows:

- If $f'(x) > 0$ for $x < c$ and $f'(x) < 0$ for $x > c$ (in a punctured neighbourhood of $c$), then $f(c)$ is a **local maximum**.
- If $f'(x) < 0$ for $x < c$ and $f'(x) > 0$ for $x > c$, then $f(c)$ is a **local minimum**.
- If $f'$ does not change sign at $c$, then $f(c)$ is **neither** a local extremum.

Geometrically, a sign change from positive to negative indicates the function rises then falls — a peak — while the reverse pattern indicates a valley.

**Worked Example.** Consider $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 2$. Differentiating gives $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$, so the critical points are $x = -1$ and $x = 1$.

Construct a sign chart for $f'$:

| Interval | $(−\infty,\,-1)$ | $(−1,\,1)$ | $(1,\,\infty)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sign of $f'$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |

At $x = -1$: sign changes from $+$ to $-$, so $f(-1) = 4$ is a **local maximum**.  
At $x = 1$: sign changes from $-$ to $+$, so $f(1) = 0$ is a **local minimum**.

**Theorem (First Derivative Test).** *Let $f$ be continuous on $(a,b)$ and differentiable on $(a,b) \setminus \{c\}$, where $c \in (a,b)$ is a critical point. If there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $f'$ has constant sign on $(c-\delta,c)$ and the opposite sign on $(c,c+\delta)$, then $f(c)$ is a strict local extremum.*

*Proof sketch.* By the Mean Value Theorem, on any subinterval of $(c-\delta, c)$ where $f' > 0$, the function is strictly increasing; hence $f(x) < f(c)$ for all $x \in (c-\delta, c)$. The decreasing condition on $(c, c+\delta)$ yields the same inequality on the right. Together these give $f(c) > f(x)$ in a punctured neighbourhood, confirming a local maximum. The minimum case is symmetric. $\blacksquare$

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, factor

x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x + 2
fp = diff(f, x)
critical_pts = solve(fp, x)

for c in critical_pts:
    left  = fp.subs(x, c - 0.01)
    right = fp.subs(x, c + 0.01)
    nature = ("local max" if left > 0 and right < 0
              else "local min" if left < 0 and right > 0
              else "neither")
    print(f"x = {c}: f'  changes sign {float(left):+.3f} → {float(right):+.3f}  → {nature}")
```

### horizontal_asymptote (15.8s)

## Horizontal Asymptote

**Definition.** A function $f(x)$ has a **horizontal asymptote** $y = L$ if

$$\lim_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = L \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$$

where $L \in \mathbb{R}$. Geometrically, the graph of $f$ approaches the horizontal line $y = L$ without necessarily touching it, as the input grows without bound in one or both directions. A function may have at most two distinct horizontal asymptotes, one in each direction.

**Worked Example.** Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{3x^2 - 5}{2x^2 + x + 1}.$$

To find horizontal asymptotes, evaluate the limit as $x \to \pm\infty$. Divide every term by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator, namely $x^2$:

$$\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{3 - \tfrac{5}{x^2}}{2 + \tfrac{1}{x} + \tfrac{1}{x^2}}.$$

As $x \to \pm\infty$, each term of the form $\tfrac{c}{x^k}$ (with $k \geq 1$) tends to zero, giving

$$\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \frac{3 - 0}{2 + 0 + 0} = \frac{3}{2}.$$

Therefore $y = \tfrac{3}{2}$ is a horizontal asymptote in both directions.

**Key Theorem.** Let $f(x) = \tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ be a rational function with $\deg p = m$ and $\deg q = n$. Then:

1. If $m < n$, then $y = 0$ is the unique horizontal asymptote.
2. If $m = n$, then $y = \tfrac{a_m}{b_n}$ is the unique horizontal asymptote, where $a_m$ and $b_n$ are the leading coefficients of $p$ and $q$ respectively.
3. If $m > n$, no horizontal asymptote exists (the function grows without bound).

*Proof sketch.* In case (2), factor $x^n$ from numerator and denominator; all sub-leading terms vanish in the limit, leaving the ratio of leading coefficients. Cases (1) and (3) follow analogously. $\blacksquare$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit, oo, Rational, plot

x = symbols('x')
f = (3*x**2 - 5) / (2*x**2 + x + 1)

L_pos = limit(f, x,  oo)   # horizontal asymptote as x → +∞
L_neg = limit(f, x, -oo)   # horizontal asymptote as x → −∞
print(f"Right asymptote: y = {L_pos}")
print(f"Left  asymptote: y = {L_neg}")

plot(f, (x, -20, 20), ylabel='f(x)', title='Horizontal Asymptote Demo')
```

Running this cell confirms $y = \tfrac{3}{2}$ in both directions and produces a graph illustrating the convergence visually.

### oblique_asymptote (14.6s)

## Oblique Asymptote

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function defined on an unbounded interval. A line $y = mx + b$ with $m \neq 0$ is called an *oblique* (or *slant*) *asymptote* of $f$ as $x \to +\infty$ if

$$\lim_{x \to +\infty} \bigl[f(x) - (mx + b)\bigr] = 0.$$

An analogous definition holds for $x \to -\infty$. The coefficients are recovered by the formulas

$$m = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x}, \qquad b = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \bigl[f(x) - mx\bigr],$$

provided both limits exist and are finite. Note that a horizontal asymptote is the degenerate case $m = 0$; oblique asymptotes require $m \neq 0$ and are therefore impossible for bounded functions such as $\sin x$.

**Worked Example.** Consider $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 3x - 1}{x + 1}$.

Perform polynomial long division:

$$\frac{x^2 + 3x - 1}{x + 1} = x + 2 + \frac{-3}{x+1}.$$

As $x \to \pm\infty$ the remainder term $\dfrac{-3}{x+1} \to 0$, so

$$\lim_{x \to \pm\infty}\bigl[f(x) - (x + 2)\bigr] = 0.$$

Thus $y = x + 2$ is an oblique asymptote in both directions. The vertical asymptote at $x = -1$ is separate and does not affect this analysis.

**Key Theorem.** *A rational function $p(x)/q(x)$ in lowest terms possesses an oblique asymptote if and only if $\deg p = \deg q + 1$. In that case the asymptote is the quotient obtained by dividing $p$ by $q$.*

*Proof sketch.* Write $p/q = (sx + t) + r(x)/q(x)$ where $\deg r < \deg q$. Since $\deg q \geq 1$, we have $r(x)/q(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, confirming $y = sx + t$ is the asymptote. If $\deg p > \deg q + 1$ the quotient is not linear, so no oblique asymptote exists. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, apart, limit, oo, factor

x = symbols('x')
f = (x**2 + 3*x - 1) / (x + 1)

m = limit(f / x, x, oo)
b = limit(f - m*x, x, oo)
print(f"Oblique asymptote: y = {m}x + {b}")   # y = x + 2
```

Running this cell confirms $m = 1$, $b = 2$, consistent with the long-division result. Students are encouraged to modify the numerator degree and observe when `m` becomes zero (horizontal) or when no finite limit exists (no asymptote).

### second_derivative_test (14.2s)

## Second Derivative Test

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function twice differentiable at a critical point $c$, meaning $f'(c) = 0$. The *second derivative test* uses the sign of $f''(c)$ to classify the nature of that critical point:

- If $f''(c) > 0$, then $f$ has a **local minimum** at $c$.
- If $f''(c) < 0$, then $f$ has a **local maximum** at $c$.
- If $f''(c) = 0$, the test is **inconclusive**; higher-order analysis or the first derivative test is required.

The geometric intuition is immediate: $f''(c) > 0$ means the graph is concave up near $c$, so the tangent line lies below the curve and the function bowl-curves upward from a minimum. Conversely, $f''(c) < 0$ means concave down, like an inverted bowl, indicating a maximum.

**Worked Example.** Consider $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5$.

1. **Find critical points.** $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3(x^2 - 2x - 3) = 3(x-3)(x+1)$. Thus $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = 3$ and $x = -1$.

2. **Apply the test.** $f''(x) = 6x - 6$.
   - At $x = -1$: $f''(-1) = -12 < 0$ $\Rightarrow$ local maximum; $f(-1) = 10$.
   - At $x = 3$: $f''(3) = 12 > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ local minimum; $f(3) = -22$.

**Key Theorem.** *(Second Derivative Test)* Suppose $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''$ is continuous near $c$. By Taylor's theorem, for small $h$,

$$f(c + h) = f(c) + \underbrace{f'(c)}_{=\,0} h + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)\,h^2 + O(h^3).$$

Hence $f(c+h) - f(c) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)\,h^2$. Since $h^2 > 0$, the sign of this difference is determined entirely by the sign of $f''(c)$, confirming the classification above.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, latex

x = symbols('x')
f = x**3 - 3*x**2 - 9*x + 5

f_prime  = diff(f, x)
f_double = diff(f_prime, x)

crits = solve(f_prime, x)
for c in crits:
    val = f_double.subs(x, c)
    kind = "local min" if val > 0 else "local max" if val < 0 else "inconclusive"
    print(f"x = {c}: f''(x) = {val}  →  {kind},  f(x) = {f.subs(x, c)}")
```

Running this cell reproduces the worked example exactly, and students can substitute any polynomial to explore the test interactively.

### curve_sketching (15.6s)

## Curve Sketching

**Definition.** *Curve sketching* is the systematic process of determining the qualitative and quantitative features of the graph of a function $f : D \subseteq \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ using calculus, without plotting individual points exhaustively. The standard checklist comprises: domain and range, intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes, intervals of increase/decrease (from $f'$), local extrema (from the First or Second Derivative Test), concavity and inflection points (from $f''$).

**Worked Example.** Sketch $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 - 4}$.

1. **Domain.** $f$ is undefined where $x^2 - 4 = 0$, so $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-2, 2\}$.
2. **Intercepts.** $f(0) = \tfrac{1}{4}$ (y-intercept); $f(x)=0 \Rightarrow x = \pm 1$ (x-intercepts).
3. **Symmetry.** $f(-x) = f(x)$, so the graph is symmetric about the y-axis.
4. **Asymptotes.** Vertical: $x = \pm 2$. Horizontal: $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty} f(x) = 1$, so $y = 1$.
5. **First derivative.**
$$f'(x) = \frac{2x(x^2-4) - 2x(x^2-1)}{(x^2-4)^2} = \frac{-6x}{(x^2-4)^2}.$$
$f'(x) > 0$ for $x < 0$ (excluding $x=-2$) and $f'(x) < 0$ for $x > 0$ (excluding $x=2$). Local maximum at $x = 0$: $f(0) = \tfrac{1}{4}$.
6. **Second derivative and concavity.** Computing $f''$ and analyzing its sign confirms concavity changes at $x = 0$ and the behavior near each vertical asymptote.

**Key Theorem (First Derivative Test).** Let $c$ be a critical point of $f$ where $f'(c) = 0$. If $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $c$, then $f(c)$ is a local maximum; if from negative to positive, a local minimum; if no sign change occurs, $c$ is neither.

*Proof sketch.* By the Mean Value Theorem, the sign of $f'$ on an interval controls whether $f$ is increasing or decreasing there. A sign change at $c$ therefore implies the function reverses monotonicity, producing an extremum. $\square$

```python
print("f'' =", simplify(diff(f, x, 2)))

```
