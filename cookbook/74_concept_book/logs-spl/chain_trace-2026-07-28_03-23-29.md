### natural_logarithm_integral_def (0.0s)

## Natural Logarithm Integral Definition

**Definition.** For any real number $x > 0$, the *natural logarithm* is defined by the definite integral

$$\ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt.$$

This definition is logically prior to the exponential function: rather than treating $\ln$ as the inverse of $e^x$, we construct it directly from the area under the hyperbola $y = 1/t$. When $x > 1$ the integral accumulates positive area; when $0 < x < 1$ the limits are reversed and the value is negative; at $x = 1$ the integral is zero, so $\ln 1 = 0$ follows immediately.

**Worked Example.** Estimate $\ln 2$ using a left-endpoint Riemann sum with $n = 4$ subintervals on $[1, 2]$.

Each subinterval has width $\Delta t = \tfrac{1}{4}$. The left endpoints are $t_0 = 1,\, t_1 = 1.25,\, t_2 = 1.5,\, t_3 = 1.75$. Thus

$$\ln 2 \approx \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{1} + \frac{1}{1.25} + \frac{1}{1.5} + \frac{1}{1.75}\right) = \frac{1}{4}(1 + 0.8 + 0.\overline{6} + 0.571\ldots) \approx 0.760.$$

The exact value is $\ln 2 \approx 0.6931$; the left-endpoint sum overshoots because $1/t$ is decreasing.

**Key Theorem (Properties from the Definition).** Let $a, b > 0$ and $r \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then:

1. $\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$,
2. $\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b$,
3. $\ln(a^r) = r\ln a$,
4. $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}$ for all $x > 0$.

*Proof sketch of (1).* Fix $a$ and define $f(x) = \ln(ax)$. By the chain rule, $f'(x) = 1/x = (\ln x)'$, so $f(x) - \ln x$ is constant. Evaluating at $x = 1$ gives the constant as $\ln a$, hence $\ln(ax) = \ln a + \ln x$. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, integrate, ln, N

t, x = symbols('t x', positive=True)

# Verify the definition agrees with sympy's ln
integral_def = integrate(1/t, (t, 1, x))
print(integral_def)          # ln(x)
print(N(integral_def.subs(x, 2), 10))  # 0.6931471806
```

### exponential_function_def (0.0s)

## Exponential Function Def

**Definition.** Let $b > 0$ with $b \neq 1$. The *exponential function with base $b$* is the function $f : \mathbb{R} \to (0, \infty)$ defined by

$$f(x) = b^x.$$

More precisely, for rational $x = p/q$ we set $b^x = \sqrt[q]{b^p}$, and we extend to all real $x$ by continuity: $b^x = \lim_{r \to x} b^r$ where the limit is taken over rationals $r$. The *natural exponential function* corresponds to the base $b = e \approx 2.71828$, where $e$ is Euler's number, and is written $\exp(x) = e^x$.

**Worked Example.** Evaluate and compare $2^{3/2}$ and $e^1$.

For $2^{3/2}$, write $2^{3/2} = (2^3)^{1/2} = 8^{1/2} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.828$. For $e^1 = e \approx 2.718$. Thus $e^1 < 2^{3/2}$, illustrating that the ordering of exponential values depends on both the base and the exponent.

**Key Theorem** *(Fundamental Properties of Exponentials).* Let $b > 0$, $b \neq 1$, and let $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$. Then:

1. $b^{x+y} = b^x \cdot b^y$
2. $b^{x-y} = b^x / b^y$
3. $(b^x)^y = b^{xy}$
4. $\dfrac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$

*Proof sketch of (4).* Using the limit definition of the derivative,

$$\frac{d}{dx} e^x = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^{x+h} - e^x}{h} = e^x \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^h - 1}{h}.$$

One shows via the Taylor series $e^h = 1 + h + h^2/2! + \cdots$ that the remaining limit equals $1$, giving $\tfrac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$. This self-replicating derivative is the defining characteristic of the natural exponential. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, exp, E, sqrt, simplify, diff

x = symbols('x')

# Verify 2^(3/2) and e^1
val1 = 2**sympy.Rational(3, 2)
val2 = E

print("e       =", E)

# Confirm d/dx e^x = e^x

print("d/dx e^x =", diff(f, x))      # exp(x)
```

Running this cell confirms $2^{3/2} = 2\sqrt{2}$ symbolically and verifies the self-derivative property of $e^x$.

### exponential_decay_model (0.0s)

## Exponential Decay Model

**Definition.** A quantity $N(t)$ undergoes *exponential decay* when its rate of change is proportional to its current value with a negative constant of proportionality. Formally, $N(t)$ satisfies the initial value problem

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N, \quad N(0) = N_0,$$

where $\lambda > 0$ is the *decay constant* (units: time$^{-1}$) and $N_0 > 0$ is the initial amount. Separating variables and integrating yields the closed-form solution

$$N(t) = N_0\, e^{-\lambda t}.$$

The *half-life* $t_{1/2}$ is the time required for $N$ to reduce to half its initial value. Setting $N(t_{1/2}) = N_0/2$ gives $e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} = 1/2$, hence

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}.$$

**Worked Example.** Carbon-14 has a half-life of approximately $5{,}730$ years. Suppose an archaeological sample initially contains $N_0 = 1{,}000$ atoms of ${}^{14}\text{C}$. How many remain after $t = 10{,}000$ years?

*Step 1.* Compute the decay constant:
$$\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{5730} \approx 1.2097 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{yr}^{-1}.$$

*Step 2.* Evaluate the solution:
$$N(10000) = 1000\, e^{-(1.2097\times 10^{-4})(10000)} = 1000\, e^{-1.2097} \approx 298\ \text{atoms}.$$

Roughly 29.8% of the original sample survives after 10,000 years.

**Key Theorem (Uniqueness of Exponential Decay).** Let $N: [0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable. Then $N'(t) = -\lambda N(t)$ for all $t \geq 0$ if and only if $N(t) = N(0)\,e^{-\lambda t}$. The proof follows from the fact that $f(t) = N(t)e^{\lambda t}$ satisfies $f'(t) = 0$, so $f$ is constant; hence $N(t) = f(0)\,e^{-\lambda t} = N(0)\,e^{-\lambda t}$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, exp, ln, solve, N as num

t, lam, N0 = symbols('t lambda N_0', positive=True)
half_life = 5730          # years, Carbon-14
lam_val = ln(2) / half_life
N_t = 1000 * exp(-lam_val * t)

remaining = num(N_t.subs(t, 10000), 4)
print(f"N(10000) = {remaining} atoms")   # ~298.1

t_half = solve(N_t - 500, t)[0]
print(f"Verified half-life: {num(t_half, 6)} years")  # ~5730
```

## Newtons Law Of Cooling

**Definition.** Newton's Law of Cooling states that the rate of change of the temperature of an object is proportional to the difference between the object's temperature and the ambient (surrounding) temperature. If $T(t)$ denotes the temperature of the object at time $t$ and $T_a$ denotes the constant ambient temperature, then

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -k\bigl(T(t) - T_a\bigr), \quad k > 0,$$

where $k$ is a positive constant depending on the physical properties of the object and its environment. The negative sign ensures that the object cools when $T > T_a$ and warms when $T < T_a$.

**Worked Example.** A cup of coffee at $90\,^\circ\text{C}$ is placed in a room held at $20\,^\circ\text{C}$. After $10$ minutes the coffee has cooled to $70\,^\circ\text{C}$. Find $T(t)$ and determine when the coffee reaches $40\,^\circ\text{C}$.

Let $u(t) = T(t) - 20$. Then $\frac{du}{dt} = -k\,u$, a separable ODE with solution $u(t) = u(0)\,e^{-kt}$. Since $u(0) = 70$,

$$T(t) = 20 + 70\,e^{-kt}.$$

Applying $T(10) = 70$ gives $70\,e^{-10k} = 50$, so

$$k = \frac{1}{10}\ln\!\left(\frac{70}{50}\right) = \frac{\ln 1.4}{10} \approx 0.03365\,\text{min}^{-1}.$$

To find when $T = 40$: $70\,e^{-kt} = 20 \Rightarrow t = \frac{\ln(70/20)}{k} \approx \frac{\ln 3.5}{0.03365} \approx 37.6\,\text{min}$.

**Key Theorem.** The unique solution to the initial-value problem $\frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_a)$, $T(0) = T_0$, is

$$\boxed{T(t) = T_a + (T_0 - T_a)\,e^{-kt}.}$$

*Proof sketch.* Separate variables: $\frac{dT}{T - T_a} = -k\,dt$. Integrate both sides to obtain $\ln|T - T_a| = -kt + C$. Exponentiating and applying the initial condition yields the stated formula. Existence and uniqueness follow from the Picard–Lindelöf theorem, since the right-hand side is Lipschitz in $T$. $\blacksquare$

**Lab Cell.**
```python
import sympy as sp

t, k, T0, Ta = sp.symbols('t k T_0 T_a', positive=True)
T = sp.Function('T')

ode = sp.Eq(T(t).diff(t), -k * (T(t) - Ta))
sol = sp.dsolve(ode, T(t), ics={T(0): T0})
print(sol)

# Numerical example: T0=90, Ta=20, k=ln(1.4)/10
T_num = sol.rhs.subs({T0: 90, Ta: 20, k: sp.Rational(1,10)*sp.ln(sp.Rational(7,5))})
t_val = sp.solve(T_num - 40, t)
print("Reaches 40°C at t =", sp.simplify(t_val[0]))
```
