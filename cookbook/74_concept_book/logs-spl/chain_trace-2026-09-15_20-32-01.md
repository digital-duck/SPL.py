### 子 (19.2s)

## 子

**Picture this:** A baby is all wrapped up snugly in blankets, cozy and safe. That's what the Chinese character 子 looked like when people first drew it thousands of years ago—a tiny, swaddled infant. But here's the clever part: over time, this character grew beyond its baby picture. It learned to do something completely different: it became a *label* you add to the end of other words.

**Why does this feel right?** Imagine you know someone is really good at thinking about hard questions. Instead of saying "that person who thinks about hard questions" every time, you need a quick way to name them. In Chinese, you take the word for thinking, add 子 at the end, and *poof*—you've just created a word that means "a thinking person" or "a philosopher." It's like 子 is a special tag that says, "This isn't just an idea—it's a person or thing connected to this idea."

**How it works:** The pattern is simple:

$$\text{(idea or concept)} + 子 = \text{(person who knows or does that idea)}$$

**Now you try:** Think of something you're learning—maybe math, or drawing, or soccer. If we put 子 at the end to mean "someone who knows about this," what would that person look like? What would they do?

### 女 (11.6s)

## 女

Picture a grandmother teaching her granddaughter to sit properly. In ancient China, when artists wanted to draw a woman, they showed this moment—a figure kneeling or bending slightly, the way women were often positioned in daily life. That simple, humble shape became 女, the symbol for woman.

When you look at 女, you're seeing a stick figure at rest—two strokes forming a bent body, with a small mark for the head. It's not trying to be fancy or complicated; it just captures the essence of a person in that kneeling pose. The shape itself is the meaning.

Here's where it gets interesting: 女 doesn't stay alone for long. Ancient Chinese writers discovered they could attach other pictures to it and create new ideas. When 女 combines with 子 (child), you get 好—literally "woman and child together," which means goodness. The 女 becomes a building block:

$$\text{woman} + \text{child} \xrightarrow{\text{combine}} \text{goodness}$$

This is how Chinese characters grow. A simple, memorable picture of a kneeling woman became the foundation for dozens of words about family, care, and relationships.

**Now you try:** Hunt through the character list for 女 hiding inside other words. Can you find three characters that contain this woman-figure? What do you think those characters mean, based on what you find?

### 宀 (20.5s)

## 宀

**Imagine your favorite cozy place** — maybe a treehouse, a tent, or even a blanket fort. The best part? There's a roof over your head that makes you feel safe and protected. In Chinese characters, there's a special shape that means exactly that: a little roof.

The radical 宀 looks like an upside-down U and sits on top of other character pieces. Whenever you see this roof shape, it's saying: "Something important and cozy is happening inside — a home, a family, a place where things settle down."

Think of it like a lid or a blanket. Whatever sits underneath the 宀 roof becomes "housed" or "sheltered" — pieces that belong together, safe and sound.

**Now you try:** Characters are built by combining pieces. A person under a roof becomes a home:

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = \text{家 (home)}$$

When you see this roof in other characters, remember: something is being sheltered inside.

### 好 (13.6s)

## 好

**Picture this:** Imagine ancient people drawing what "good" looks like. They didn't use abstract words—they drew pictures. One of the most beautiful ideas they captured was this: what does wellbeing look like? A mother and child together.

**The intuition:** In Chinese characters, 好 (hǎo) means "good." But look at what's inside: a woman (女) and a child (子) sitting together. Why would our ancestors combine these two pictures to mean "good"? Because for thousands of years, people understood something simple and true—when a child is safe, cared for, and close to someone who loves them, that's *good*. That's wellbeing. That's what makes life feel right.

This isn't just a word. It's a story about what matters.

**The pattern:**
$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{好 (good)}$$

**Now you try:** Think about other things in your life that feel "good." Do any of them involve people caring for each other? What pictures would *you* draw to show what "good" means?

### 字 (14.8s)

## 字

**Picture this.** Imagine you want to draw the idea of a child. Simple: draw a tiny person. Now imagine that child is sitting safely inside a house. Draw a roof above them. What do you have? A picture of home, of family, of a child being raised and taken care of.

The ancient Chinese looked at this picture—a child under a roof—and said: *This is how new ideas are born.* When we take old pictures and combine them, we create new ones. Just like a child grows up with their family, new characters grow up from older pieces.

So the Chinese gave this special picture a name: 字 (zì). It means "character" or "a child under a roof." But it also means something deeper—it's the word for *all* Chinese characters, because every character is born the same way: by combining two or more picture-pieces together.

**How it's built:**
$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字$$

**Now you try.** Look at 字 and try to see the roof and the child tucked underneath. Can you spot them inside?

### vocabulary_compounds (28.6s)

## Vocabulary Compounds

Picture a child building a tower with LEGO bricks. They take one brick, then add another on top—suddenly they have something new. Words work the same way, but instead of plastic bricks, we stack *characters* on top of each other.

You already know how characters are built from smaller pieces (like radicals). But here's the really cool part: *words are built from characters using the exact same idea.* It's LEGO all the way down.

Look at the forest 森林. One tree character doesn't feel like a forest, does it? But when you put *two* tree characters together—木 + 木—suddenly your brain knows: that's a whole forest! The word makes sense because the pieces tell the story.

It works for other words too:

$$\underbrace{好}_{\text{good}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} = 好人 \text{ (good person)}$$

$$\underbrace{安}_{\text{peace}} + \underbrace{全}_{\text{whole}} = 安全 \text{ (safety)}$$

Notice something? When characters link up, their meanings link up too. The word *means* what the pieces mean, combined. That's not magic—that's just good building.

**Now you try:** Think of the word 明天 (tomorrow). Can you guess what 明 and 天 might mean separately? Does it help you remember what the word means?
