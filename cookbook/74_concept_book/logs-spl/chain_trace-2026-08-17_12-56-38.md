## Triangle

A **triangle** is a polygon with exactly three vertices, three edges, and three interior angles. The angles of any triangle always sum to $180°$, a fact that follows from the parallel postulate of Euclidean geometry and underpins nearly every calculation involving triangles.

Triangles are classified by their sides — equilateral (all sides equal), isosceles (two sides equal), or scalene (no sides equal) — and by their angles — acute (all angles less than $90°$), right (one angle exactly $90°$), or obtuse (one angle greater than $90°$). The right triangle is especially important because it connects the three sides through the **Pythagorean theorem**: if $a$ and $b$ are the legs and $c$ is the hypotenuse, then

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2.$$

**Worked example.** A ramp rises $1.2\ \text{m}$ vertically over a horizontal run of $3.5\ \text{m}$. What is the length of the ramp surface?

Model the situation as a right triangle: the vertical rise $a = 1.2$, the horizontal run $b = 3.5$, and the ramp itself is the hypotenuse $c$.

$$c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{(1.2)^2 + (3.5)^2} = \sqrt{1.44 + 12.25} = \sqrt{13.69} \approx 3.70\ \text{m}.$$

The ramp surface is about $3.70\ \text{m}$ long.

**Problem-solving application.** Triangles arise whenever you need to decompose a geometric situation into measurable relationships. For non-right triangles, two additional tools extend coverage to any triangle: the **Law of Sines**,

$$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C},$$

and the **Law of Cosines**,

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C.$$

Use the Law of Sines when you know two angles and one side (AAS or ASA). Use the Law of Cosines when you know two sides and the included angle (SAS) or all three sides (SSS). For example, if a surveyor measures two sides of a triangular plot as $80\ \text{m}$ and $60\ \text{m}$ with an included angle of $110°$, the third side is

$$c = \sqrt{80^2 + 60^2 - 2(80)(60)\cos 110°} \approx \sqrt{6400 + 3600 + 3284} \approx 116\ \text{m}.$$

Triangles are the atomic unit of 2-D geometry: any polygon can be triangulated, making these three tools the foundation for area calculation, structural analysis, navigation, and computer graphics.

## Triple

A **triple** is an ordered, fixed-length sequence of exactly three values. In Python, a triple is simply a tuple with three elements: `(a, b, c)`. Because tuples are immutable, a triple is a natural choice when three related values belong together and should not be altered — a 3-D coordinate, an RGB color, or a database record key. The three positions carry meaning by convention: swapping them produces a different object even if the values are identical.

Formally, a triple belongs to the Cartesian product $A \times B \times C$, meaning the first element comes from set $A$, the second from $B$, and the third from $C$. When all three sets are the same (say, real numbers), you write $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Worked example.** Suppose you are building a knowledge graph where every fact is stored as a *(subject, predicate, object)* triple:

```python
facts = [
    ("Alice", "knows", "Bob"),
    ("Bob",   "works_at", "CERN"),
    ("Alice", "works_at", "MIT"),
]

def query(facts, predicate):
    return [(s, o) for s, p, o in facts if p == predicate]

print(query(facts, "works_at"))
# [('Bob', 'CERN'), ('Alice', 'MIT')]
```

Unpacking `s, p, o` in the loop is idiomatic Python: it names each position without an index, making the intent self-documenting.

**Problem-solving application.** Many geometric algorithms pass points as triples. Consider computing the centroid of a triangle given its three vertices:

```python
def centroid(v1, v2, v3):
    x = (v1[0] + v2[0] + v3[0]) / 3
    y = (v1[1] + v2[1] + v3[1]) / 3
    z = (v1[2] + v2[2] + v3[2]) / 3
    return (x, y, z)

A = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
B = (3.0, 0.0, 0.0)
C = (0.0, 4.0, 0.0)
print(centroid(A, B, C))   # (1.0, 1.333..., 0.0)
```

The centroid formula is $G = \left(\frac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3},\; \frac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3},\; \frac{z_1+z_2+z_3}{3}\right)$, and returning it as a triple keeps the result in the same shape as the inputs — a design discipline that makes function composition easier.

When choosing between a triple and a small class, prefer a triple for read-only, positional data (coordinates, colors, graph edges); prefer a class when the data needs methods or when positional semantics would confuse future readers.

## Clustering Coefficient

In a social network, your friends are often friends with each other — this cliquishness is what the clustering coefficient measures. Formally, the **local clustering coefficient** of a node $v$ is the fraction of possible edges among $v$'s neighbors that actually exist. If $v$ has degree $k$, then there are $\binom{k}{2} = \frac{k(k-1)}{2}$ possible edges among its neighbors. If $e_v$ of those edges are present, the clustering coefficient is:

$$C(v) = \frac{e_v}{\binom{k}{2}} = \frac{2\,e_v}{k(k-1)}$$

A value of $C(v) = 1$ means every neighbor of $v$ is connected to every other neighbor — a perfect clique. A value of $0$ means none of $v$'s neighbors are connected to each other. The **global clustering coefficient** of a graph averages $C(v)$ over all nodes with degree $\geq 2$.

**Worked example.** Consider node $A$ connected to $B$, $C$, and $D$. Among those three neighbors, suppose the edges $B$–$C$ and $B$–$D$ exist, but $C$–$D$ does not. Then $k = 3$, $e_A = 2$, and:

$$C(A) = \frac{2 \times 2}{3 \times 2} = \frac{4}{6} \approx 0.667$$

Two-thirds of the possible neighbor connections are realized.

**Problem-solving application.** Clustering coefficients are a key diagnostic in network analysis — high values signal tightly-knit communities, while low values suggest a hub-and-spoke structure. Using NetworkX in Python:

```python
import networkx as nx

G = nx.karate_club_graph()          # classic social network benchmark
cc = nx.clustering(G)               # local C(v) for every node
avg_cc = nx.average_clustering(G)   # global average

high_cc = [v for v, c in cc.items() if c > 0.5]
print(f"Average clustering: {avg_cc:.3f}")
print(f"Nodes with C > 0.5: {high_cc}")
```

Nodes in `high_cc` sit inside dense subcommunities — strong candidates for community cores. In fraud detection, an unusually low clustering coefficient for a node that has many connections can flag a broker sitting between otherwise-disconnected groups, a structural signature of money-laundering intermediaries. When comparing two networks, a random graph of the same size and density has a clustering coefficient near $p$ (the edge probability), so a real-world coefficient significantly above $p$ is evidence of genuine social structure rather than random wiring.

## Epidemic Dissemination

**Epidemic dissemination** is a strategy for spreading information (or data) across a distributed network by having each node periodically contact a small number of random peers and exchange state with them. The name comes from the analogy to how a biological disease spreads: each infected individual exposes a few others, who in turn expose more, creating exponential propagation without any central coordinator. In computer systems, epidemic dissemination — also called **gossip protocols** — is used to replicate database updates, propagate cluster membership information, and synchronize distributed logs.

The core mechanics are simple. In each round, every node that holds a piece of information (a "rumor") selects $k$ random neighbors and sends them the rumor. A node that receives the rumor becomes a spreader in the next round. If $N$ is the total number of nodes and $I_t$ is the number of informed nodes after round $t$, the spread follows roughly:

$$I_{t+1} \approx I_t + k \cdot I_t \cdot \frac{N - I_t}{N}$$

This is a discrete logistic growth equation. Early on, when $I_t \ll N$, growth is nearly exponential; it decelerates as the susceptible pool shrinks. Full coverage is probabilistic rather than guaranteed — some nodes may never be reached — but the probability of missing any node drops exponentially with the number of rounds.

**Worked example.** Suppose a 1,000-node cluster needs to propagate a configuration update. Each node gossips to $k = 3$ peers per round. Starting with 1 informed node, after round 1 we expect roughly $1 + 3(1)(999/1000) \approx 4$ informed nodes. After 10 rounds the model predicts coverage exceeding 99%. In practice, Apache Cassandra and Consul use exactly this pattern to disseminate ring membership and health data.

**Applying the concept.** When designing a gossip-based system, three parameters govern behavior: fan-out $k$ (higher means faster spread but more bandwidth), round interval (shorter rounds reduce latency but increase load), and message retention (nodes must remember what they have already forwarded to avoid infinite re-propagation). A practical tuning exercise: set a coverage target — say, 99.9% of $N = 10{,}000$ nodes within 20 rounds — then solve for the minimum $k$ that satisfies it under the logistic model. Because gossip is inherently probabilistic, production systems pair it with an anti-entropy mechanism (a periodic full Merkle-tree comparison) to catch the rare node that gossip missed.
