### 子 (8.3s)

## 子

You already know 子 as "child" — it started as a simple drawing of a swaddled baby, with two little arms poking out and the legs wrapped up snug. That picture is worth holding onto, because it explains why 子 shows up in so many family-related characters. But 子 has a second life you're ready to explore now: it's one of the busiest "helper" pieces in Chinese, turning ordinary words into nouns the way "-er" or "-let" does in English.

Think about English suffixes like "-let" in *booklet* or *piglet* — they don't mean much on their own, but they signal "this is a small, distinct thing." 子 works the same way as a noun suffix. Attached to the end of a word, it often means "this is a thing, not an action or a description."

$$
\underbrace{椅}_{\text{sit-support}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{noun suffix}} = \text{椅子 (chair)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{帽}_{\text{head-cover}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{noun suffix}} = \text{帽子 (hat)}
$$

$$
\underbrace{房}_{\text{room}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{noun suffix}} = \text{房子 (house)}
$$

Notice the pattern: 椅 alone can feel abstract, almost like a verb-ish idea ("to sit against"), but adding 子 locks it down as a solid, nameable object. That's the rule to remember — 子 often turns a "thing-quality" into a "thing-itself."

**Practice:** 帽 by itself relates to covering the head. Using the pattern above, write the equation showing how adding 子 gives you the word for "hat," and explain in one sentence why 子 is needed there rather than left off.

### 女 (7.0s)

## 女

You already know 女 as a picture of a kneeling, bowing figure — one of the earliest characters ever drawn, representing "woman." But its real power in Chinese isn't as a standalone word so much as a building block. Once you start noticing it, you'll see 女 sitting inside dozens of other characters, quietly doing the work of pointing toward family, relationships, and people connected to one another.

Look at how it combines. Add 子 (child) next to 女, and you get 好 — "good." The traditional story is that a woman with a child represented something complete and favorable, though scholars debate the exact original logic. What matters for you is the pattern: 女 rarely appears alone in compound characters — it appears as a *component*, contributing meaning rather than sound.

$$
\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = \underbrace{好}_{good}
$$

This is called a semantic radical — a piece of a character that hints at meaning, not pronunciation. Once you know 女 as a radical, you can guess that any character containing it probably relates to women, family roles, or relationships, even before you know how to say it. That's a real reading strategy, not just trivia.

**Try it yourself:** The character 姐 (older sister) is built from 女 plus 且. Based on what you just learned about how 女 works as a radical, what do you think 女 is contributing to the meaning of 姐 — and what job might 且 be doing instead?

### 宀 (16.2s)

## 宀

Picture a simple sloped roof drawn in just a few strokes — a peak at the top, sides sweeping down. That's 宀, the "roof radical." On its own it isn't a full character, but as a component, it tells you something important is going on underneath: someone is sheltered, something is stored, or a space has become a proper dwelling.

Once you start looking for 宀, you'll spot it constantly at the top of characters about home and household life. Think of it as a signal — "this character is about a roof over something." What sits beneath the roof usually gives the specific meaning:

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = \text{安 (ān, peaceful, safe)}
$$

A woman settled safely under a roof — peace and security. Here's another example using the same roof radical:

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{字 (zì, word/character)}
$$

A child under a roof — this is where a child is raised, taught, and given a name. Over time, "字" grew to mean the written characters used to name and record things, and eventually just "word" or "character" itself.

**The pattern**: when you see 宀 sitting on top of a character, ask yourself — what is being housed, protected, or settled? The piece underneath usually answers that question directly, whether it's a person, an object, or a family relationship.

**Practice problem**: The character 安 (ān) means "peaceful, safe," built from 宀 (roof) + 女 (woman). Now try 宁 (níng, an old form meaning "peaceful, calm" — different from today's simplified 宁 for "rather"), which is just 宀 (roof) sitting over a small courtyard-like space, without a second component underneath. Using what you learned about 宀 as a signal, write a sentence explaining why a roof by itself — with a settled, enclosed space beneath it — might already suggest calm or stillness.

### 好 (13.2s)

## 好

You already know 好 (hǎo) means "good" — the kind of good you say when food tastes right, a plan works out, or a friend asks how you're doing. But look closely at the character, and it turns out to be one of the oldest surviving snapshots of what ancient Chinese scribes actually believed "good" looked like.

好 is built from two pieces standing side by side: 女 (woman) and 子 (child).

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \underbrace{好}_{\text{good}}$$

Bronze inscriptions and oracle bone carvings from over three thousand years ago already show this same pairing. Scholars generally read it as a mother with her child — a picture of family, continuity, and care, which early Chinese culture treated as the clearest image of something being *right* or *whole*. The character doesn't argue for this meaning with logic; it just shows you the scene and lets the feeling of "good" arise from it.

This is worth noticing as a pattern: many Chinese characters aren't built from abstract symbols, but from tiny frozen scenes — a person leaning against a tree, a hand holding a brush, a woman with a child. When you meet a new character, ask what small picture its pieces might be drawing, not just what sound or meaning to memorize.

**Practice:** The character 如 (rú, meaning "as" or "like," as in "as expected") is also built from 女 (woman) placed beside 口 (mouth). Sketch out the composition equation for 如 the way we did for 好, and in one sentence, explain what "scene" the character might be picturing.

### 字 (12.9s)

## 字

You already know 字 (zì) means "character" — but look closer and it's telling you something about itself. Peel back the roof, and you find a child underneath.

Trace the pieces: the top is 宀, a roof — the same shape you'd see over 家 (home) or 安 (peace). Underneath sits 子, meaning "child." Put them together and you get a picture of a child sheltered under a roof — being raised, growing up.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字\ (\text{character, to be born/raised})$$

Why would "character" mean "a child under a roof"? Because that's exactly how Chinese characters work. A small set of simple pictures — sun, moon, tree, person — get combined and recombined, the way children grow up inside families, to produce new characters. Each new character is "born" from older ones already living in the system. 字 isn't just naming the concept; it's demonstrating it. This is also why a whole method for studying how characters connect to each other is called ZiNets (字网) — networks of 字, mapped out like a family tree.

Here's the pattern to carry forward: when you meet an unfamiliar character, ask what's sheltered inside it, and what older pieces it was built from. Characters rarely appear out of nowhere — most were assembled, like 字 itself.

**Practice:** The character 好 (hǎo, "good") is also built using 子 (child), this time paired with 女 (woman) standing side by side. Write the composition equation for 好 the way we did for 字 above, then in one sentence, guess why "woman + child" might have come to mean "good."

### vocabulary_compounds (13.4s)

## Vocabulary Compounds

You already know that a character like 森 is built from smaller bricks — three 木 stacked into "forest." Here's the surprising part: that same trick works one level up. Once you have finished characters, you can snap *them* together to build words. Characters are LEGO bricks; words are LEGO built from LEGO.

Take 森林. Both pieces already mean "trees/forest" on their own — 森 is "dense forest," 林 is "woods." Put them together and you get a word that means "forest," reinforcing the same idea twice:

$$\underbrace{森}_{\text{dense forest}} + \underbrace{林}_{\text{woods}} = 森林 \text{ (forest)}$$

Not every compound repeats meaning, though. Some combine two *different* ideas into something new. 明天 fuses 明 ("bright") with 天 ("day/sky") to mean "tomorrow" — literally "the bright day," the one still ahead:

$$\underbrace{明}_{\text{bright}} + \underbrace{天}_{\text{day}} = 明天 \text{ (tomorrow)}$$

That's the whole rule, and it doesn't change no matter what word you meet next: **combine known pieces, and the meaning either repeats for emphasis or blends into something new.** Characters build from strokes and radicals; words build from characters. It's the same graph, just zoomed out one notch.

Once you notice this, you start reading unfamiliar words like puzzles instead of memorizing them cold. Try it yourself: 好 means "good," 人 means "person" — what do you expect 好人 to mean, and is it a repeating-meaning word or a blended one?

**Practice:** 火 means "fire" and 车 means "vehicle." Guess what 火车 means, and explain your reasoning the way you would for 明天.
