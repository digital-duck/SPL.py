### 木 (5.6s)

## 木

Picture a small tree standing alone in a field. You can see its trunk going straight up, and its branches spreading out like arms reaching for the sky. That's exactly what 木 looks like — a simple drawing of a tree, with the trunk down the middle and branches on each side.

Here's something fun about 木: it doesn't just mean "tree" — it also helps build other characters, sometimes for its *meaning* and sometimes for its *sound*.

When 木 shows up in 林 (a forest — literally two trees together!) or 休 (to rest, like someone leaning against a tree), it's contributing its meaning — trees, wood, things that grow.

But in 沐 (to wash or bathe), 木 isn't about trees at all — it's borrowed just for its sound, "mu."

So think of 木 like a brick with two jobs: sometimes it builds the *idea* of a word, and sometimes it just lends its *sound*.

**Now you try:** Look at 林 and 休 — can you spot the tree hiding inside each one, and guess why it's there?

### 林 (6.7s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing alone in a field. Now imagine a second tree standing right next to it. Together, they start to feel like something new — not just "two trees," but the beginning of a *place*: a woods.

That's the idea behind 林. It's built from 木 (tree), written twice, side by side. Chinese characters do this often: take a simple picture, and repeat it to mean "more of it" or "a lot of it." One tree is just a tree. Two trees together start to feel like a little forest.

This is the simplest building rule in the whole writing system — like snapping two identical LEGO blocks together to make a bigger shape. No new pieces needed, just doubling what you already have.

Later you'll meet an even bigger woods, made of *three* trees stacked together. But for now, just two side by side is enough to plant the idea.

**Now you try:** Draw one tree. Draw it again right beside itself. Does it start to feel like more than just two trees?

### 森 (5.9s)

## 森

Picture a single tree, standing alone: 木. Now picture two trees close together, growing side by side: 林, a grove. Now picture a whole crowd of trees, so many you can't count them from where you're standing: 森, a forest.

Notice what just happened. One tree became two trees, and two trees became three trees, drawn one on top and two below, like trees on a hillside. Each time a tree gets added, the word doesn't just mean "more trees" — it means something new. One tree is just a tree. Two trees are a small cluster. Three trees are enough to feel lost in, enough to have shadows and paths and animals hiding inside.

That's the trick: adding bricks doesn't just add quantity, it changes the *feeling*. Chinese characters often grow this way — piece by piece, until the meaning grows too.

**Now you try**: Draw one tree, then two, then three. At which drawing does it start to feel like a forest to you?
