## Physics

Physics is the science of describing how energy, matter, space, and time interact — the search for the smallest set of rules from which every observed phenomenon, from a falling apple to the glow of a star, can be derived. Unlike sciences that catalog what happens, physics asks *why*, and insists that the answer be expressed as a mathematical relationship precise enough to predict new observations.

**Worked example.** Consider a ball dropped from a height $h$ near Earth's surface. Newton's second law, $F = ma$, combined with the gravitational force $F = mg$, gives a constant acceleration $a = g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Integrating acceleration twice with respect to time yields the position:

$$
y(t) = h - \frac{1}{2}gt^2
$$

Setting $y(t) = 0$ and solving for $t$ gives the fall time $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$. This is not a curve-fit to data — it is a consequence of a general law ($F=ma$) applied to a specific force ($mg$), and the same derivation, with different forces substituted, predicts planetary orbits or the trajectory of a thrown ball.

**Why the mathematics is unavoidable.** Physics could describe motion in prose — "the ball speeds up as it falls" — but such a description cannot be used to predict *when* the ball lands or *how fast* it will be moving. Differential equations like $F=ma$ encode a relationship between a quantity and its rate of change; solving them (via calculus) converts a qualitative law into a quantitative, testable prediction. This is why physics leans on calculus, linear algebra, and differential equations rather than treating them as optional decoration.

**Problem-solving application.** A satellite in circular orbit provides a second layer: setting gravitational force equal to the centripetal force required for circular motion,

$$
\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}
$$

lets you solve for orbital speed, $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$, without ever needing to know the satellite's mass $m$ — it cancels. This illustrates a recurring problem-solving strategy in physics: write down every law that applies (here, gravitation and circular kinematics), set the relevant quantities equal, and let the algebra reveal which variables matter and which are irrelevant to the outcome you're solving for.

## Classical Physics

Classical physics is the body of theory built from roughly the work of Galileo and Newton in the 17th century through Maxwell and the thermodynamicists of the 19th century. It describes how objects move, how forces act on them, and how energy transforms — for everyday objects, everyday speeds, and everyday gravitational fields. The label "classical" is retrospective: physicists started using it only after relativity and quantum mechanics arrived around 1900 and exposed the boundaries where these older theories stop working. Within its domain — macroscopic objects moving much slower than the speed of light, in gravitational fields too weak to warp spacetime noticeably — classical physics remains exactly as accurate and useful as it ever was. It is not "wrong"; it is a limiting case of more general theories, valid whenever relativistic and quantum corrections are negligibly small.

The core of classical mechanics is Newton's second law, relating net force $\vec{F}$, mass $m$, and acceleration $\vec{a}$:

$$\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$$

Consider a 1200 kg car accelerating from rest to 20 m/s in 8 seconds on a level road. The required average net force is

$$F = ma = m\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = 1200 \times \frac{20}{8} = 3000\ \text{N}$$

This single equation, paired with the definitions of momentum ($p = mv$) and kinetic energy ($KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$), lets you predict trajectories, design bridges, and launch satellites into low Earth orbit — all without ever needing relativistic corrections, since orbital speeds there are still under 0.003% of light speed.

Problem-solving with classical physics means recognizing which conservation law simplifies a given scenario. For a 0.5 kg ball dropped from a 20 m tower, instead of tracking forces moment to moment, conservation of energy gives the impact speed directly:

$$mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \implies v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(20)} \approx 19.8\ \text{m/s}$$

Classical electromagnetism (Maxwell's equations) and thermodynamics extend this same deterministic, continuous framework to fields and heat, together forming a complete, self-consistent description of the macroscopic, low-speed, weak-gravity world — the world of engineering, everyday mechanics, and most laboratory measurement.
