## Olfactory Epithelium

The olfactory epithelium is a specialized sheet of tissue, roughly 2–4 cm² in humans, located high in the nasal cavity along the superior concha and adjacent septum. It contains three principal cell types: olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs), which detect odorant molecules; supporting (sustentacular) cells, which provide structural and metabolic support and help regulate the chemical environment around the neurons; and basal cells, stem-like progenitors that continuously regenerate OSNs throughout life — one of the few examples of ongoing neurogenesis in adult mammals. Each OSN extends cilia into a layer of mucus at the epithelial surface; odorant molecules dissolve in this mucus and bind receptor proteins on the cilia, triggering a signal that travels along the neuron's axon, through the cribriform plate of the skull, to the olfactory bulb.

A useful worked example is tracing a single odorant molecule's path from air to brain: an airborne molecule enters the nasal cavity during inhalation, diffuses into the mucus layer, and binds an olfactory receptor on a cilium. This binding activates a G-protein-coupled signaling cascade inside the OSN, opening ion channels and depolarizing the cell. If depolarization crosses threshold, the neuron fires an action potential that travels down its axon to synapse with mitral cells in the olfactory bulb — the only sensory modality that bypasses the thalamus before reaching cortex. Because each OSN expresses only one type of olfactory receptor gene (out of several hundred in humans), axons expressing the same receptor converge onto the same glomerulus in the bulb, so a given odorant activates a specific combinatorial pattern of glomeruli rather than a single dedicated pathway.

This wiring principle is also the key to problem-solving with the system: if you're asked why humans with roughly 400 functional olfactory receptor genes can distinguish over a trillion odorant combinations, the answer lies in combinatorial coding, not receptor count. Each receptor responds to multiple odorants, and each odorant activates multiple receptors, so the perceived scent corresponds to a unique activation pattern across the epithelium — analogous to how a small alphabet can spell an enormous number of words. Basal cell turnover further explains why anosmia (loss of smell) after epithelial injury is often temporary: the epithelium can regenerate functional neurons and reestablish connections to the bulb over weeks.

## Olfactory Sensory Neuron

The olfactory sensory neuron (OSN) is the primary sensory cell of the sense of smell, located in a specialized patch of tissue in the nasal cavity called the olfactory epithelium. Each OSN is a bipolar neuron: one end sends a single dendrite toward the nasal cavity, ending in a knob covered with several cilia that project into the mucus layer lining the nose; the other end sends a single, unbranched axon through the skull directly into the olfactory bulb of the brain. The cilia are the actual site of odor detection — embedded in their membrane are olfactory receptor (OR) proteins, which are G-protein-coupled receptors. A defining rule of the olfactory system, established through the Nobel Prize–winning work of Linda Buck and Richard Axel, is that each mature OSN expresses only one type of OR gene out of roughly 400 functional variants in humans (and far more in rodents). This "one neuron, one receptor" principle means the several million OSNs in the epithelium are functionally divided into hundreds of distinct populations, each tuned to bind a different, often overlapping, set of odorant molecules.

When an odorant molecule binds its matching receptor, it triggers a signaling cascade — activating the G-protein $G_{olf}$, which raises cyclic AMP and opens ion channels — that depolarizes the neuron and, if threshold is reached, fires an action potential down the axon. Because a given odor molecule typically activates several receptor types to different degrees, the brain identifies smells not from a single neuron's output but from the *combinatorial pattern* of which OSN populations fire and how strongly — the same logic used for color vision, where three cone types combine to encode millions of hues.

A second distinguishing feature of OSNs is their turnover: unlike most neurons in the adult brain, OSNs are continuously replaced throughout life, typically every few weeks to months. Basal cells sitting at the base of the epithelium act as a resident stem cell population, dividing to produce new OSNs that extend fresh axons to the olfactory bulb and reestablish precise wiring. This regenerative capacity — rare among neurons, which are usually irreplaceable once lost — makes the olfactory epithelium a key model system for studying neurogenesis, axon guidance, and strategies for neural repair after injury or disease.

## Olfactory Dysfunction

Olfactory dysfunction refers to an impaired sense of smell, ranging from partial reduction (hyposmia) to complete loss (anosmia), or to qualitative distortion in which familiar odors are perceived as unpleasant or unrecognizable (parosmia). Smell perception depends on a functioning relay: odorant molecules bind receptors on olfactory sensory neurons embedded in the olfactory epithelium, these neurons fire signals along the olfactory nerve to the olfactory bulb, and the brain interprets the pattern as a specific scent. Damage at any point in this chain — the epithelium's supporting cells, the sensory neurons themselves, or their capacity to regenerate — can degrade or corrupt the signal. Common causes include normal aging, head trauma that severs olfactory nerve fibers, neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's and Alzheimer's (where smell loss often precedes motor or cognitive symptoms by years), and viral infections like Covid-19, which attack the supporting sustentacular cells rather than the neurons directly, temporarily disabling the epithelium's ability to process odors.

Consider a patient who develops smell loss after a Covid-19 infection. Weeks later, coffee smells like burning rubber and onions smell rotten even when fresh — a hallmark of parosmia. This pattern is diagnostically informative: because sustentacular (supporting) cells, not sensory neurons, were the primary casualties, the neurons survive but reconnect to the olfactory bulb imperfectly during regeneration, producing miswired, distorted signals rather than silence. This distinguishes infection-related anosmia, which is often reversible over months as epithelial cells regenerate, from neurodegenerative anosmia, where progressive neuronal death makes recovery unlikely.

For clinical problem-solving, this distinction guides both diagnosis and prognosis. A clinician evaluating sudden smell loss should ask: was onset abrupt (suggesting infection or trauma) or gradual (suggesting neurodegeneration)? Is the loss total or distorted (parosmia points toward misdirected neural regeneration rather than simple sensory blockage)? Standardized tests such as the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) quantify severity and track recovery over time, while smell training — repeated, deliberate exposure to a fixed set of odors — leverages the olfactory system's unusual regenerative capacity (sensory neurons are among the few neurons in the body that regenerate throughout life) to accelerate and improve the rewiring process during recovery.
