## Central Dogma

The central dogma of molecular biology describes the directional flow of genetic information in living cells: DNA is transcribed into messenger RNA (mRNA), and mRNA is translated into protein. Formally, information flows $\text{DNA} \rightarrow \text{RNA} \rightarrow \text{Protein}$, with each arrow representing a distinct biochemical process governed by its own enzymes and rules. Transcription copies a gene's DNA sequence into a complementary mRNA strand using RNA polymerase; translation then reads that mRNA in triplet units called codons, using the ribosome and transfer RNA (tRNA) to assemble a specific sequence of amino acids into a protein. Because the genetic code maps 64 possible codons to only 20 amino acids (plus stop signals), the code is redundant but essentially unambiguous in the forward direction — a given codon almost always specifies the same amino acid.

**Worked example.** Consider a short DNA coding-strand segment: `ATG-GGC-TTT-TAA`. Transcription produces the mRNA `AUG-GGC-UUU-UAA` (thymine is replaced by uracil). Translation then reads each codon: AUG codes for methionine (also the start signal), GGC codes for glycine, UUU codes for phenylalanine, and UAA is a stop codon terminating translation. The resulting protein fragment is Met–Gly–Phe. This illustrates why a single nucleotide change in DNA — a mutation — can alter, delete, or leave unchanged the resulting amino acid, depending on where in the codon it occurs and how redundant that codon's third position is.

**Problem-solving application.** The dogma explains a foundational puzzle in biology: every cell in a multicellular organism carries the same DNA, yet a liver cell and a neuron look and behave completely differently. The resolution lies not in different genes but in differential gene expression — which genes are transcribed into mRNA in a given cell. Regulatory proteins (transcription factors) bind specific DNA sequences near a gene and control whether RNA polymerase transcribes it, effectively turning genes on or off. Cell identity is therefore not encoded by unique DNA content but by a selective, cell-type-specific transcription pattern. This framework lets scientists predict, for instance, that comparing mRNA levels (via RNA sequencing) across tissue types reveals which genes distinguish one cell type from another — a technique now central to diagnosing cancers and identifying drug targets by their tissue-specific gene expression signatures.
