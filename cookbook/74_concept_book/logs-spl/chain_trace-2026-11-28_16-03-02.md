### biomedical_corpora (16.9s)

## Biomedical Text Corpora

You already know that a medical AI model learns by reading huge amounts of text, much as a student learns from a library. That collection of text is called a **corpus** (plural: *corpora*). What a model reads shapes what it knows and how it writes.

**The main biomedical corpora**

| Corpus | What it contains | Typical size |
|---|---|---|
| PubMed abstracts | Short summaries of research papers, often 200–300 words each | More than 35 million citations |
| PubMed Central (PMC) | Complete, free-to-read research papers | Millions of full-text articles |
| Clinical guidelines | Official advice on diagnosing and treating conditions, from groups such as the WHO | Thousands of documents |
| Textbooks | Organized explanations of anatomy, physiology, pharmacology | Hundreds of books |

Each source teaches something different. Abstracts give a model **breadth**: it sees almost every disease, drug, and gene name in use. Full-text papers give it **depth**: methods, results tables, and careful reasoning. Guidelines show it **how doctors actually decide** what to do. Textbooks supply **foundations**, the settled background knowledge that research papers take for granted.

**Quality matters as much as quantity**

More text isn't automatically better. Some older papers report findings that were later overturned, and guidelines get updated as evidence changes. A model trained only on outdated text can confidently repeat outdated advice. That's why builders often give extra weight to high-quality sources and keep track of publication dates.

**A useful pattern:** to estimate a corpus's size, multiply the number of documents by the average length of each one. Size is usually counted in words or in *tokens*, the small chunks of text a model actually reads.

$$
\text{total words} = \text{number of documents} \times \text{average words per document}
$$

**Practice problem**

A student builds a small corpus from 2,000 PubMed abstracts that average 250 words each, plus 50 full-text papers that average 4,000 words each.

1. How many words come from the abstracts?
2. How many come from the full-text papers?
3. Which source supplies more words, and what does each one give the model that the other doesn't?

### llm_basics (15.5s)

## Large Language Model Basics

You may already know that a large language model (LLM) is the kind of system behind chatbots, and that it answers by writing text one piece at a time. Now let's look at how that works underneath.

An LLM doesn't read whole words the way you do. It breaks text into **tokens**, which are small chunks such as a short word, part of a longer word, or a punctuation mark. "Unbelievable!" might become three tokens: "un", "believ", and "able!". The model is a **neural network**, a large mathematical function with billions of adjustable numbers called **parameters**. During **training**, it reads a huge amount of text and keeps nudging those parameters so it gets better at one job: guessing the next token.

For each possible next token, the model produces a probability. Written in standard notation, it estimates

$$
P(\text{next token} \mid \text{all previous tokens})
$$

which you can read as "the chance of this next token, given everything written so far." The model picks a likely token, adds it to the text, and repeats the process. A full answer is just this loop run hundreds of times.

**The key pattern:** an LLM produces text that *sounds likely*, not text that has been *checked as true*. It has no built-in fact table to look things up in. When its training data was thin or mixed on a topic, it can write a confident, fluent sentence that is wrong. This is often called a **hallucination**. That's why its answers on facts, numbers, or health advice need checking against reliable sources.

**Practice problem:** A model has seen the text "The capital of France is" and gives these next-token probabilities: "Paris" 0.92, "Lyon" 0.03, "a" 0.04, "the" 0.01.
1. Check that the probabilities add up to 1.
2. Which token is the model most likely to choose?
3. Suppose the model writes "The capital of Australia is Sydney." (The real capital is Canberra.) In one sentence, explain how a next-token predictor could make that mistake.

### domain_adaptation (22.4s)

## Domain Adaptation

**Recap.** A general large language model (LLM) has read a huge mix of text, including news, novels and websites, so it knows a little about everything. Domain adaptation is like sending a well-read generalist to medical school. You keep what it already knows and give it extra, focused training on medical text.

**How it works.** There are two main stages, and each uses a different kind of data:

| Stage | Training data | What the model gains |
|---|---|---|
| Continued pretraining | Large amounts of raw medical text: journal articles, textbooks, and clinical notes with patient details removed | Words like "tachycardia" or "HbA1c" and the way clinicians write |
| Fine-tuning | Smaller sets of question–answer pairs written or checked by experts | The ability to answer medical questions clearly and helpfully |

In continued pretraining, the model does the same job it did at the start: it predicts the next word. For a passage of words $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$, its training loss is

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})
$$

When this loss goes down, the model is less "surprised" by medical writing.

**The pattern.** Start general, then go narrow. Broad knowledge comes first, medical text second and task examples last. Each stage usually uses much less data than the one before it. There is one risk. If you train too long on narrow text, the model can lose some of its general skills, which is called *catastrophic forgetting*. To prevent this, teams often mix some general text back in. Also, a better score on medical tests does not turn a model into a doctor. Qualified people still need to check its answers.

**Practice problem.** A general model was pretrained on 2 trillion tokens. A team continues pretraining it on 50 billion tokens of medical text. Then they fine-tune it on 20,000 question–answer pairs that doctors have reviewed.

(a) The medical text is what percentage of the original pretraining amount?

(b) Which stage helps most with learning the word "bradycardia"? Which stage helps most with answering "What does a low resting heart rate mean?" Explain your reasoning.

### hallucination_and_limits (18.5s)

## Hallucination and Limits

**Quick recap:** A large language model (LLM) writes by predicting which words are likely to come next. That's why it sounds fluent. It also means the model is producing text that sounds right, which is not the same as checking whether the text is true.

**How it goes wrong.** When an LLM confidently makes something up, it's called a *hallucination*. Health questions bring out three common kinds:

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| False fact | Claiming a resting heart rate of 120 beats per minute is "normal for teens" (the usual adult range is about 60–100 bpm) |
| Invented citation | Naming a study, journal, or author that doesn't exist |
| Unsafe advice | Suggesting a medicine dose that is too high, or telling you to skip seeing a doctor |

The model doesn't sound any less certain when it's wrong. Its tone tells you nothing about whether it's accurate.

**A deeper limit: missing information.** Even a model that never made a mistake still couldn't see you. A doctor uses four things a chatbot doesn't have:

- **Examination:** listening to your lungs, looking at a rash
- **History:** your past illnesses, allergies, and family patterns
- **Test results:** blood glucose, blood pressure, X-rays
- **Follow-up:** checking whether a treatment is actually working

So a general answer about "chest pain" can't tell a pulled muscle apart from a heart problem. Only an in-person assessment can.

**The rule to remember:** Use AI to *learn and prepare questions*, not to *diagnose or dose*. Check any specific number, citation, or treatment against a trusted source, such as a pharmacist, a doctor, or an official health website. Urgent symptoms always go to a real person right away.

**Practice problem:** A chatbot tells your friend, "Take 1,500 mg of ibuprofen at once for a headache — Smith et al. (2019) proves it's safe." Find **two** warning signs in this answer. Then write one question your friend should ask a pharmacist instead. (Hint: compare the dose to what's printed on a typical over-the-counter label.)

### medical_benchmarks (15.1s)

## Medical Benchmarks

You already know that an AI model can answer medical questions. How do we decide whether one model does it better than another? Researchers use **medical benchmarks**: fixed sets of exam-style questions that every model answers under the same conditions, so the scores can be compared fairly.

**How a benchmark is built.** Each benchmark is a collection of questions with known correct answers. Two common examples:

| Benchmark | Question style | Answer format |
|---|---|---|
| MedQA | Questions modeled on medical licensing exams (such as the US exam doctors take) | Multiple choice (4–5 options) |
| PubMedQA | A short summary of a research paper plus a question about it | Yes / No / Maybe |

MedQA mostly tests whether a model can **recall** medical facts and **reason** through a patient case written as text. PubMedQA tests whether it can read research findings and draw the right conclusion.

**The scoring rule.** Most of these benchmarks report **accuracy**:

$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{number of correct answers}}{\text{total number of questions}} \times 100\%
$$

**What the score does not tell you.** A high score means the model is good at answering *written test questions*. Real patient care is different. Patients describe symptoms vaguely, information is often missing, and a wrong answer can hurt someone. A benchmark doesn't check whether a model admits when it's unsure, notices a dangerous drug combination, or knows when to say "see a doctor now." That's why researchers say benchmarks measure **knowledge and reasoning on questions, not safety in real care**. Passing an exam is one step toward being trusted, for people and for AI, and it is never the only step.

**Practice problem.** Model A answers 820 of 1,000 MedQA questions correctly. Model B answers 410 of 500 correctly.
1. Calculate each model's accuracy.
2. Which model scored higher?
3. Name one thing a real patient might need that this score cannot show you.

### open_weights_licensing (16.0s)

## Open Weights and Licenses

You already know that some AI models are "open": their creators publish the trained **weights**, the billions of numbers the model learned during training. If you download those weights, you can run the model on your own computer without sending your data to a company's server. That is the starting idea. The next step is understanding that "open" comes in several forms.

**What actually gets released?** A model has three main parts. The **weights** are the learned numbers. The **code** is the software that loads and runs them. The **training data** is the text the model learned from. Many "open" models release the weights and code but keep the training data private. That is why people say **open weights** instead of **open source**: you can use the model, but you can't fully rebuild it from scratch.

**What are you allowed to do?** Every release comes with a **license**, a legal document that sets the rules. Most licenses fall into one of three groups:

| License type | Typical example | Commercial use? |
|---|---|---|
| Permissive | Apache 2.0, MIT | Yes, with few conditions (keep the notice) |
| Custom "community" license | Several big-company model licenses | Usually yes, but with conditions, such as an acceptable-use policy or limits for very large companies |
| Non-commercial / research-only | Creative Commons NC, research licenses | No, only study and experiments |

**The rule in words:** being able to download a model doesn't mean you have permission to use it any way you like. Before building something, check three things: (1) can you use it commercially, (2) what uses does it forbid, and (3) what do you have to do in return, such as giving credit or passing the same license along?

**Practice problem:** You want to build a free homework-helper app now and maybe sell a premium version later. Model A uses Apache 2.0. Model B is labeled "research use only." Model C has a community license that bans certain harmful uses. Which models could you use for the paid version, and what should you read before choosing Model C?

### benchmark_vs_clinical_safety (21.4s)

## Benchmark Score vs Clinical Safety

**Quick recap:** An AI model can score very high on MedQA. MedQA is a large set of multiple-choice questions written in the style of the US medical licensing exam. A high score shows a real skill. Passing an exam is still not the same as being safe with a real patient.

**What a score actually measures:** A benchmark score measures one thing, which is accuracy:

$$
\text{accuracy} = \frac{\text{number of correct answers}}{\text{total number of questions}}
$$

Every MedQA question gives the model a tidy case. The key facts are already written out, there are four or five options, and exactly one is correct. Real clinical work is messier. Being safe depends on behaviors the exam never tests:

| Skill | Tested by MedQA? |
|---|---|
| Choosing the best answer from a list | Yes |
| Asking follow-up questions when information is missing | No |
| Noticing red flags (chest pain with shortness of breath, a sudden "worst-ever" headache) | Only when the question spells them out |
| Saying "get emergency care now" or refusing an unsafe request | No |

A model can be 90% accurate and still give confident, calm advice to someone describing stroke symptoms without ever telling them to call emergency services. The exam would never notice that failure.

**The rule:** A benchmark score tells you what a model knows when the question is clean and complete. Safety has to be measured separately, by testing how the model behaves when information is incomplete, the situation is urgent, or the request is risky. You need both kinds of evidence before trusting a model with health questions.

**Practice problem:** A model answers 1,080 of 1,200 MedQA questions correctly.
(a) What is its accuracy as a percentage?
(b) A user writes: "My dad suddenly can't lift his left arm and his speech is slurred. What vitamin should he take?" List two things a clinically safe reply must do that the accuracy score from part (a) cannot tell you.

### biological_foundation_models (16.3s)

## Biological Foundation Models

You already know that a language model learns by reading huge amounts of text and guessing the missing or next word. Biological foundation models use the same idea, but their "text" comes from biology. They learn the patterns of life's molecules from millions of examples.

**What counts as a "sentence"?** The trick is to write biological data as a sequence of tokens:

| Model | Training data | One "token" | What it predicts |
|---|---|---|---|
| AlphaFold | Protein sequences and known 3D structures | One amino acid | How the protein folds in 3D |
| Geneformer | About 30 million single cells | One gene, ranked by how active it is | Which genes matter in a cell's state |
| scGPT | About 33 million single cells | A gene and its activity level | Cell types and responses to changes |

A protein is a chain built from 20 kinds of amino acids, so you can write it as a string of letters such as $\texttt{MKTAYIAK}\ldots$. A cell's gene activity can be written as a list of numbers, one for each of roughly 20,000 genes.

**How they learn.** During pretraining, the model hides some tokens and tries to predict them from the tokens around them. If it guesses a hidden amino acid or gene correctly, it has learned something real about how biology fits together. Researchers can then fine-tune it for a specific task, such as spotting a disease-related cell type. AlphaFold's protein structure predictions were good enough that its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

**Why the search space is huge.** For a protein that is $n$ amino acids long, each position has 20 choices, so the number of possible sequences is

$$
N = 20^{n}
$$

That is far too many to test in a lab one at a time. This is why models that learn the patterns are so useful.

**Practice problem:** A short peptide is 4 amino acids long. How many different sequences are possible? Now add one more amino acid. By what factor does the count grow?

### digital_physiology_models (16.9s)

## Digital Physiology Models

You already know that AI chatbots learn patterns from huge amounts of text. A digital physiology model works differently. Instead of guessing from examples, it builds a simplified copy of the body out of physics and chemistry rules, then calculates what happens. Researchers call these *mechanistic* models, because they spell out how each part actually works.

**How they are built.** Each model has three layers:

| Layer | What it holds | Example tool |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Bones, joints, muscles, organs, cells | OpenSim (muscles and joints) |
| Rules | Equations for force, flow, and chemical reactions | CellML and SBML (cell and organ models) |
| Inputs | Measured values like heart rate or muscle activation | Data from sensors or experiments |

OpenSim is free, open-source software. With it you can simulate a person walking and estimate how hard each leg muscle pulls. The Physiome Project is an international effort to link models of cells, tissues, and organs into one connected picture of the body. CellML and SBML are shared file formats, so a heart-cell model built in one lab can run in another lab's software.

**The core pattern.** You give the model inputs, it applies known rules, and it predicts outputs. Then you check those predictions against real measurements. A model is only trusted after it passes that check. Many heart models are built on one standard relationship:

$$
\text{CO} = \text{HR} \times \text{SV}
$$

Here $\text{CO}$ is cardiac output (how much blood the heart pumps per minute), $\text{HR}$ is heart rate in beats per minute, and $\text{SV}$ is stroke volume (how much blood leaves the heart with each beat).

**Practice problem.** A heart model is set to a resting heart rate of $70$ beats/min and a stroke volume of $70$ mL. (a) Calculate the cardiac output in liters per minute. (b) The simulated person starts jogging. Heart rate rises to $140$ beats/min and stroke volume rises to $100$ mL. What is the new cardiac output? (c) By roughly how many times did it increase?

### open_medical_llms (18.2s)

## Open Medical LLMs

A general large language model learns from a huge mix of text from the internet. An open medical LLM starts from one of those general models and is then trained further on medical text. Think of a student who finishes general school and then goes on to medical school. "Open-weight" means the model's learned numbers, called weights, are published. Anyone can download the model, run it on their own computer, and study or adjust it.

**How these models are built.** Most open medical LLMs follow the same three steps:

| Step | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Base model | Start from a general open model | Llama 2, Llama 3, Mistral, Gemma |
| 2. Domain adaptation | Keep training on biomedical text such as research papers, textbooks and clinical guidelines | PubMed Central articles |
| 3. Instruction tuning | Teach the model to answer questions in a helpful format | Medical exam-style Q&A |

Here is how some well-known models map onto these steps:

- **Meditron** (EPFL): built on Llama 2 and trained further on PubMed papers and clinical practice guidelines.
- **BioMistral**: built on Mistral and trained on PubMed Central articles.
- **OpenBioLLM**: built on Llama 3 and tuned for biomedical tasks.
- **PMC-LLaMA**: built on LLaMA and trained on medical papers and textbooks.
- **MedGemma** (Google): built on Gemma. It has a multimodal version that can read medical images such as X-rays alongside text.

**The pattern to remember:** a medical model inherits the strengths, weaknesses and *license* of its base model, plus whatever its medical training adds. Models are updated and relicensed often, so check the current version and license before using one. Some licenses restrict commercial or clinical use. A high score on a medical exam benchmark does not mean a model is safe to use with real patients. None of these models replaces a doctor.

**Practice problem:** A school science club wants a model that can describe what it sees in a chest X-ray image for a class project. Which model above is the best fit, and why? Name two things the club should check before downloading it.

### retrieval_grounding (14.8s)

## Retrieval and Grounding (RAG)

You already know that a language model can sound confident and still be wrong. Retrieval and grounding is one way to fix part of that problem. Instead of answering from memory alone, the model first looks up real sources and then answers from what it found.

**How it works.** A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system has three steps:

1. **Retrieve.** Your question goes to a search system. It pulls the most relevant passages from a trusted collection, such as clinical guidelines, research papers, or your own class notes.
2. **Ground.** Those passages are added to the prompt, along with an instruction like "Answer only from these sources."
3. **Cite.** The model writes its answer and marks which source supports each claim, for example "[2]", so you can check it.

Suppose you ask, "What fasting blood glucose level counts as diabetes?" A RAG system might pull a passage from an American Diabetes Association guideline and answer: "A fasting level of 126 mg/dL or higher, confirmed on repeat testing [1]." You can open source [1] and confirm the number yourself.

**Why it helps, and where it doesn't.** Grounding cuts down on made-up facts because the model has real text in front of it. It doesn't remove them completely. The model can still:

- misread or misquote a passage
- mix up two sources
- attach a citation to a claim the source never makes
- use an outdated or low-quality source if that's what the search returned

**The rule:** an answer is only as good as its sources, and a citation is only useful if you actually check it.

**Practice problem.** A RAG chatbot says: "Adults should get at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise per week [1], and drinking 5 liters of water daily prevents all colds [2]." You open both sources. Source 1 is a WHO activity guideline. Source 2 is a general nutrition article that says nothing about colds. Which claim is grounded? Which one is not? What should you do with each one?

### tcm_text_corpora (18.4s)

## TCM and Chinese Medical Text

You already know that a language model learns by reading huge amounts of text. A model that answers questions about TCM learns the same way: it reads Chinese medical writing and picks up how words like qi (气), meridians (经络, jingluo) and pattern (证, zheng) are used.

**Three kinds of text**

Most of this material falls into three groups, and each one teaches a model something different:

| Source type | Examples | What a model picks up |
|---|---|---|
| Classical works | *Huangdi Neijing* (Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic), *Shanghan Lun* (Treatise on Cold Damage), *Bencao Gangmu* (Compendium of Materia Medica) | Core terms and the theory built from them, written in old, compressed classical Chinese |
| Clinical records | Case records (医案) from doctors, past and present | How a pattern is named from symptoms, tongue and pulse, and which herbs were then prescribed |
| Modern Q&A | Online consultations, textbooks, licensing-exam questions | Everyday modern wording and a question-then-answer format |

**The pattern to remember**

A model learns how a word is used. It does not learn whether the idea behind the word is true. If a word like "qi" shows up in thousands of passages, the model gets good at using it fluently in sentences like the ones it read. Being fluent is not the same as having medical evidence. Many TCM concepts, meridians among them, have no confirmed physical structure, and controlled trials don't support many classical claims. A model that writes confidently about them is repeating the texts it read. It is not confirming anything.

Each source type also has its own blind spots. Classical works are often unclear and are read in different ways by different commentators. Case records tend to keep the successes and leave out the failures. Online Q&A ranges from careful to plainly wrong.

**Practice problem**

Sort each item into one of the three source types:
(a) a 1920s doctor's notes on treating a patient's cough;
(b) a forum post that asks "What does damp-heat mean?" and the reply under it;
(c) a passage from the *Shanghan Lun*.

Then choose one of the three and write a sentence explaining why a model trained mostly on that source type might give a misleading answer.

### general_vs_specialized (15.7s)

## General vs Specialized Models

Earlier you saw that an AI model is like a student who learned from a huge pile of text. A "general" model read widely: science, history, recipes, conversations. A "specialized" model took a general model and kept studying, here on medical textbooks and clinical notes. That process is called **fine-tuning**. You might guess the specialist always wins on health questions. Often it doesn't.

**Why the general model can keep up**

Strong open general models such as Llama, Qwen and Gemma already read a lot of medical material during training. New versions come out often, and each one usually improves on reasoning. A medical fine-tune is often built on an *older* base model. By the time it is released, a newer general model may score just as well on medical question-answering tests, or better. The word "medical" in a model's name is a label, not proof.

**How to choose**

| Factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Recent benchmarks | How does it score on current medical question tests? |
| Language support | Does it handle the language your users speak? |
| Size | Will it fit on the computer you have? |
| License | Are you allowed to use it for your purpose? |

Size has a real rule. The memory a model needs is roughly its number of parameters times the bytes stored for each one:

$$
\text{memory} \approx \text{parameters} \times \text{bytes per parameter}
$$

At 16-bit precision each parameter takes 2 bytes, so an 8-billion-parameter model needs about $8 \times 10^9 \times 2 = 16$ GB. That doesn't count extra working memory.

Even the best model can be confidently wrong. Health answers from any AI still need checking by a qualified person.

**Practice problem**

Your school laptop's graphics card has 12 GB of memory. You're choosing between a 7-billion-parameter medical fine-tune and a 4-billion-parameter general model. The general model scored higher on a medical quiz benchmark released this year. Both run at 16-bit precision.

1. How much memory does each model need?
2. Which one fits, and which would you pick? Give two reasons.

### safe_personal_use (13.9s)

## Safe Personal Use of Health AI

**A quick recap.** A health AI model is a powerful study partner. It can explain words like "systolic" or "insulin resistance" in plain language, at any hour. But it is not a doctor. It has never examined you, it cannot see your lab results in context, and sometimes it states wrong things with complete confidence.

**How safe use works.** It helps to sort the tasks you might give an AI into two groups:

| Good uses (tutor and drafting aid) | Unsafe uses (leave these to a clinician) |
|---|---|
| "Explain what a resting heart rate of 72 beats per minute means." | "Do I have a heart problem?" (diagnosis) |
| "Compare aerobic and strength training." | "Should I skip my asthma inhaler this week?" (changing medication) |
| "Help me write questions for my doctor." | "My chest hurts, but can I wait until Monday?" (delaying care) |
| "Summarize what the CDC says about sleep for teens." | Trusting any claim without checking a source |

The good uses all share one feature: a qualified person, or a reliable source, still makes the final call. The AI helps you understand things and get ready to ask good questions. It does not make medical decisions for you.

**The rule, in words.** *Ask it to explain, compare, and draft questions. Check what it says against primary sources* such as the CDC, the NIH, the WHO, or your clinician's handouts. *Never let it diagnose you, change a prescribed medicine, or delay care.* Warning signs mean you seek real help right away: call emergency services or tell a trusted adult. These signs include chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, sudden weakness on one side of the body, or thoughts of self-harm.

1. Which unsafe use is happening here?

### simulation_vs_language_models (15.7s)

## Simulators vs Language Models

You've already seen that computers can help us understand the body in two very different ways. One kind of program calculates what the body should do. The other kind writes about what people have said the body does. Let's look at how each one works.

A **simulator**, or mechanistic model, starts from equations that describe physical or biological processes. Take the heart. The amount of blood it pumps each minute, called **cardiac output**, follows a standard relationship:

$$
CO = HR \times SV
$$

Here $HR$ is heart rate in beats per minute and $SV$ is stroke volume, the millilitres of blood pushed out with each beat. Give a simulator new inputs, such as a faster heart rate during exercise, and it computes a new answer. The key feature is that its predictions can be **tested**. You can measure a real person's heart rate and blood flow and check whether the model got it right.

A **language model** works differently. It learns patterns from huge amounts of text and produces a likely-sounding summary of what has been written. It can explain a concept, compare research findings, or put jargon into plain words. But it doesn't run the body's equations, so its answer reflects what people wrote, which may be outdated or wrong.

**The pattern to remember:**

| Question type | Better tool |
|---|---|
| "What number happens if…?" | Simulator |
| "What is known or claimed about…?" | Language model |
| "Is this explanation correct?" | Check it against a simulator or a real measurement |

The two work best as a team. The language model helps you frame the question and explain the result. The simulator produces numbers you can check.

**Practice problem:** A student's resting heart rate is $70$ beats per minute and their stroke volume is $70$ mL. During a jog, their heart rate rises to $140$ beats per minute and their stroke volume rises to $90$ mL.

1. Calculate the cardiac output in mL/min at rest and during the jog.
2. A chatbot says, "Cardiac output roughly doubles when you jog." Use your answers to decide whether that claim holds for this student, and say which tool settled the question.

### tcm_language_models (14.7s)

## TCM Language Models

You already know that a large language model learns patterns from huge amounts of text and then predicts likely next words. A TCM language model is the same kind of system, but its training text includes Chinese medical writing. That is why it can handle the specialized vocabulary of TCM.

**How these models differ.** They fall into two broad groups:

| Type | Examples | Where its TCM knowledge comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Specialized medical models | HuatuoGPT, ShenNong-TCM | Extra training (fine-tuning) on medical questions and answers, and on TCM material |
| General Chinese models | Qwen, DeepSeek | Huge amounts of Chinese text in general, which happens to include many TCM terms and classic texts |

Fine-tuning starts with a general model and trains it further on a narrower set of examples. That is how a specialized model gets used to answering in the style of a clinic or a textbook. General models often do just as well with terminology because they have read so much Chinese text. A term like 气虚 (qi deficiency) or the name of an herbal formula is familiar to them.

**What they are good at.** You can use them to explain a term, translate between Chinese and English, summarize a classical passage, or quiz yourself. **What they are not good at:** deciding what someone should take. These models produce fluent text, not verified facts. They can invent herb doses, miss drug interactions, or confidently repeat claims that clinical research hasn't supported. Some herbs are toxic or interact with medicines, so a wrong suggestion can cause real harm.

**A simple rule.** Use a TCM language model as a study partner and drafting tool. Check its claims against a reliable textbook or source. Never treat its output as a prescription. Questions about treatment belong with a licensed clinician.

## The Health-AI Learning Workflow

You already know that an AI chatbot can explain health topics quickly, and that it can also be confidently wrong. A **workflow** fixes that problem. It is a set of habits that turns a chatbot from something you just believe into a study partner you check.

**The structure: six steps, each with a job**

| Step | What you do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Choose | Pick a model whose license you have read: an open general model, a medical one like MedGemma, or a Chinese-language model for TCM terms | You know what the model is allowed to be used for and what it was trained to handle |
| 2. Protect | Run the model locally if your question involves personal health data | Your data stays on your own computer |
| 3. Ground | Ask for answers with cited sources, then open those sources yourself | AI models sometimes invent references |
| 4. Cross-check | Compare the answer with a textbook or a simulator, such as a heart-rate or glucose model | A second, independent source catches errors |
| 5. Record | Write down what you learned and where you checked it | You can trace and correct the claim later |
| 6. Defer | Leave diagnosis and treatment to qualified professionals | Learning about health is not the same as practicing medicine |

**The pattern in words:** *trust grows only with checking.* An answer you haven't verified is a lead to follow up, not a fact. Step 4 matters most when the topic has real numbers you can test. Steps 3 and 5 matter most for qualitative ideas. For example, in TCM, qi is described in traditional texts, not measured, so your notes should say which text the idea comes from.
