## 火

**Definition.** 火 (huǒ) is a pictographic character depicting rising flames — the earliest oracle-bone forms show a stylized tongue of fire with flaring points, later regularized into the three-stroke standing form used independently today. As a component (bùshǒu 部首) within compound characters, 火 does not always retain its upright shape: beneath a character, it is compressed and rotated into 灬, a horizontal row of four dots, most often seen under characters denoting cooking, heat, or roasting.

**Mechanism.** The transformation from 火 to 灬 is a case of positional allography — the same semantic radical takes a different graphic form depending on where it sits in the character's layout. When 火 occupies the left or top position (as in 炎 "blaze," itself two 火 stacked), it keeps its full pictographic outline. When it occupies the bottom position beneath another component, scribes flattened and abbreviated it to four dots to fit the square character block (fāngkuàizì 方块字) without collapsing the character's vertical proportions. This can be shown as a simple substitution:

$$\underbrace{火}_{\text{fire, standing form}} \xrightarrow{\text{bottom position}} \underbrace{灬}_{\text{fire, four-dot form}}$$

The composed character then reads as:

$$\underbrace{\text{top component}}_{\text{meaning/sound cue}} + \underbrace{灬}_{\text{fire}} = \text{compound}$$

Examples following this pattern include 烹 (pēng, "to cook/boil," from a top component suggesting a vessel or offering over fire), 煮 (zhǔ, "to boil," with 者 phonetic over 灬), and 熬 (áo, "to simmer/stew"). Not every 灬 is fire, however — 燕 (yàn, "swallow [bird]") and 魚-derived characters sometimes carry 灬 as a stylized rendering of legs or a tail, a graphic convergence rather than a semantic one. This ambiguity is itself a documented feature of Chinese paleography, not an exception to be dismissed.

**Literature context.** The 说文解字 (Shuōwén Jiězì, 100 CE) already classifies 火 as one of the foundational pictographs and groups derived characters under it as a radical class. Modern paleographic scholarship (e.g., work compiling oracle-bone and bronze-inscription forms) traces the 火→灬 flattening as part of the broader Warring-States-to-Han standardization process that regularized variable pictographic strokes into fixed grid-compatible components. What remains genuinely open, and is not settled in any single reference, is a systematic inventory: which 灬 characters are semantically fire-derived versus which are graphic coincidences from unrelated etyma (legs, tails), and whether frequency of the two readings shifted across historical periods or across simplified versus traditional orthographies.

**Suggested investigation.** A graduate project could compile a corpus of all Unicode CJK characters containing 灬, cross-reference each against Shuōwén and oracle-bone/bronze paleographic databases (e.g., 汉语多功能字库), and classify each occurrence as fire-derived, legs/tail-derived, or indeterminate. A complementary case study would track a handful of cooking-related 灬 characters (烹, 煮, 蒸, 煎, 熬) across historical texts to see whether their culinary senses narrowed or diversified over time.
