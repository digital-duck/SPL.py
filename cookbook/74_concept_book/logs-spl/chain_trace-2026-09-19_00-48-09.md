### conductance (0.0s)

## Conductance

Conductance measures how easily electric charge flows through a given pathway — an ion channel, a patch of membrane, or a segment of cytoplasm. It is the reciprocal of resistance, $G = 1/R$, and is measured in siemens ($\text{S} = \text{A}/\text{V}$). Where resistance describes how much a pathway impedes current, conductance describes how much it permits current — a single scalar that captures channel density, pore geometry, and ion selectivity all at once.

The defining relationship comes directly from Ohm's law. Current through a conductive pathway is:

$$
I = G \cdot V
$$

where $V$ is the voltage (electrochemical driving force) across the pathway and $I$ is the resulting current. Because $G$ folds resistance's inverse into a single term, a *larger* conductance means a *larger* current for the same driving force — the opposite of how resistance behaves. Physiologically, $G$ rises or falls as channels open or close: more open channels means a larger conducting pathway means a higher $G$.

**Worked example.** Suppose a patch of membrane has a total potassium conductance of $G = 2\ \text{nS}$ (a value reflecting how many potassium channels happen to be open at that moment). If the driving force across the membrane at that instant is $V = 20\ \text{mV}$, the resulting current is:

$$
I = G V = (2 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{S})(0.02\ \text{V}) = 4 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{A} = 40\ \text{pA}
$$

**Problem-solving application.** This relationship lets you predict how a cell's electrical behavior changes when channel populations shift — the basis of neuronal excitability. If a drug blocks half the open potassium channels, the pathway's conductance is cut in half, from 2 nS to 1 nS. For the same 20 mV driving force, the current drops proportionally, from 40 pA to 20 pA — because $I$ scales linearly with $G$ whenever $V$ is fixed. Conversely, if a neuron's conductance for a given ion increases sharply (as happens when voltage-gated channels open during an action potential), the membrane's voltage will shift rapidly toward that ion's equilibrium potential, since higher conductance means that pathway dominates the total current balance across the membrane. Working through $G$, $V$, and $I$ together — and tracking how a change in one forces a change in the others — is the standard toolkit for modeling how shifts in channel behavior translate into measurable electrical signals.

### electrical_current (0.0s)

## Electrical Current

Electrical current, $I$, is the rate at which electric charge flows past a point in a conductor. Formally,

$$
I = \frac{dQ}{dt}
$$

where $Q$ is charge measured in coulombs and $t$ is time in seconds. The SI unit of current is the ampere (A), defined as one coulomb per second. When charge flows at a constant rate over an interval, this simplifies to $I = \Delta Q / \Delta t$. Conventional current is defined as flowing in the direction that positive charge would move, even in metallic conductors where the actual charge carriers are negative electrons drifting the opposite way — a historical convention that remains standard in circuit analysis.

**Worked example.** Suppose a copper wire carries a steady current of 2 A. How much charge passes through a cross-section of the wire in 5 minutes? Convert time to seconds: $t = 300\ \text{s}$. Since $I = Q/t$ for constant current, rearrange to solve for charge:

$$
Q = I \cdot t = (2\ \text{A})(300\ \text{s}) = 600\ \text{C}
$$

This also tells us the number of electrons involved: dividing 600 C by the elementary charge $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ gives approximately $3.75 \times 10^{21}$ electrons — an enormous number, which is why current feels continuous rather than discrete at the macroscopic scale.

**Problem-solving application.** Current is rarely constant in real circuits, so the definition $I = dQ/dt$ becomes essential when charge flow varies with time, such as in AC circuits or during capacitor charging. For instance, if the charge on a capacitor is given by $Q(t) = Q_0(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ during charging, the current at any instant is found by differentiating:

$$
I(t) = \frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{Q_0}{\tau} e^{-t/\tau}
$$

This shows current is largest at $t = 0$ and decays exponentially as the capacitor approaches full charge — a result you cannot obtain from the simple ratio form and that directly explains why charging current drops off over time in RC circuits.

## Electrical Potential

Every membrane in a living cell is a tiny battery. On one side sits a solution rich in some ion — say, potassium — and on the other, a solution poor in it. Left alone, those ions would diffuse down their concentration gradient, spreading out until evenly mixed. But ions carry charge, and a membrane restricts their movement to specific channels. As positive potassium ions leak out through open channels, they leave behind unbalanced negative charge inside the cell, and the resulting electrostatic pull drags the escaping ions back. The system settles into a standoff: chemical diffusion pushing ions one way, electrostatic attraction pulling them the other. The "pressure" stored in that standoff — the work required to move a unit of charge from one side of the membrane to the other — is the electrical potential, measured in volts (joules per coulomb).

This potential is not abstract; it is directly measurable and directly consequential. A resting neuron holds its interior at about $-70\ \text{mV}$ relative to the outside, meaning roughly 70 millivolts of pent-up driving force sit ready to move charge the instant a channel opens. That voltage is set by the balance of ion gradients, described quantitatively by the Nernst equation:

$$E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{outside}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{inside}}}\right)$$

Here $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is temperature in kelvin, $z$ is the ion's charge, $F$ is Faraday's constant, and the bracketed terms are concentrations on each side. This equation is unavoidable, not decorative: it is the only way to predict the equilibrium voltage for a given ion gradient, since it captures the exact point where the electrical force balances the diffusional one.

Problem-solving application: suppose extracellular potassium concentration is 5 mM and intracellular is 140 mM. At body temperature ($T \approx 310\ \text{K}$), with $z = +1$, plugging into the Nernst equation yields $E_K \approx -91\ \text{mV}$ — close to a real neuron's resting potential, confirming potassium is the dominant driver at rest. The same equation, applied to any other permeant ion, gives that ion's own equilibrium voltage from its own inside/outside concentration ratio — a single calculation you repeat, plugging in different concentrations and charges, to characterize any ion gradient across any membrane. Computing $E_{\text{ion}}$ this way is the standard first step in modeling any excitable-cell circuit.
