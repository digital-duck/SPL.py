### scalar_multiplication (12.3s)

## Scalar Multiplication

**Definition.** Scalar multiplication takes a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and a vector $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n)$ and produces a new vector by multiplying every component by $c$:

$$c\mathbf{v} = (cv_1, cv_2, \ldots, cv_n)$$

Geometrically, this stretches or shrinks $\mathbf{v}$ by a factor of $|c|$, and flips its direction if $c < 0$. If $|c| > 1$, the vector lengthens; if $0 < |c| < 1$, it shortens; if $c = 0$, it collapses to the zero vector. Two nonzero vectors $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ are called **parallel** (or scalar multiples of each other) precisely when $\mathbf{w} = c\mathbf{v}$ for some scalar $c$ — this single operation is what lets us formalize the idea of "same direction, different length."

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{v} = (3, -2, 1)$. Compute $2\mathbf{v}$, $-1\mathbf{v}$, and $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}$:

$$2\mathbf{v} = (6, -4, 2), \qquad -\mathbf{v} = (-3, 2, -1), \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v} = \left(\tfrac{3}{2}, -1, \tfrac{1}{2}\right)$$

Each result points along the same line through the origin as $\mathbf{v}$; only the length and, for $-\mathbf{v}$, the direction change. Scalar multiplication satisfies key algebraic properties that make it compatible with vector addition — distributivity, $c(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = c\mathbf{v} + c\mathbf{w}$, and associativity, $(cd)\mathbf{v} = c(d\mathbf{v})$ — which together with addition make $\mathbb{R}^n$ a vector space.

**Problem-solving application.** A common use is normalizing a vector: converting $\mathbf{v}$ into a unit vector (length 1) pointing in the same direction, by choosing $c = \tfrac{1}{\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert}$. For $\mathbf{v} = (3, 4)$, we have $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, so the unit vector is

$$\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{1}{5}(3, 4) = (0.6, 0.8)$$

This technique appears constantly in physics (finding a direction of force without its magnitude) and in computer graphics (normalizing surface normals for lighting calculations). Scalar multiplication also lets you test whether a given vector lies along a specific line: solve $c\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}$ component-by-component and check that a single consistent $c$ satisfies all equations — if it does, the vectors are parallel; if not, they diverge in direction.

### vector_addition (12.4s)

## Vector Addition

The first radical of a vector space is closure under a binary operation: given two vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$, their sum $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ must also belong to $V$. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, addition is defined componentwise:

$$
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n) + (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n) = (u_1+v_1, u_2+v_2, \dots, u_n+v_n)
$$

Geometrically, this is the tip-to-tail rule: place the tail of $\mathbf{v}$ at the tip of $\mathbf{u}$; the sum is the arrow from the origin to the new tip. The operation is commutative ($\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}$) and associative, and it has an identity element, the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$, since $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{u}$ for every $\mathbf{u}$. These properties are not incidental — they are exactly the axioms that make $V$ a group under addition, and closure is what guarantees the operation never sends you outside the space.

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{u} = (3, -1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (-2, 4, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Then

$$
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (3+(-2),\ -1+4,\ 2+1) = (1, 3, 3)
$$

The result is again a triple of real numbers, confirming closure: $\mathbf{R}^3$ never produces a vector with, say, four components or a non-real entry.

**Problem-solving application.** Closure becomes a diagnostic tool, not just a formality, when you check whether a subset $W \subseteq V$ is itself a subspace. Suppose $W = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$, the unit circle. Take $\mathbf{u} = (1, 0)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (0, 1)$, both in $W$. Their sum is $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$, and $1^2 + 1^2 = 2 \ne 1$, so the sum leaves $W$. Closure fails, and $W$ is not a subspace — no further axioms need checking. This test generalizes: whenever you must decide if a candidate set (a solution set of a linear system, the set of polynomials of bounded degree, the set of continuous functions satisfying $f(0)=0$) forms a vector space, verifying closure under addition first is the fastest way to either rule it out or clear the path to confirming the remaining axioms.

### linear_combination (10.5s)

## Linear Combination

A **linear combination** of vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ is any vector built by scaling each one and adding the results together:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i = \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \cdots + \alpha_n v_n
$$

where the $\alpha_i$ are scalars. This single expression is the atomic operation of linear algebra — every subspace, every matrix-vector product, every solution to a linear system is, at bottom, a statement about which linear combinations are achievable and which are not.

**Worked example.** Let $v_1 = (1, 0)$ and $v_2 = (0, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The vector $(3, -2)$ is the linear combination

$$
3v_1 + (-2)v_2 = 3(1,0) + (-2)(0,1) = (3, -2).
$$

Because $v_1$ and $v_2$ span all of $\mathbb{R}^2$, *every* vector in the plane can be written this way, with the coordinates themselves serving as the scalars $\alpha_i$. This is why $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots)$ and "linear combination of the standard basis vectors" mean the same thing.

**Problem-solving application: is $b$ reachable?** A recurring question in linear algebra is whether a target vector $b$ can be expressed as a linear combination of a given set $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$. This is exactly the question $Ax = b$ asks, where the columns of $A$ are the $v_i$ and $x$ holds the unknown scalars $\alpha_i$. Solving the system is nothing more than searching for coefficients that make the combination land on $b$.

For instance, suppose $v_1 = (1, 2)$, $v_2 = (2, 4)$, and $b = (3, 5)$. Setting up $\alpha_1(1,2) + \alpha_2(2,4) = (3,5)$ gives the system

$$
\alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2 = 3, \qquad 2\alpha_1 + 4\alpha_2 = 5.
$$

The second equation is exactly twice the first equation's left side but not its right side ($2 \times 3 = 6 \neq 5$), so no scalars satisfy both — $b$ is *not* a linear combination of $v_1$ and $v_2$. Geometrically, $v_1$ and $v_2$ are parallel (both point along the direction $(1,2)$), so every combination of them lies on that one line, and $b$ sits off it.

This reachability test — can a target be written as $\sum \alpha_i v_i$ — is the seed from which span, linear independence, and the rank of a matrix all grow.

### linear_map (11.9s)

## Linear Map

A linear map is a function $f: V \to W$ between two vector spaces that preserves the algebraic structure of a linear combination: for all vectors $u, v \in V$ and scalars $\alpha, \beta$,

$$f(\alpha u + \beta v) = \alpha f(u) + \beta f(v)$$

This single condition packages two familiar properties — additivity, $f(u+v) = f(u) + f(v)$, and homogeneity, $f(\alpha u) = \alpha f(u)$ — into one statement. A consequence worth internalizing immediately: every linear map sends the zero vector to the zero vector, since $f(0) = f(0 \cdot u) = 0 \cdot f(u) = 0$. Any function that fails this is automatically not linear.

**Worked example.** Let $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be rotation by $90°$ counterclockwise, $f(x,y) = (-y, x)$. Check linearity directly: for $u=(x_1,y_1)$, $v=(x_2,y_2)$,

$$f(\alpha u + \beta v) = f(\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2,\ \alpha y_1 + \beta y_2) = (-\alpha y_1 - \beta y_2,\ \alpha x_1 + \beta x_2)$$

which equals $\alpha(-y_1, x_1) + \beta(-y_2, x_2) = \alpha f(u) + \beta f(v)$. Compare this to $g(x,y) = (x+1, y)$, a translation: $g(0,0) = (1,0) \neq (0,0)$, so $g$ fails immediately — translations are never linear, only affine.

**Problem-solving application.** Because a linear map is fully determined by where it sends a basis, you never need to check the defining equation for infinitely many vectors — only for a handful of basis vectors, then extend by linearity. For $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, knowing $f(1,0) = (2,3)$ and $f(0,1) = (-1,4)$ determines $f$ everywhere: since any $(x,y) = x(1,0) + y(0,1)$,

$$f(x,y) = x f(1,0) + y f(0,1) = x(2,3) + y(-1,4) = (2x - y,\ 3x + 4y)$$

This is exactly the logic behind representing $f$ as a matrix acting on coordinate vectors, and it turns an infinite verification problem — "does this hold for every input?" — into a finite computation on basis images. When solving problems, use this shortcut: to determine an unknown linear map, find its action on a basis; to verify a candidate map is linear, test the defining equation abstractly rather than plugging in numerical examples, since no finite set of numerical checks can prove linearity in general.

### eigenpair (10.2s)

## Eigenpair

An eigenpair of a linear map $f$ is a nonzero vector $v$ paired with a scalar $\lambda$ such that

$$f(v) = \lambda v.$$

Here $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue** and $v$ is the **eigenvector**. The equation says something geometrically sharp: for most vectors, applying $f$ rotates and stretches them in some complicated way, but along the special direction $v$, $f$ acts as nothing more than scalar multiplication — pure stretching, shrinking, or flipping, with no change of direction. Eigenpairs are the directions along which a linear map's action reduces to arithmetic.

**Worked example.** Let $f$ be given by the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Take $v = (1,0)$: then $Av = (2,0) = 2v$, so $(\lambda, v) = (2, (1,0))$ is an eigenpair. Take $v = (0,1)$: then $Av = (0,3) = 3v$, giving eigenpair $(3, (0,1))$. Now try $u = (1,1)$: $Au = (2,3)$, which is not a scalar multiple of $u$ — so $u$ is not an eigenvector. This illustrates that eigenpairs are special; a generic vector gets rotated relative to itself, not just scaled.

**Finding eigenpairs.** Rearranging $f(v) = \lambda v$ as $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$ shows that $v \neq 0$ exists only when $A - \lambda I$ is singular, i.e., $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. This is the **characteristic equation**; its roots are the eigenvalues, and substituting each root back lets you solve the linear system for the corresponding eigenvector(s).

**Problem-solving application.** Eigenpairs let you decompose a complicated repeated transformation into simple pieces. Suppose you want $A^{10}v$ for the matrix above. Instead of multiplying $A$ by itself ten times, write $v$ in the eigenbasis, say $v = c_1(1,0) + c_2(0,1)$. Then $A^{10}v = c_1 \cdot 2^{10}(1,0) + c_2 \cdot 3^{10}(0,1)$, since each eigenvector direction just gets its eigenvalue raised to the tenth power. This trick — replacing repeated matrix multiplication with repeated scalar multiplication along eigendirections — underlies applications from population growth models to Google's PageRank algorithm, wherever a system evolves under the same linear map applied many times.

### normal_modes (13.4s)

## Normal Modes

A system of coupled oscillators looks complicated when you track individual masses: each one pulls on its neighbors, so a disturbance anywhere ripples through the whole system. Normal modes are the hidden coordinates in which this coupling disappears — patterns of motion where every part of the system oscillates at the *same* frequency, in a fixed ratio of amplitudes, as if the masses were independent single oscillators.

Formally, for small oscillations near equilibrium, Newton's second law for $n$ coupled masses takes the matrix form

$$M\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = -K\mathbf{x},$$

where $\mathbf{x}$ is the displacement vector, $M$ is the (diagonal) mass matrix, and $K$ is the stiffness matrix encoding the spring couplings. Seeking solutions of the form $\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}e^{i\omega t}$ turns this differential equation into an algebraic eigenvalue problem:

$$K\mathbf{v} = \omega^2 M \mathbf{v}.$$

Each eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_k$ (a normal mode shape) has a matching eigenvalue $\omega_k^2$ (its squared normal frequency). Because $K$ and $M$ are symmetric and positive (semi-)definite, the eigenvectors form a complete, $M$-orthogonal basis — any physically allowed motion of the system is a superposition of these modes.

**Worked example.** Two equal masses $m$ connected to walls by outer springs of constant $k$ and to each other by a coupling spring $k_c$ satisfy $M = mI$ and $K = \begin{pmatrix} k+k_c & -k_c \\ -k_c & k+k_c \end{pmatrix}$. Solving $\det(K-\omega^2 M)=0$ gives two modes: the symmetric mode $\mathbf{v}_1=(1,1)$ with $\omega_1^2 = k/m$ (masses move together, coupling spring unstretched), and the antisymmetric mode $\mathbf{v}_2=(1,-1)$ with $\omega_2^2 = (k+2k_c)/m$ (masses move oppositely, stretching the coupler and raising the frequency).

**Application.** To solve for motion from arbitrary initial conditions, decompose $\mathbf{x}(0)$ into the mode basis, evolve each mode independently at its own $\omega_k$, then recombine. This same eigenvalue strategy — diagonalizing a coupling matrix to find independent oscillatory modes — reappears in molecular vibrations, structural engineering (building sway modes), and even quantum mechanics, making the normal-mode decomposition one of the most reusable tools in applied linear algebra.
