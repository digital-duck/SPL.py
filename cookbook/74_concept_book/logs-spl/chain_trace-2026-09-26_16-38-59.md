### 一 (1.4s)

## 一

Hold up one finger. Just one. Before you can count two, three, or a hundred, you need this single idea: *oneness* — a single, whole, complete thing standing by itself.

The character 一 draws exactly what it means: one flat stroke. No curls, no extra parts — because "one" is the simplest idea there is. It's the starting point every other number grows from, the same way the number $1$ is the starting point of counting: $1, 2, 3, \dots$

But 一 means more than the number one. It also means *whole* or *entire* — like saying "the entire day" or "throughout the journey." Think of a single circle drawn without lifting your pencil: it's one continuous, undivided thing. That's the deeper feeling behind 一 — not just "a single item," but "one complete whole with nothing missing and nothing extra."

In Chinese writing, 一 is also a building block. It is the very first radical, numbered 1, meaning many characters that involve flatness, ground, or a single line start with this same stroke.

**Now you try:** Draw one straight horizontal line. That line *is* 一. Can you think of something in your day that comes as "one whole thing" — one sun, one you?

### 丨 (1.2s)

## 丨

Imagine a tally mark — the single line you scratch on paper to count "one." A farmer counting sheep going through a gate makes a mark for each one: 丨丨丨丨. In Chinese writing, this exact idea survives as its own character: 丨, meaning "line" or "vertical stroke." It shows up as a straight line dropping from top to bottom, like a plumb line hanging under gravity, or a flagpole standing straight up.

Why does a single line deserve to be its own character? Because in Chinese, meaning is built the way a shape is built — from a small set of basic strokes, the same way every letter you write is built from lines and curves. 丨 is one of the most fundamental strokes, and it becomes a building block inside many other characters, like 中 (middle) and 十 (ten), where a vertical line cuts through something to divide or mark it.

Now you try: draw a straight vertical line on paper, top to bottom, without lifting your pencil. Can you find that same line hiding inside another character you know?

### 又 (1.7s)

## 又

Picture a small hand, palm turned to grab something in front of you. Long ago, this was drawn as a simple curved shape — three strokes, like fingers closing. That drawing became 又.

Its first job was to mean "again" or "also" — because a hand that grabs once often grabs again. But something clever happened next: instead of inventing a brand-new drawing every time scribes needed a "hand" idea inside a bigger character, they reused 又 as a building block. Component and meaning combine — this is a real principle in character formation called compound indication: a new character's meaning comes from combining the meanings of its parts, written simply as

$$C = A + B$$

where $A$ and $B$ are components already known, and $C$ is their combined meaning. Take 取 (to take): an ear, 耳, plus a hand, 又 — literally "hand reaching for an ear" — becomes "to take."

Now you try: find 又 hiding inside 双 or 对. What hand-action might it be pointing to?

### 十 (1.5s)

## 十

Hold up all ten fingers. That's it — that's the whole idea behind 十. When you count on your fingers and run out of them all at once, you've hit something special: a full group of ten, the size we use to organize every number after it.

Look at the character: a horizontal stroke 一 crossing a vertical stroke 丨, like two hands meeting, or a line marking a finished count. The horizontal line is "one" — a single mark. The vertical line cuts straight through it, as if to say: this isn't just one anymore, it's a whole new unit.

Here's the elegant part. Once you have ten, you don't need a brand-new symbol for eleven or twelve — you just say "ten-one" (十一), "ten-two" (十二). Ten becomes a building block, like a bag that holds exactly ten single things. This grouping-by-tens idea is the seed of place value, the system where where a digit sits changes what it's worth.

Now you try: count ten pebbles, put them in a cup. That cup is now one "ten." How many pebbles are two cups?

### 寸 (1.3s)

## 寸

Picture this: long ago, someone needed to measure a piece of wood but had no ruler. So they used what was always with them — their own hand. They pressed a finger against their wrist, right where the pulse beats, and called that tiny distance one "cùn" (寸). Look at the character: 一 (a mark) sits just below 又 (a hand) — literally "a hand, marked one unit up."

This is the birth of a measurement unit: a fixed, agreed-upon length that everyone can use to compare things. Before rulers existed, humans needed something small, body-based, and repeatable.

Later, cùn became part of an official system: 10 cùn make 1 chǐ (a foot-like unit), so $1 \text{ chǐ} = 10 \text{ cùn}$. This ratio matters — units aren't random, they nest inside each other by clean multiples, the same way centimeters nest inside meters.

Now you try: measure the width of your own thumb with a ruler. Is it close to 1 inch? Congratulations — your ancestors used exactly this trick to build the very first rulers!

### 丿 (1.3s)

## 丿

Imagine drawing one clean stroke of a pencil, sweeping down and to the left, like a leaf falling from a tree in the wind. That single curved slide is called 丿, and Chinese scholars long ago noticed something clever: if you sorted every character by its very first meaningful stroke-shape, you could organize thousands of characters the way a librarian organizes books — by a shared starting feature instead of by sound or meaning.

Why does this feel right? Because it's a sorting rule, not a guess. In the Kangxi Dictionary, 丿 is radical number 4, one of 214 radical "buckets" that every character can be filed under. This is the same idea mathematicians use when they classify objects by a defining property: pick one consistent feature, then group everything that shares it.

丿 itself is simple: one downward-left stroke, direction fixed, no branching, no crossing.

Now you try: find three characters that begin with a stroke sliding down-left, and guess whether 丿 is hiding inside them.

### 土 (1.2s)

## 土

Picture a farmer pushing a stick straight down into the ground, then laying another stick flat across the dirt at its base. That crossing point — where a mound of earth meets a marker — is exactly the shape of 土 (tǔ), meaning earth, soil, or clay.

Look closely and you'll see the character is built from two simpler pieces: 十 (the number ten, a cross shape) sitting on top of 一 (the number one, a flat line). The flat line is the ground; the cross above it is a small mound or plant pushing up through the soil. This is how many Chinese characters work — small, already-known pieces combine to build a new meaning, much like snapping together blocks you already recognize.

We can write this composition simply:
$$
土 = 十 + 一
$$

Here "+" means "stacked to form a new character," not arithmetic addition — the meaning comes from position, not sum.

Now you try: find one part of 土 in another character you know, and guess whether that part means "ground" there too.

### 木 (1.5s)

## 木

Picture a single tree standing alone in a field: a trunk, a spread of branches, and roots holding it steady in the ground. That's the whole idea behind 木 — the character itself is just a tiny drawing of that tree, its branches reaching out to both sides.

Why does this feel right? Because your eye already knows what a tree looks like, so the shape doesn't need to be explained — it needs to be recognized. Ancient writers didn't invent a code; they sketched what they saw and simplified the sketch until it became fast to write.

Once you know 木 as "tree," you can build with it. Two trees side by side make 林, a small woods. Three trees together make 森, a dense forest. And when a body feels heavy and unresponsive, as if it's turned to wood, that same character appears in 麻木, meaning numb.

Now you try: draw one tree, then two, then three, and say what each new picture might mean before you look it up.

### 宀 (1.6s)

## 宀

Picture the roof of a little house, drawn in just two strokes — one slanting down on each side, meeting at a peak, like a tent or the letter A with no crossbar. That's it. That's 宀. It doesn't say what's *under* the roof — just that something is being sheltered.

Why does this feel right? Because a roof is the first thing that makes a "house" different from just "walls." Ancient scribes noticed that lots of words about home, family, and safety all seem to need that shelter shape somewhere in them — so they reused the same roof symbol as a building block, again and again.

You've already seen it hiding inside bigger characters: in 家 (home — a roof over what was originally a pig, meaning a household), in 安 (peaceful — a roof over a woman, suggesting safety indoors), and in 定 (settled — a roof over "correct," meaning fixed in place).

This radical is called 寶蓋 (bǎo gài), meaning "treasure lid" — a lid that covers and protects something precious underneath.

Now you try: find one more character with a roof on top, and guess — what's it protecting?

### 寺 (1.8s)

## 寺

Long ago, workers measured land using their hands: a thumb's width, a palm's span. The character 寸 began as a picture of a hand with a small mark near the wrist — showing the exact spot where you feel your pulse, about one inch from the base of your thumb. That's why 寸 means "a tiny, precise measure."

Now picture 土, the sign for earth or ground — a plant sprouting up from a mound of soil.

Put them together: earth held with careful, measured hands. That is 寺 — a place built on the ground with exact, disciplined care. Long ago it named a government office, where officials measured out laws and duties with precision. Later, the same idea of careful devotion came to describe temples and mosques — places where people give precise, measured attention to prayer and ritual, day after day.

So 寺 teaches us that sacred and official places share something: they are built on earth, but shaped by disciplined, careful hands.

Now you try: Can you find 寸 hiding inside another character you know? What "careful measuring" idea might it add there?

### 彳 (3.1s)

## 彳

Imagine you are walking down a path, but for just one step you only use your left foot — like a soldier practicing a slow-motion march. That single, deliberate left-footstep is exactly what this character pictures. It comes from an old picture of a crossroads or a walking road, and this piece keeps just the left half of that picture.

Here's why that feels right: many Chinese characters about walking, moving, or traveling use this small picture as a building block, because the artist who invented the character needed a quick way to say "this word has something to do with going somewhere on foot." So whenever you spot 彳 tucked into the left side of a bigger character, your brain gets a hint: motion is involved.

Think of it like a tag or label glued onto other characters, similar to how a shoe icon on a map tells you "walking trail here" without spelling out the whole word.

Now you try: look at 行 or 往 — can you find the left-footstep hiding inside?

### 朱 (1.3s)

## 朱

Picture a tree trunk with a single horizontal cut through its middle — that's the old picture of this character. Long ago, "朱" pointed to the reddish heartwood found right at the center of certain trees, like cinnabar-colored pine. That deep red-orange core gave its name to the color itself: vermilion.

Here's why the pieces fit: the character is built from 木 (tree), with a stroke marking a spot right in the trunk — like slicing an apple and noticing the core is a different color from the skin. The mark says "look here, at the middle," and what's at the middle is that rich red.

Over time, "朱" stopped meaning "tree" and started meaning just the color — a bright, warm red-orange, historically prized and even used for royal seals in some cultures.

**Now you try**: Find 木 (tree) inside 朱. Can you spot the single horizontal stroke marking the "core"? Try naming three red things you know — is any of them closer to orange, like vermilion, than to fire-engine red?

### 兔 (1.2s)

## 兔

Imagine an ancient scribe watching a rabbit dart across a field — small body, tall ears pricked up, and a stubby tail flicking as it bounds away. To draw "rabbit" quickly, the scribe sketched exactly that silhouette: a rounded body, two ears bent back, legs tucked for jumping, and a tiny tail at the back. Over thousands of years that sketch smoothed into the character we see today, 兔.

Notice the shape still whispers its story: the top curve is the ear and head, the middle is the crouching body, and the little stroke at the bottom-right is the tail — that dot is what makes 兔 different from its cousin 免 (which means "to avoid" and has no tail).

As a building block, 兔 lends its sound to other characters — for example it appears inside 菟 (a plant name), carrying its pronunciation along.

Now you try: cover the character, picture a crouching rabbit with big ears and a short tail, then draw 兔 from that image. Does your tail stroke end up in the same corner?

### 守 (1.2s)

## 守

Picture a small house with someone standing quietly inside the doorway, watching over everything within. That is the story hidden inside 守 (to guard, to keep watch).

Look at its two parts. On top sits 宀, a simple roof — it means "house" or "shelter." Underneath sits 寸, which originally pictured a hand held steady, careful, measuring — the same hand you see in words about rules and precision. Put a careful, steady hand *under* a roof, and you get someone protecting the home, watching its boundary, keeping its rules. That is why 守 means both "to guard" and "to observe rules" — guarding a place and guarding a law feel like the same job to the person who wrote this character.

Minimal formalization: $守 = 宀 \text{(house)} + 寸 \text{(steady hand)}$, a guardian keeping watch inside a shelter.

Now you try: think of someone who "guards" something in your own life — a gatekeeper, a rule, a promise. Draw a roof, then draw a hand beneath it. That's 守.

### 待 (1.2s)

## 待

Picture this: you are standing at a bus stop. The road stretches ahead of you (彳), and you are standing still, holding your place, like someone keeping watch outside a temple (寺). That is exactly what this character pictures — a person paused on a path, holding position until the right moment arrives.

Why does this feel right? Walking (彳) is the action you are *not* doing yet; the temple-like stillness (寺) is the patience you *are* doing. Put them together and you get the state of readiness-in-pause: to wait, to treat someone with care, to be about to act.

Formally, this character is a semantic-phonetic compound: 彳 supplies the meaning domain (movement, path, going somewhere), while 寺 anchors the sound and echoes a sense of composed stillness. Combined, one component tells you *where* the meaning lives, the other tells you roughly *how* it once sounded.

Now you try: can you find another character built from 彳 that also involves movement along a path, like 得 or 很?

### 株 (2.3s)

## 株

Imagine a whole forest, but you only need to talk about *one* tree — not its leaves, not the birds in it, just the tree itself, standing there as one countable thing. That's what 株 (zhū) points to: the trunk, the base, the single standing plant. Picture a little sapling stump in your yard — solid, rooted, one unit you could count on your fingers: one 株, two 株, three 株.

Here's why this feels right: in Chinese, when you count trees or plants, you don't just say "three trees" — you use a counter word that matches the *shape* of the thing, and 株 is the counter for anything tall and rooted, like a tree or a stalk of bamboo. It's like how in English we say "three loaves" of bread instead of "three breads" — the counter carries a picture of the object's form.

Beyond counting, 株 also names the rootstock in biology (a "strain") and appears in words about a shady group of people tangled together like roots — because roots spread and connect underground, out of sight.

Now you try: next time you see a tree, count it silently as "one 株" — trunk, roots, and all — instead of just "one tree."

### phrase_守株待兔 (16.1s)

## Phrase 守株待兔

A farmer was plowing his field when a rabbit ran full speed into a tree stump, broke its neck, and dropped dead. Free dinner! Thrilled, the farmer stopped farming and sat by the stump every day, waiting for another rabbit to crash into it. None ever did. His crops withered while he waited.

Why did this feel like a reasonable bet, and why did it fail? The rabbit's collision was a rare accident — nothing about the stump *caused* rabbits to run into it. Believing a lucky coincidence will repeat, and building a strategy around waiting for it, is the mistake this phrase warns against.

In probability, if an event is independent and rare, its chance of happening again does not go up just because it already happened once:
$$
P(\text{rabbit hits stump again}) = P(\text{rabbit hits stump first time})
$$
Past luck does not raise future odds — real results come from repeated effort, not repeated waiting.

Now you try: name one thing you got right by pure luck once — and one thing you get right by practicing it again and again. Which one can you count on tomorrow?
