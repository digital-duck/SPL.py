## Germany 1848 Revolutions 1848 Frankfurt Parliament

The revolutions sweeping German states in 1848 were driven by two intertwined impulses: liberal demands for constitutional governance, and fervent nationalism seeking unification. The resulting political body was the Frankfurt National Assembly (Frankfurter Nationalversammlung), which convened in May 1848. Representing delegates from various regions—including Prussia, Bavaria, and free cities—the Parliament's goal was ambitious: to draft a constitution and create a unified German state ($\text{Grossdeutschland}$ or $\text{Kleindeutschland}$). In the spring of 1849, it successfully drafted a highly progressive constitution that enshrined civil liberties, popular sovereignty, and representative government. Critically, however, the Parliament lacked any coercive military force; its authority was purely legislative.

The collapse of the movement is perhaps the most famous study in failed state formation. When the constitution was finalized, the Assembly offered the crown of a unified Germany to Frederick William IV, King of Prussia. This offer was not merely political disagreement; it represented a profound clash between revolutionary liberal idealism and entrenched monarchical legitimacy. The king's refusal—partially fueled by his own historical grievances regarding supposed slights against his royal dignity—signaled that the ultimate locus of power remained with hereditary monarchs, not with popular will or constitutional decree.

To understand this failure, consider the key variable: *Sovereignty*. In 1848, sovereignty was theoretically shifted to the people (the source of the Parliament's legitimacy). However, when a powerful existing state actor, like Prussia, refused to recognize the new legal framework, the practical reality reverted to monarchical power. The failure demonstrates that constitutional success requires both popular will *and* military implementation by key regional powers.

**Problem-Solving Application: Analyzing Systemic Failure in Statecraft**

A student might incorrectly model this event as a simple linear negotiation where "if A happens, then B follows." Instead, one must analyze the structural dependencies. The Parliament successfully created a constitutional framework (the *Law*) but failed to secure its enforcement mechanism (the *Force*). When Prussia refused to accept the crown—a symbolic act that carried overwhelming real-world weight—it signaled that the revolutionary system lacked the critical link between legal legitimacy and executive power. The subsequent military crackdown by Prussian forces effectively dissolved the constitutional experiment, proving that even a perfectly drafted constitution is merely aspirational without the consent of the existing deep state powers.

## Germany 1848 Revolutions 1848 Frankfurt Parliament

**定义**：1848年革命是席卷欧洲（包括德意志诸邦）的一系列自由主义与民族主义起义。在德意志,起义者要求立宪政府、出版自由、以及打破38个邦国分立的局面,建立统一的德意志民族国家。1848年5月,来自各邦的代表在法兰克福圣保罗教堂召开"法兰克福国民议会"(Frankfurter Nationalversammlung),这是德意志历史上第一个经选举产生的全国性议会,共有约830名代表,多为律师、教授、官员等中产阶级知识分子,因此常被称为"教授议会"(Professorenparlament)。

**具体事例**：法兰克福议会用近一年时间辩论并于1849年3月完成一部宪法草案,规定建立君主立宪制的统一德意志帝国,并将皇冠授予普鲁士国王腓特烈·威廉四世。然而,腓特烈·威廉四世拒绝接受这顶"从阴沟里捡来的皇冠"(他本人的说法),因为这顶皇冠是由民选议会而非其他世袭君主授予,违背了他信奉的"神授王权"原则。他的拒绝直接导致议会失去实际权威:温和派代表陆续退出,残余的激进派代表迁往斯图加特,最终在1849年6月被符腾堡军队武力驱散。同年,巴登和普法尔茨爆发的支持宪法的武装起义也被普鲁士军队镇压。

**问题解决应用**：分析法兰克福议会失败的原因,需要区分结构性因素与偶然性因素。结构性因素包括:议会没有军队或税收权力,其法令的执行完全依赖各邦君主的配合;议会内部也在"大德意志"(包含奥地利)与"小德意志"(以普鲁士为核心、排除奥地利)两条统一路线之间长期分裂,削弱了谈判筹码。偶然性因素则是腓特烈·威廉四世个人的保守立场——若换一位更务实的君主,历史进程可能不同。这一案例常被用作历史论证题的经典材料:统一进程最终由1862年后俾斯麦以"铁血政策"、通过三场战争(普丹战争、普奥战争、普法战争)自上而下实现,而非1848年自下而上的议会路径完成,这一对比也解释了后来德意志帝国宪法中议会权力薄弱、军事与君主权力强大的制度基因。

## Germany 1862 Bismarck Becomes Prussian Minister President

**Definition:**
Following the failed revolutions of 1848 and the subsequent collapse of revolutionary liberal movements in German states, Prussia faced deep political instability. The monarchy struggled to balance traditional aristocratic power with burgeoning industrial capitalism and demands for constitutional reform. Into this vacuum stepped Otto von Bismarck, a pragmatic statesman who rejected the ideals of liberal unity championed by the Frankfurt Parliament. Instead, he embraced *Realpolitik*—a policy centered on practical considerations of state power rather than ethical or ideological principles. His strategy, which became known as "Blood and Iron," dictated that Germany could not be unified through parliamentary debate or popular consensus; it required decisive military force wielded by a strong central government (Prussia) to overcome entrenched regional loyalties and political opposition.

**Worked example:**
When Bismarck took ministerial power in 1862, he immediately faced constitutional resistance from the conservative Prussian Parliament, which sought to control state spending. The parliamentarians favored fiscal restraint, while Bismarck demanded massive funds to rebuild and modernize the Prussian army, viewing military strength as the singular guarantor of national ambition. When his funding requests were repeatedly blocked by the legislature—an effort that paralyzed the government for years—Bismarck’s actions demonstrated his core philosophy: constitutional debate was secondary to strategic necessity. Rather than compromising on troop levels or modernization, he governed through royal decree and direct military action, effectively bypassing the institutional checks and balances of the state in favor of building a professional, modern war machine capable of projecting power across German borders.

**Problem-solving application:**
The core problem Bismarck solved was how to achieve German unity—an ambition that had failed repeatedly since 1848—without relying on the cooperation of all major German states (like Austria and Bavaria), which retained strong local loyalties. His solution required a calculated sequence of conflict and diplomatic isolation. Instead of trying to build a grand, multi-state federation immediately, Bismarck focused on solidifying Prussian dominance first. He used military force not merely as an end goal, but as a tool for *political* consolidation. For instance, by initiating the conflicts with Denmark (1864) and then Austria (1866), he did not just fight wars; he forced smaller German states to choose sides and eliminated existing obstacles to Prussian hegemony. This process of controlled conflict demonstrated that military strength was the ultimate arbitrator in the complex geopolitical landscape of Central Europe, effectively replacing idealistic constitutionalism with the undeniable power of the army-state.
