### 木 (14.2s)

## 木

Picture a tree: a trunk anchored in soil, branches reaching upward. The character 木 captures this economy perfectly—just a few strokes, but they hold a whole visual idea. You've likely spotted it before in words like 树 (tree) or 林 (forest). But 木 does more than just picture things; it *builds* meaning.

When 木 sits inside a larger character, it often signals "something to do with trees or plants." The character 林 is a perfect example:

$$\underbrace{木}{tree} + \underbrace{木}{tree} = \underbrace{林}{forest/grove}$$

Two trees make a grove—the meaning literally stacks. Similarly, 休 (to rest) combines 人 (person) leaning against 木 (tree), picturing someone pausing for shade.

But here's where it gets interesting: 木 doesn't just *mean* tree. It can also *sound* like wood. In the character 沐 (to bathe), 木 contributes its sound—the character is pronounced roughly "mù," borrowing from 木's own sound, even though 沐's primary meaning comes from the water radical 氵. **One brick, two jobs**: 木 carries both meaning *and* sound across different characters.

This is how Chinese characters are built—not from random strokes, but from reusable components that know how to do double duty. Learning to spot 木 in compounds helps you decode unfamiliar characters and see how writers stack meaning economically.

**Practice**: Look at the character 橡 (rubber tree). Can you identify the 木 radical? What do you think 木 contributes to 橡's meaning? (Hint: What does the word describe?)

### 女 (10.7s)

## 女

You might guess that 女 started exactly where it looks like it did: someone drew a picture of a woman in a kneeling position, and over time, the drawing got more stylized until it became the neat character you see today. That's basically right. In the earliest Chinese oracle-bone script (bone carvings from 1200 BCE), 女 literally shows a figure on their knees, arms folded. As calligraphers refined the character across dynasties, those curves and angles simplified into the four strokes we write now: a vertical line down the left, a horizontal line at the top, and two curved strokes filling the right side.

But here's where 女 becomes really interesting: it's not just a picture frozen in time. It's a **radical**—a building block that shows up inside hundreds of other characters to signal their meaning. Whenever you see 女 nested inside another character, you're looking at a clue. That character probably has something to do with women, family, relationships, or traits connected to those ideas.

For example, 好 (good) combines 女 (woman) with 子 (child) to originally mean "a woman with a child"—the ancient idea that this was fundamentally good. The character 妈 (mother) is 女 plus a sound-marker. And 姐 (older sister), 妹 (younger sister), 婚 (marriage)—they all carry 女 to tell you: "This word is about women or family roles."

This pattern works like a code. When Chinese speakers encounter a new character they've never seen, the radical gives them a head start: "This has something to do with this category of meaning."

### 人 (21.8s)

## 人

**What you see**: The character 人 looks like a simple figure—two strokes that lean toward each other, like legs taking a step. When you first learn it, that's the whole idea: it's the picture of a person, walking or standing. Straightforward, right?

**How it builds other characters**: But 人 becomes much more powerful once you realize it's also a *building block*. This character shows up inside hundreds of other characters in the Chinese writing system, and every time it does, it carries a hint of meaning: something to do with people, actions people take, or states people are in. Look at these examples:

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = 众 \text{ (crowd)}$$

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 休 \text{ (rest)}$$

The first shows three people together making a crowd. The second shows a person leaning against a tree—rest. When 人 appears on the *left side* of a character (as the "people radical"), it usually signals: "this has to do with humans or human action."

**The pattern**: Chinese characters aren't random pictures—they're *built from smaller meaningful pieces*. 人 is one of the most common of these pieces. Recognizing where 人 hides inside a character gives you a foothold into what that character might mean, even if you've never seen it before.

### 子 (50.9s)

## 子

Imagine a picture of a baby wrapped snugly in blankets — that's the original idea behind 子 (zǐ), the character for "child" or "son." When you see 子 in a character, you're often looking at that core image: something small, dependent, new.

But 子 does more than just name children. It's also a *building block*. When 子 appears inside a larger character, it carries meaning forward. Look at how two characters combine:

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = \text{好 (good)}$$

This composition shows something important: a mother with a child suggests goodness and completeness. That connection between caring and goodness stuck around in the character system.

**The pattern**: When you see 子 in a character, ask two questions: *Is this about children or descendants?* Or *Is this about something small, developing, or incomplete?* These core meanings branch out through the entire character system.

Additionally, 子 works as a *suffix* in spoken Chinese — it attaches to nouns to shape them. 孩子 (háizi, "child"), 女子 (nǚzǐ, "woman"), and even 故事 (gùshì, "story") all carry 子 as a marker meaning: "This is a *thing*, an entity, something you can point to and count."

### 宀 (56.2s)

## 宀

When you first see the character 宀, it looks like a roof or canopy — a flat line on top with two short walls beneath it. That's not just clever design; it's the radical's whole job. In Chinese, 宀 is called the "roof radical," and it's placed over other components to suggest that whatever sits underneath is sheltered, protected, or belongs in one place. Think of it as drawing a roof directly over an idea.

The radical works by *composition*: you take 宀 and add a component beneath it to build new meanings. For example:

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字 \text{ (character, literally: child under roof)}$$

When 宀 is placed over something, you're being told: "This thing is fixed, settled, or belongs to a home or community." The roof doesn't change meaning; it *frames* the component underneath by putting it in a protected or domestic context. This pattern repeats across dozens of characters.

Here's the pattern to remember: **whenever you encounter 宀, expect the character's meaning to connect to shelter, family, residence, or settlement.** It's like a visual cue that says, "Look for a home-related or containment idea here."

### 马 (40.5s)

## 马

You've figured out that 马 looks like a horse and means "horse"—good intuition. But 马 is remarkably busy. Sometimes it shows up in other characters and lends its *sound* instead of its meaning.

Look at 妈 (mother). It contains 马, but it has nothing to do with horses. Instead, it's using 马 as a **phonetic component**—a sound-lender. Here's how it's built:

$$\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{马}_{sound: mā} = \text{妈 (mother)}$$

The radical (女, woman) tells you the character is related to people or family. The 马 tells you how to pronounce it—the character "horse" sounds like "mā," which is exactly the sound this word needs. The horse meaning disappears entirely; only the pronunciation matters.

This reveals something important about how Chinese characters work: they're often built from two layers of information. One component tells you the sound; another gives you a hint about meaning. 马 is particularly useful as a sound-lender because "ma" is a common sound in Chinese, so it gets recycled across many characters. A student seeing 马 for the first time might guess "horse," but a student learning character construction recognizes it as a phonetic component with a specific sound-lending job.

### 从 (23.2s)

## 从

**The picture: Two people, one behind the other**

When you see 从, you're looking at the simplest idea in motion. Imagine a path: one person walks forward, and another person walks behind, following along. That's exactly what the character shows—not just the idea of following, but the *picture of it happening*. The first person moves, and the second person moves in their wake. This is how 从 works as a verb: to follow, to go along with, to join in.

**How the character is built**

The genius of 从 is that you can see its meaning. Chinese characters often work this way—they're built from smaller pieces that combine to create new meaning:

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = 从$$

The first person (人) stands in front; the second person (人) follows behind. When you arrange these simple pieces, the meaning emerges from their relationship. This compositional method is one of the main ways Chinese characters carry meaning. Not every character works this way—some carry sound clues, others trace back to older pictures—but many do, and learning to see the building blocks helps you guess meaning even when you meet an unfamiliar character.

**The pattern: Composition reveals relationship**

When a character combines two of the same piece, pay attention. Two people → togetherness or following. Two trees (木 + 木 = 林) → a forest. Three trees (木 + 木 + 木 = 森) → even denser. The pattern is consistent: repetition often means something bigger, multiplied, or intensified. Here, two people specifically capture one-after-another movement.

### 口 (33.7s)

## 口

Look at the character 口 — it's a simple square, almost like a box drawn from above. What's the main idea? An **opening**. Just as your mouth is an opening in your face, this character represents any kind of opening: literal mouths, the things mouths do (speak, eat), and even the way we mark a question (an opening for an answer).

The power of 口 comes from how it *builds* the meaning of other characters. Take the verb "to speak" — 说 — and you'll find 口 sitting right there on the left side. Why? Because speaking comes out of your mouth. Or look at 吃 (to eat): again, 口 appears, because eating is something a mouth does. The character 问 (to ask) combines 口 with another element — because asking opens a conversation where both people listen and speak.

Here's the pattern: whenever 口 appears in a character, especially when paired with elements about sounds, words, or actions, the meaning usually involves the mouth or communication. The character isn't just a picture; it's a **building block** that carries meaning when combined with other pieces. Think of it like how the prefix "un-" in English tells you something is reversed or negated — 口 tells you something relates to the mouth, opening, or the act of communication.

### 日 (25.8s)

## 日

You already know the sun gives us light and warmth, and that ancient writers noticed things—rocks, animals, the sky itself. When Chinese writers needed a symbol for "sun" or "day," they didn't grab a random shape. They drew what they saw: a circle, the sun's disk in the sky, with a mark in the middle to show its brightness. But brushes don't make perfect circles. Hold a brush and try—you get squared corners. So the sun became a square with a dot: **日**.

The structure here is elegant:

$$\underbrace{\text{circle}}_{\text{sun's shape}} + \underbrace{\text{center mark}}_{\text{inner brightness}} = \text{日}$$

This teaches us how Chinese writing thinks. Rather than copying nature perfectly, the character *contains* the idea by holding the sun's circular concept in a brushable square, with that inner mark showing the sun's concentrated brightness—what makes everything else visible.

Once writers had 日, they built from it. Add a moving element and it can mean "speak"—the sun's heat makes things *move*. Add warmth-related pieces and you get family words sharing that core brightness idea. This is what makes 日 powerful: it's not just a picture, it's a *piece* that carries meaning into bigger concepts, a building block for ideas about light, time, and energy.

### 月 (15.8s)

## 月

Remember how 月 started as a picture of a crescent moon? The shape is simple and striking — a curved line or small circle enclosed in a rectangle. Early Chinese writers saw the moon hanging in the night sky and drew what they saw. Over time, the brushstrokes became more standardized, but the idea remained: 月 = moon, a standalone character with its own meaning.

Here's where it gets interesting. The same shape that represents "moon" was repurposed for something completely different: a **radical**. A radical is a building block—a smaller component that repeats across many characters to hint at their meaning. 月 became the radical for flesh, body, and bodily things.

When 月 appears inside another character (often on the left side, as a vertical rectangle rather than a perfect crescent), it usually signals "this has something to do with the body." Look at 肉 (flesh/meat) — that's 月 with an extra stroke added. Or 肩 (shoulder) — 月 combined with another component. Even 脑 (brain) uses 月 because a brain is, after all, part of the human body.

This is the double life of 月: standing alone, it's a picture of the moon in the night sky. Nested inside other characters, it becomes a signpost pointing toward anything physical or bodily. **One shape; two ancestries; two jobs.**

This pattern — where a single shape means one thing independently but hints at something different when embedded in a larger character — repeats throughout Chinese. It's efficient and elegant: the same visual element works hard, carrying multiple meanings depending on context.

### 林 (13.3s)

## 林

You've learned that 木 (mù) means a single tree or piece of wood — it's the basic building block. But what if you want to say there's not just one tree, but a *collection* of trees? Chinese has an elegant answer: do it again. Add another 木, and you get 林 (lín), which means a small grove or woods.

This is one of the oldest rules in Chinese character-building, and it works exactly like LEGO blocks. When you stack the same piece twice, you don't get "tree-tree" as a description — you get a new word that captures a whole new idea. One tree sitting alone is 木. But trees together, creating shade and shelter and a place animals live, that's 林. The repetition *transforms* the meaning.

Here's the structure:

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林 \text{ (woods)}$$

This pattern — repeating a simple character to intensify or expand its meaning — is one of the most honest rules you'll find in written Chinese. It shows you how language grows: not by inventing completely new marks, but by combining what you already have in clever ways. The repetition rule says, "If you want to describe *more* of something, or a *collection* of it, use the building block multiple times."

### 水 (19.7s)

## 水

When you first see the character 水, it looks like someone drew flowing lines—and that's exactly what it is! In ancient Chinese writing, people literally sketched what they saw: wavy lines to show water moving. Over centuries, those squiggly sketches became the neat strokes we write today. The character 水 still carries that original meaning: water.

But here's where it gets clever. As Chinese grew, people needed new characters for all the liquids and water-related ideas—like rain, tears, juice, or sweat. Rather than invent brand-new drawings from scratch every time, they took 水 and squeezed it into a compact form: 氵 (three dots arranged like drops). This short version is called a **radical**, and it's a building block.

When you see 氵 on the left side of a character, it's a visual hint: "This word has something to do with water or liquid." For example, 湖 (lake) has 氵 on the left because a lake is full of water. Same with 泪 (tears) or 油 (oil). The pattern is consistent—氵 acts like a label.

This is how Chinese characters work: they're not random squiggles. They follow a **compositional rule**: a simple radical (like 氵 for liquids) combines with other parts to build meaning. You already know the picture part; now you add context using the structure.

### 休 (14.5s)

## 休

When you see 休, picture exactly what the character means: a person taking a break, resting against a tree. It's not abstract; it's a small scene frozen into a symbol.

This is where Chinese characters reveal their genius. Instead of inventing a completely new symbol for "rest," the language-makers took two pictures you already know—a person (人) and a tree (木)—and put them side by side. The combination creates a new meaning that grows naturally from the two pieces:

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = \text{休 (rest)}$$

Here's the insight: meanings in Chinese characters aren't random. When you see a character, you can often decode it by looking at its building blocks. If you know what 人 and 木 mean on their own, you can make an educated guess about 休 even if you've never seen it before. The character is practically showing you the definition.

This pattern doesn't work for every character—some are too old or too specialized to decode this way—but it works often enough that learning to "read" the parts can unlock entire groups of related words. Once you understand 休, you're ready to recognize other characters with 木 in them, like 林 (forest) or 棋 (game/chess). You start to see that the language isn't just giving you symbols to memorize; it's giving you clues to unlock meanings.

### 众 (16.6s)

## 众

You've already met the pattern: take one simple thing, repeat it three times, and suddenly you're talking about the whole category. We saw this with 森 (forest): one tree becomes three trees. Now let's watch it work on people.

The character 众 represents the concept of "crowd"—not just any collection of people, but that special feeling when many voices become one. It's built exactly like 森:

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = \text{众 (crowd)}$$

Here's why this composition matters: one 人 (person) is an individual with their own ideas and choices. Two people together start to create something new—conversation, agreement, disagreement. But three or more? That's where the *crowd* really begins. It's the point where individual voices start to blend into something bigger than themselves. The character captures this shift.

This tripling rule is surprisingly powerful in Chinese. It's not just "more and more." It's specifically "enough to matter"—the threshold where quantity becomes quality. A few trees don't make a forest; three does. A few people don't make a crowd; three does. The number three acts like a signal that says "this is now a whole new thing."

### 吗 (10.8s)

## 吗

You already know that in English, we often signal a question by changing word order ("You like pizza?" vs. "You like pizza.") or by using a question mark. Chinese has a different system—it uses *particles*, small words that attach to the end of a sentence to show that you're asking. The question particle **吗** (ma) is one of the most common.

But here's something interesting: you can actually see how this particle was built, right in its shape.

$$
\underbrace{口}_{mouth/\text{spoken}} + \underbrace{马}_{ma} = 吗
$$

The left side, 口, is the "mouth" radical—a picture of speech happening. The right side is 马 (ma), which is pronounced the same way but means "horse." When you combine them, you get 吗: *the spoken sound* ma *acting as a question marker*. This composition isn't random; it tells you that this particle's job is to speak a question aloud.

**How it works**: You take any statement and add 吗 to the end. That's it. No word order change, no special grammar—just attach 吗 and you've turned it into a yes-or-no question.

For example:
- 你喜欢比萨。(You like pizza. — statement)
- 你喜欢比萨吗？(You like pizza? — question)

The 吗 sits at the very end, after the verb and object, and it signals to the listener: "I'm asking you to confirm this." Once you notice this pattern, you'll see it everywhere in Chinese conversation—it's the workhorse of quick questions.

### 好 (16.5s)

## 好

You already know what "good" means in English. But let's ask a different question: *how would you draw* "good" if you had to invent a picture for it?

Three thousand years ago, Chinese speakers faced exactly that problem. They were building a written language from pictures, and they had to decide: what does "good" *look* like? Their answer reveals something deep about what they valued.

The character 好 is built from two pictures joined together. The left side, 女, is the picture of a woman. The right side, 子, is the picture of a child. Together, they create a single idea:

$$\underbrace{女}_{woman} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = 好 \text{ (good)}$$

Notice what this says without words: goodness, in this ancient view, was *a woman with a child*—safety, family, care, continuity. The picture isn't saying women or children are good; it's saying that *this image*—a caregiver and a young person together—represents wellbeing. It's a snapshot of what made life worth living.

This way of building meaning is called *composition*, and it's one of the oldest tricks in Chinese writing. Instead of inventing a completely new picture each time, writers combined smaller, simpler pictures to build bigger ideas. A picture of a tree was "tree"; three trees together meant "forest." A picture of a woman became part of words for kindness, safety, and love.

When you learn Chinese characters, you're not just learning symbols—you're learning how people *thought* about the world. Every combination tells a story.

### 妈 (16.8s)

## 妈

You already know that Chinese characters tell stories in their shapes. But how do they work when you're trying to describe someone really important—like your mom? This is where Chinese shows off a clever trick: combining a piece that means something with a piece that sounds like something.

Take a look at how 妈 is built:

$$\underbrace{女}_{female} + \underbrace{马}_{sounds~like~ma} = 妈~(mother)$$

Here's what's happening. The left side, 女 (nǚ, "female person"), is a *semantic component*—it tells you the character is related to women or family. The right side, 马 (mǎ, "horse"), isn't there because mothers are horses! It's there because the sound "ma" is what we say when we call our mothers. In Chinese, this right side is a *phonetic component*—it gives you a clue about pronunciation.

This is one of the most powerful patterns in Chinese. Instead of drawing something that looks like a mom (which would be tricky!), the character system lets you combine a meaning-clue with a sound-clue. Once you know this pattern, you can guess the meaning *and* sound of characters you've never seen before.

You'll notice 女 shows up in lots of family words: 姐 (sister), 妹 (younger sister), 妇 (woman). And 马 appears in other characters too, always bringing its "ma" sound along. Learning these pieces is like collecting building blocks—the more you gather, the more characters you can unlock.

### 字 (35.1s)

## 字

When Chinese speakers say 字 (zì)—"character"—they're using a picture that tells the whole story. The character itself shows 子 (a child) sitting under 宀 (a roof). Why? Because characters are born and raised, just like children. Each new character grows from older, simpler pieces, combined and refined over time. The writing system named itself with this image: *I am made of building blocks, and so are my words.*

This "birth" metaphor isn't just poetic—it describes how Chinese characters actually work. Most characters aren't random marks; they're **compositions of smaller, reusable pieces called radicals**. When you learn a new character, you're not memorizing an isolated shape. You're discovering how its component pieces fit together to hint at meaning or sound.

Take the structure of 字 itself:

$$\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{子}_{child} = 字$$

The roof doesn't mean "house"—it's a *semantic hint* that something is contained or protected. The child is a hint about meaning. Together, they create something new.

Here's the pattern: **A character is a miniature puzzle where each piece carries meaning, and the whole tells a story.**

This is how you build vocabulary strategically. When you meet a new character, ask: *What pieces is it made from? What does each piece mean?* Even if you don't know the full character yet, the pieces give you clues.

### 安 (18.9s)

## 安

You probably know that 安 (ān) means "peace" or "safe." But where does that meaning come from? The character itself tells a story: it shows a **woman under a roof** — and that simple picture captures the whole idea.

Think about it: if you're home, sheltered and secure under your own roof, you feel at peace. You're not worried or rushing. You're settled. That's exactly what 安 represents. The character combines two elements:

$$\underbrace{宀}_{roof} + \underbrace{女}_{woman} = 安$$

The roof radical (宀) appears in many characters about home and shelter — 宫 (palace), 宗 (ancestor, clan home), 宝 (treasure kept safe at home). The woman radical (女) by itself can mean "female," but here it's used to show a person in that sheltered space. Together, they create a powerful visual metaphor: safety comes from having a secure place to belong.

You can see this meaning in many compound words. 平安 (píng-ān) — "level-peace" — means "safe and sound," the kind of peace where everything is stable. 安全 (ān-quán) — "peace-complete" — means "safety and security." Even 安定 (ān-dìng) means "stable and settled." All of them build on this core idea of being at peace because you have a safe, settled place.

### 明 (41.6s)

## 明

Imagine standing at twilight between day and night. On one side, the sun provides warmth and visibility; on the other, the moon offers its gentler glow. Together, they create a fuller picture of illumination. This is the root idea behind 明 (míng)—*bright* or *light*.

The character 明 is built from two simpler characters placed side by side:

$$\underbrace{日}_{sun/day} + \underbrace{月}_{moon} = 明$$

This isn't just a picture; it's a principle. The Chinese writing system often builds meaning by combining characters that represent core ideas. Here, the character for sun (日, which also means "day") and moon (月) sit together to create the idea of brightness itself. The visual arrangement—putting light sources next to each other—mirrors the concept: to be truly bright is to gather all available light.

In Chinese, when you combine characters this way, you're not just adding images; you're building meaning through relationship. 明 teaches us that brightness comes from combining multiple light sources. This same principle appears in other characters: 林 (forest) combines three 木 (tree) characters, showing how individual trees become something richer when gathered together. **Related elements placed together create a new, richer meaning.**

The structure of the character often hints at the concept's structure in the world. Once you notice how 日 and 月 combine, you start seeing this pattern everywhere—characters arrange their pieces to show how real things relate and build on each other.

**Try this:** Compare 明 (bright) and 林 (forest). In both cases, something becomes richer or more powerful by combining similar elements. What does the character design tell us about what makes each of these concepts special? Can you think of other ideas in nature or your own life that become something entirely different and more powerful when you bring similar things together?

### 森 (15.6s)

## 森

You probably know that 森 means "forest" and it's built from three trees. But here's what makes Chinese characters special: the character's *structure* actually *shows* you what it means. Let's see how.

Start with 木 (wood, or tree). One character, one tree. Now, what if you put two trees together? You get 林 (grove or small forest) — two 木 side by side. The meaning grows: more trees, denser vegetation. Do it again. Three trees stacked? You get 森 (forest). The full, thick forest.

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \text{ (forest)}$$

This isn't random. The character literally *shows* you quantity and density through repetition. One tree = 木. Many trees close together = 林. A thick, deep forest = 森. The more brushstrokes you add, the more crowded and full the image becomes — and so does the meaning.

Here's the pattern: **In Chinese, some characters build meaning by repeating a simpler part.** It's like a visual equation. You can often *guess* what a complex character means if you recognize what it's built from. The structure teaches you the logic.

Now, think about this: We've seen 木→林→森. What do you think a character made from two 火 (fire) might mean? Try to sketch it or describe what the image and meaning might suggest.

### 沐 (12.9s)

## 沐

You've been noticing a pattern: the radicals we've seen so far do double duty—they give meaning *and* often hint at the character's sound through a picture. Water means "liquid things," and it sounds like it looks. Wood means "plants," and you can visualize it. But 沐 is where that pattern breaks. Here, 水 (water) carries all the meaning—the character is about washing, about liquid, about cleansing. Then 木 (wood) shows up... except it's not there to mean "wood" at all. It's there *only* for its sound: "mu."

This is a turning point. In earlier characters, the sound component was also a picture. Now we're learning that 木 can do something different—it can be purely a sound marker, a phonetic helper that has nothing to do with what the character actually means. It's like learning that in English, a letter in a word doesn't always tell you about meaning; sometimes it's just the sound you need.

The structure is stark and elegant:

$$\underbrace{水}_{meaning:\text{ water, wash}} + \underbrace{木}_{sound:\text{ mu}} = \text{沐}$$

This split—meaning and sound completely separate—is how many Chinese characters are actually built. You can now start spotting characters where the radical says "what it's about" and another component says "how to pronounce it."

**Your turn:** Think of three other characters you've already learned. For each one, can you identify which part gives the *meaning* and which part hints at the *sound*? You might find that not every character does both clearly—some are trickier than others.

### phonetic_borrowing (24.2s)

## Phonetic Borrowing

You've already seen how a single piece can do double duty in Chinese characters. Think of it like this: once you know how to pronounce 木 (mù, "wood"), you've learned something useful that other characters can borrow. When you see 沐 (also pronounced mù), the wood isn't there because the word is about wood—it's there to lend its sound. That's phonetic borrowing.

Here's the idea: when a character maker wanted a new word with a specific sound, they would pick a character they knew how to say and use it inside a new character. The borrowed piece becomes a kind of pronunciation stamp. Meanwhile, another piece—the form piece—hints at the meaning. You get the sound from one piece and the meaning from the other.

The principle works like this: a phonetic piece (声旁, "sound side") pairs with a meaning piece to create a new character. The sound piece is the gift; the meaning piece is the context.

Look at 马 (mǎ, "horse"). This character lends its sound to:
- 妈 (mā, "mother")
- 吗 (ma, question particle)

Neither word is actually about horses, but both borrow that *ma-* sound. The sound stays; the meaning changes based on the form piece paired with it. This is the efficiency of the system: instead of inventing brand-new shapes for every new word, you recycle pronunciations you've already learned.

This principle solved a real problem. Chinese writers could express new ideas and new words without starting from scratch every time. A single pronunciation could serve multiple meanings, each bundled with its own meaning hint.
