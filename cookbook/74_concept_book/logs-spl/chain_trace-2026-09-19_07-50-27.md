### acoustic_wave (13.4s)

## Acoustic Wave

An acoustic wave is a longitudinal pressure wave: a disturbance in which molecules of a medium (air, water, steel) oscillate back and forth *parallel* to the direction the wave travels, creating alternating zones of compression (molecules pushed together, higher pressure) and rarefaction (molecules spread apart, lower pressure). No matter is transported net-distance; only energy and momentum propagate outward from the source, the way a pulse travels down a slinky without any single coil migrating far from its rest position.

The wave's speed depends on the medium's stiffness (bulk modulus $B$) and inertia (density $\rho$):

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}}
$$

This explains why sound travels faster in water (~1480 m/s) than in air (~343 m/s at 20°C): water is far stiffer, and that stiffness advantage outweighs its higher density. For an ideal gas, this reduces to $v = \sqrt{\gamma R T / M}$, showing that sound speed in air rises with temperature but does not depend on pressure alone — a common point of confusion.

**Worked example.** A submarine sonar pulse at 1480 m/s needs 0.6 s to return an echo from the seafloor. Since the pulse travels down and back, the one-way distance is
$$
d = \frac{v t}{2} = \frac{1480 \times 0.6}{2} = 444 \text{ m}.
$$

**Problem-solving application.** Once you know $v = f\lambda$, acoustic wave problems become an exercise in relating frequency, wavelength, and medium properties. Suppose a 500 Hz tone travels from air (343 m/s) into water (1480 m/s). Because frequency is set by the source and stays fixed across the boundary, wavelength must change: $\lambda_{\text{air}} = 343/500 = 0.686$ m, while $\lambda_{\text{water}} = 1480/500 = 2.96$ m — the wave stretches out because the medium is stiffer. This same reasoning underlies ultrasound imaging (choosing wavelengths short enough to resolve tissue structures), architectural acoustics (predicting how wavelength compares to room dimensions to avoid standing-wave resonances), and noise engineering (low frequencies have long wavelengths and diffract around obstacles more easily than high frequencies, which is why bass travels through walls better than treble). In each case, the physics reduces to tracking how $v$, $f$, and $\lambda$ trade off as the wave moves through different materials or interacts with boundaries.

### frequency (10.9s)

## Frequency

Frequency measures how rapidly a sound wave cycles between compression and rarefaction at a fixed point in space, expressed in Hertz (Hz), where $1\text{ Hz} = 1$ cycle per second. As a sound wave propagates through a medium, molecules oscillate back and forth around their equilibrium positions, and frequency counts how many complete oscillations — one full compression plus one full rarefaction — occur at a given location each second.

Frequency is intrinsically tied to period, the time required for one complete cycle. If $T$ denotes the period in seconds, then frequency $f$ is its reciprocal:

$$f = \frac{1}{T}$$

This inverse relationship is not a convenience but a mathematical necessity: if a wave repeats every $T$ seconds, then in one second it must complete exactly $1/T$ cycles. A sound wave with a period of $T = 0.002\text{ s}$ (2 milliseconds) therefore has frequency $f = 1/0.002 = 500\text{ Hz}$ — the pitch of a note close to a B4 on a piano.

Frequency also connects to wave speed $v$ and wavelength $\lambda$ (the physical distance between successive compressions) through the universal wave equation:

$$v = f\lambda$$

Because sound travels at a fixed speed through a given medium (approximately $343\text{ m/s}$ in air at room temperature), frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional: higher-frequency sounds have shorter wavelengths. This lets you solve for any one quantity given the other two — a recurring problem-solving pattern in acoustics.

**Worked example.** A tuning fork produces a tone with wavelength $\lambda = 0.784\text{ m}$ in air, where $v = 343\text{ m/s}$. Find its frequency.

Rearranging $v = f\lambda$ gives $f = v/\lambda = 343 / 0.784 \approx 437.5\text{ Hz}$ — close to the standard concert pitch A4 (440 Hz).

**Application.** Because $v = f\lambda$ holds for any wave speed in any medium, the same equation lets you predict how frequency must change if a sound wave passes from air into water, where $v \approx 1480\text{ m/s}$. If frequency is fixed by the source (say $f = 500\text{ Hz}$), the wavelength in water becomes $\lambda = v/f = 1480/500 = 2.96\text{ m}$ — nearly 5 times longer than in air, since the medium's speed changed while the source's oscillation rate did not.

### amplitude (12.9s)

## Amplitude

Amplitude measures how far a sound wave's air pressure swings away from normal atmospheric pressure — the size of the push (compression) and pull (rarefaction) as the wave passes. Quantitatively, it is the maximum deviation from equilibrium pressure, expressed in pascals (Pa). Because the range of pressures the human ear can detect spans many orders of magnitude — from about $2 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa (threshold of hearing) to over $20$ Pa (threshold of pain) — amplitude is usually reported on a logarithmic scale as sound pressure level (SPL), in decibels:

$$
L_p = 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right) \text{ dB}
$$

where $p$ is the measured pressure amplitude and $p_0 = 2 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa is the reference threshold of human hearing. The factor of 20 (rather than 10) appears because sound *intensity* is proportional to the *square* of pressure amplitude, and the decibel is defined as a ratio of intensities; squaring $p/p_0$ inside a base-10 log multiplies the coefficient by 2.

**Worked example.** Suppose a vacuum cleaner produces a pressure amplitude of $p = 0.2$ Pa. Its SPL is:

$$
L_p = 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{0.2}{2 \times 10^{-5}}\right) = 20 \log_{10}(10{,}000) = 20 \times 4 = 80 \text{ dB}
$$

This matches the commonly cited value for vacuum cleaner noise.

**Problem-solving application.** The logarithmic scale means decibel differences correspond to multiplicative pressure ratios, not additive ones — a fact essential for noise-exposure calculations. Suppose a city ordinance caps street noise at 70 dB, and a jackhammer alone measures 90 dB. How much must the pressure amplitude be reduced to meet the limit?

Set up the ratio of the two SPLs:

$$
90 - 70 = 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right) \implies \log_{10}\left(\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right) = 1 \implies \frac{p_1}{p_2} = 10
$$

So the pressure amplitude must drop by a factor of 10 — not by "20 dB worth of pressure" in any linear sense — to bring the sound down to the legal limit. This kind of calculation, converting between decibel differences and pressure or intensity ratios, is the standard skill tested when amplitude appears in acoustics and audio engineering problems.

### periodic_aperiodic_sound (22.5s)

## Periodic Aperiodic Sound

Every sound is a pressure wave, and how it repeats over time determines whether we hear a pitch or noise. A **periodic** sound has a waveform that repeats identically over a fixed interval $T$, the period, satisfying $p(t + T) = p(t)$ for all $t$. The reciprocal $f = 1/T$ gives the fundamental frequency, which the ear interprets as pitch. An **aperiodic** sound has no such repeating pattern — its waveform never settles into a cycle — and is perceived as noise, such as static, wind, or a cymbal crash.

Crucially, real periodic sounds are rarely pure sine waves. A vibrating string or air column produces a **harmonic series**: a fundamental frequency $f$ plus integer multiples $2f, 3f, 4f, \dots$, all present simultaneously with different amplitudes. Fourier's theorem guarantees that any periodic waveform, no matter how complex its shape, can be decomposed into a sum of sine waves at these harmonic frequencies:

$$
p(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \sin(2\pi n f t + \phi_n)
$$

Here $A_n$ is the amplitude and $\phi_n$ the phase of the $n$th harmonic. This is why a violin and a flute playing the same fundamental frequency $f$ sound different — they share the same pitch but differ in the relative amplitudes $A_n$ of their harmonics, a property called timbre.

**Worked example**: A guitar string tuned to concert A vibrates at $f = 440\text{ Hz}$. Its harmonic series includes components at $440, 880, 1320, 1760, \dots$ Hz. If you record the string and compute its Fourier transform, you'll see sharp spikes exactly at these integer multiples — evidence of periodicity. Compare this to a snare drum hit: its Fourier transform shows energy spread continuously across many frequencies with no clean spikes, because the waveform never repeats. That spectral difference — discrete harmonic spikes versus a continuous smear — is the mathematical signature distinguishing periodic from aperiodic sound.

**Problem-solving application**: Given a recorded waveform, you can classify it as periodic or aperiodic by computing its Fourier transform and inspecting the resulting spectrum. If the energy concentrates into sharp, evenly spaced spikes at integer multiples of some frequency $f$, the sound is periodic with fundamental $f$ — this is exactly the harmonic-series signature from the worked example above. If the energy instead spreads continuously across the spectrum with no such spacing, the sound is aperiodic. This spectral test is the basis for pitch-detection algorithms in music software: the software searches the spectrum for the lowest frequency whose harmonic spikes best explain the observed peaks, and reports that frequency as the detected pitch.

### harmonic_series_spectrum (11.9s)

## Harmonic Series Spectrum

A vibrating source rarely produces a single pure tone. A plucked string, a blown pipe, or a struck bar vibrates simultaneously in several modes, and for many idealized sources these modes occur at frequencies that are integer multiples of a fundamental frequency $f_0$. This set of frequencies,

$$
f_n = n f_0, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

is the **harmonic series**: $f_1 = f_0$ is the fundamental, $f_2 = 2f_0$ the second harmonic, $f_3 = 3f_0$ the third, and so on. Each harmonic carries its own amplitude $A_n$, and the full set of pairs $(f_n, A_n)$ is the sound's **spectrum** — the "fingerprint" that distinguishes a violin from a flute even when both play the same fundamental pitch. This connects directly to the composition of *frequency* (which sets pitch) and *amplitude* (which sets loudness of each component) into a structured, *periodic* sound.

**Worked example.** Suppose a guitar string is tuned so its fundamental is $f_0 = 220\text{ Hz}$ (the note A3). Its harmonic series is $220, 440, 660, 880, 1100, \dots$ Hz. If the string is plucked near its middle, even-numbered harmonics ($440, 880, \dots$) are suppressed because that plucking point sits near a node for those modes, while odd harmonics dominate. A spectrum reading might show $A_1 = 1.0$, $A_2 \approx 0.05$, $A_3 = 0.6$, $A_4 \approx 0.03$, $A_5 = 0.3$ — a pattern that gives the guitar its characteristic "hollow" timbre compared to a bowed violin, whose bowing excites harmonics more evenly.

**Problem-solving application.** Given a recorded spectrum, you can work backward to identify the source. Suppose a spectral analyzer detects strong peaks at $150, 300, 450, 600\text{ Hz}$ with no significant energy at other frequencies. Since $150, 300, 450, 600$ are exactly $1\times, 2\times, 3\times, 4\times$ a common value, you can infer $f_0 = 150\text{ Hz}$ and classify the source as a harmonic (periodic) vibrator rather than a noise source. This is precisely how tuners, pitch-detection software, and instrument-classification algorithms operate: they extract the fundamental from a peak spectrum and use the relative amplitudes $A_n$ as a timbre signature for further analysis.

### loudness_pitch_timbre (12.2s)

## Loudness Pitch Timbre

Every sound can be described along three independent perceptual dimensions, each of which maps onto a measurable physical property of the underlying wave. **Loudness** is the perceptual correlate of amplitude — how much energy the wave carries, typically measured via sound pressure level in decibels. **Pitch** is the perceptual correlate of frequency — how many times per second the waveform repeats, measured in hertz. **Timbre** is the perceptual quality that lets you distinguish a violin from a trumpet playing the identical pitch at the identical loudness; it arises from the harmonic series, the specific mixture of the fundamental frequency and its integer-multiple overtones that make up the sound's spectrum.

The decibel scale is logarithmic because human loudness perception is logarithmic, not linear, in sound pressure:

$$L = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right)$$

where $p$ is the measured sound pressure and $p_0$ is a reference pressure (the threshold of human hearing, $20\ \mu\text{Pa}$). This means a sound with ten times the pressure amplitude is perceived as an increase of 20 dB, not a tenfold increase in loudness — doubling perceived loudness roughly corresponds to a 10 dB increase.

**Worked example.** A tuning fork vibrates at $f = 440\ \text{Hz}$ (pitch: concert A). A violin and a flute can both produce a 440 Hz fundamental, but the violin's spectrum has strong energy at the 2nd, 3rd, and higher harmonics ($880\ \text{Hz}, 1320\ \text{Hz}, \dots$), while the flute's spectrum is dominated by the fundamental with weaker overtones. The ear fuses this whole harmonic complex into a single pitch percept (440 Hz) while using the relative amplitudes of the harmonics — the spectral envelope — to identify the source as "violin" rather than "flute."

**Problem-solving application.** Given a recorded waveform, you can separate these three dimensions computationally: compute the RMS amplitude for loudness, find the spectrum's lowest strong peak (via a Fourier transform) for pitch, and examine the relative magnitudes of the harmonic peaks above it for timbre. This decomposition underlies audio engineering tasks like equalization (reshaping timbre without changing pitch), pitch correction (auto-tune), and instrument classification algorithms, all of which manipulate one dimension while holding the others fixed.

### psychoacoustics (30.0s)

## Psychoacoustics

Psychoacoustics is the branch of psychophysics that studies how listeners perceive the physical properties of sound — frequency, amplitude, and spectral content — and how that perception departs from a simple, linear readout of the physical signal. Where acoustics measures sound pressure and frequency with instruments, psychoacoustics asks a different question: given a stimulus of known physical intensity, what does a human listener actually report hearing? The answers come from controlled experiments — presenting calibrated tones or noises to subjects and recording judgments of loudness, pitch, or discriminability — and they consistently show that perception is nonlinear and context-dependent.

The central quantitative result, and the one new primitive this section introduces, is the Weber–Fechner law: the just-noticeable difference in a stimulus is proportional to the stimulus's existing magnitude, which means perceived intensity grows with the *logarithm* of physical intensity rather than in direct proportion to it. Applied to sound, with intensity $I$ measured relative to a reference intensity $I_0$ (the threshold of human hearing, about $10^{-12}\,\text{W/m}^2$), perceived loudness level in decibels is defined as

$$
L = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)
$$

This logarithmic relationship explains a counterintuitive fact: doubling the physical power of a sound source raises the decibel level by only about 3 dB, and listeners perceive that as a modest increase in loudness, not a doubling. It also explains why the same absolute change in intensity is far more noticeable in a quiet room than at a rock concert — the ear responds to *relative* change, not absolute change.

This same logarithmic sensitivity underlies how lossy audio compression works. Human hearing is limited to roughly 20 Hz–20 kHz, so anything outside that range can be discarded outright. More importantly, because the ear judges loudness relatively rather than absolutely, a loud tone can make quieter, nearby frequencies effectively inaudible even though they are still physically present in the signal — the same logarithmic insensitivity to small absolute differences that makes a 3 dB change go unnoticed. Formats like MP3 use this to identify and discard spectral content a listener cannot actually detect, achieving 5–10x compression with minimal perceived quality loss — a direct engineering payoff from measuring the gap between physical signal and perceptual experience.
