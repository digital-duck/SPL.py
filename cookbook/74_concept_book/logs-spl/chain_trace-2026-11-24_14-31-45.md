### macronutrients (0.6s)

## Macronutrients

**Macronutrients** are the nutrients the body needs in large amounts (grams per day, not milligrams): **protein**, **carbohydrate**, and **fat**. They do two jobs. They supply energy, and they supply raw material for building and maintaining tissue. Water and alcohol are sometimes discussed alongside them, but the three above are the core of nutrition planning. Vitamins and minerals, called micronutrients, supply no energy.

### Energy values

Food energy is measured in kilocalories (kcal). The standard Atwater values are:

| Macronutrient | Energy (kcal per gram) | Main roles |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 4 | Preferred fuel for brain and working muscle; stored as glycogen |
| Protein | 4 | Building and repairing muscle, enzymes, hormones, immune proteins |
| Fat | 9 | Concentrated energy store; cell membranes; absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K |

Fat carries more than twice the energy per gram of the other two. This is why a small amount of oil adds many calories to a meal.

### Worked example

A serving of granola is labeled: 30 g carbohydrate, 8 g protein, 12 g fat. Find its energy and the share that comes from fat.

$$
E = 30(4) + 8(4) + 12(9) = 120 + 32 + 108 = 260 \text{ kcal}
$$

The fat share is:

$$
\frac{108}{260} \approx 0.42
$$

So about 42% of the calories come from fat, even though fat is only 24% of the food's mass in grams ($12/50$). Comparing calories and grams gives different pictures, and you need to know which one a recommendation uses.

### Problem-solving application

A common reference range for adults is 45–65% of energy from carbohydrate, 10–35% from protein, and 20–35% from fat. Suppose a person eats 2,200 kcal per day and wants 20% of that from protein.

1. Protein energy: $0.20 \times 2200 = 440$ kcal.
2. Convert to grams: $\frac{440}{4} = 110$ g of protein.

Check this against body size. For a 70 kg person, 110 g is about 1.6 g per kg of body mass. That is within the range often suggested for people doing regular strength training (roughly 1.2–2.0 g/kg). It is well above the 0.8 g/kg minimum recommended for sedentary adults. The percentage target and the per-kilogram target can disagree, so check both.

The same method works in reverse. If a meal plan gives 250 g carbohydrate, 90 g protein, and 70 g fat, the total is $1000 + 360 + 630 = 1990$ kcal. Fat supplies $\frac{630}{1990} \approx 32\%$, inside the reference range.

### Key points

- Energy comes from carbohydrate and protein at 4 kcal/g and from fat at 9 kcal/g.
- Protein is the main source of amino acids for building tissue. It is used for energy mainly when other fuel is short.
- Convert between grams and percentages using the energy values, and check totals against a person's body size and activity.

### dietary_fiber (0.6s)

## Dietary Fiber

**Dietary fiber** is the part of plant foods made of carbohydrates that human digestive enzymes cannot break down. Because it is not digested in the small intestine, fiber passes into the large intestine, where it does two jobs: it adds bulk to stool, and it serves as food for gut bacteria.

Fiber is usually sorted into two types by how it behaves in water:

| Type | Behavior | Main effects | Common sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soluble | Dissolves or swells into a gel | Slows glucose absorption, lowers LDL cholesterol, fermented by gut bacteria | Oats, beans, apples, psyllium |
| Insoluble | Does not dissolve; stays largely intact | Adds bulk, speeds passage through the gut | Whole wheat, bran, vegetable skins, nuts |

Most plant foods contain both types in different proportions.

When gut bacteria ferment fiber, they produce short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate. Cells lining the colon use butyrate as a fuel. Because of this, fiber supplies a small amount of energy, about 2 kcal per gram on average, compared with about 4 kcal per gram for digestible carbohydrate.

### Worked example

Adult intake targets are about 14 g of fiber per 1,000 kcal eaten. A person eating 2,200 kcal per day has this target:

$$\frac{14\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{kcal}} \times 2200\ \text{kcal} = 30.8\ \text{g}$$

Suppose their day looks like this:

| Food | Fiber (g) |
|---|---|
| 1 cup cooked oatmeal | 4 |
| 1 medium apple with skin | 4 |
| Sandwich on white bread | 2 |
| 1/2 cup cooked lentils | 8 |
| 1 cup cooked broccoli | 5 |
| **Total** | **23** |

The shortfall is $30.8 - 23 = 7.8$ g.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem:** Close the 7.8 g gap using swaps rather than adding calories.

1. Swap white bread for whole wheat bread. This adds about 3 g, so the gap is now about 4.8 g.
2. Add 1 cup of raspberries as a snack. At about 8 g per cup, this covers the remaining gap.

The new total is about 34 g, which meets the target.

**A practical caution:** increase fiber gradually over a few weeks and drink enough water. A sudden jump from 10 g to 35 g per day often causes gas, bloating, and cramping, because gut bacteria need time to adjust. Fiber also needs fluid to move through the gut. Without it, a high-fiber diet can worsen constipation.

**Interpreting labels:** a food labeled "whole grain" may contain little fiber. Check the grams of fiber per serving on the nutrition label, and compare products per 100 kcal or per serving. Fruit juice has very little fiber, while the whole fruit has much more, so the whole fruit is the better choice for fiber.

### carbohydrate_quality (0.8s)

## Carbohydrate Quality

**Definition.** Carbohydrate quality describes how a carbohydrate-containing food affects blood glucose and overall health, based on its fiber content, degree of processing, and how quickly its sugars reach the bloodstream. Whole grains, legumes, vegetables, and intact fruit are digested slowly because fiber and intact cell structure slow the breakdown of starch. Refined starches (white bread, white rice, pastries) and sugary drinks are digested quickly and produce a sharper rise in blood glucose.

Two standard measures quantify this:

- **Glycemic index (GI):** a ranking from 0 to 100 of how fast a food raises blood glucose compared with pure glucose, using a fixed portion containing 50 g of available carbohydrate.
- **Glycemic load (GL):** the GI adjusted for the portion actually eaten.

$$\text{GL} = \frac{\text{GI} \times \text{available carbohydrate (g)}}{100}$$

Available carbohydrate means total carbohydrate minus fiber. A GL of 10 or below is generally considered low, 11–19 medium, and 20 or above high.

**Worked example.** Compare two breakfasts that each supply about 40 g of total carbohydrate.

*Breakfast A:* one cup of sweetened corn flakes with a glass of juice. Suppose the available carbohydrate is 38 g and the combined GI is about 80.

$$\text{GL} = \frac{80 \times 38}{100} \approx 30$$

*Breakfast B:* a cup of cooked rolled oats with an apple. Suppose the available carbohydrate is 33 g (7 g of fiber already subtracted) and the combined GI is about 50.

$$\text{GL} = \frac{50 \times 33}{100} \approx 17$$

The total carbohydrate is similar, but Breakfast A is a high-GL meal and Breakfast B is a medium-GL meal. Blood glucose after A typically peaks higher and falls faster, which often brings earlier hunger.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose a person with prediabetes wants to cut the glucose load of lunch without eating less food. A practical process:

1. Estimate the GL of the current meal. For example, 1.5 cups of white rice (GI about 73, 60 g available carbohydrate) gives $\text{GL} = \frac{73 \times 60}{100} \approx 44$.
2. Swap one refined item for a whole, fiber-rich one. Replacing it with 1.5 cups of cooked lentils (GI about 30, 35 g available carbohydrate) gives $\text{GL} = \frac{30 \times 35}{100} \approx 10.5$.
3. Add protein, fat, or vinegar-based dressing. These can slow stomach emptying and lower the glucose response further.
4. Check the result with a glucose meter or continuous monitor if available, since individual responses vary.

Two cautions apply. GI values vary with ripeness, cooking time, and food combinations, so treat published values as estimates. GL also does not capture everything: a low-GL food such as ice cream is not automatically healthy. Use GL together with fiber, processing level, and overall nutrient content when judging a food.

### dietary_fat_quality (0.8s)

## Dietary Fat Quality

**Definition.** Dietary fat quality describes *which* fats you eat, not just how much. Fats differ in chemical structure, and the structure changes how they affect blood lipids and cardiovascular risk.

- **Unsaturated fats** have one or more carbon–carbon double bonds. Monounsaturated fats are found in olive oil, avocado and almonds. Polyunsaturated fats are found in walnuts, flaxseed, sunflower oil and fatty fish such as salmon. Omega-3 and omega-6 fats are polyunsaturated.
- **Saturated fats** have no double bonds. They are found in butter, fatty red meat, cheese, coconut oil and palm oil.
- **Industrial trans fats** are made by partially hydrogenating vegetable oils. They both raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol, which is why many countries have banned or restricted them.

All fats provide about 9 kcal per gram, so quality is a separate question from quantity. Replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat generally lowers LDL cholesterol. Replacing it with refined carbohydrate does not give the same benefit.

**Worked example.** Dietary guidelines commonly suggest keeping saturated fat below about 10% of daily energy. Some cardiology guidance suggests about 6% for people who need to lower LDL.

Suppose someone eats 2,000 kcal per day, and 14% of that comes from saturated fat.

1. Energy from saturated fat: $0.14 \times 2000 = 280$ kcal.
2. Grams of saturated fat: $\frac{280}{9} \approx 31$ g.
3. A 10% limit is $0.10 \times 2000 = 200$ kcal, or $\frac{200}{9} \approx 22$ g.

The person is about 9 g over the limit.

**Problem-solving application.** Closing that 9 g gap by swapping foods works better than cutting fat in general. Compare these two lunches:

| Choice | Approx. saturated fat |
|---|---|
| Cheeseburger with fries cooked in a hard shortening | 12 g, plus possible trans fat |
| Grilled salmon, olive-oil dressing, small handful of walnuts | 3 g |

Choosing the second lunch removes about 9 g of saturated fat, which closes the gap in one meal. It also adds omega-3 fats. A similar swap is butter for olive oil when cooking, or a snack of nuts in place of a pastry made with shortening.

To apply the idea to any food, follow these steps:

1. Read the Nutrition Facts label for saturated fat and trans fat.
2. Check the ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated oil." If it appears, the product contains industrial trans fat, even if the label says 0 g. Labels may round amounts under 0.5 g per serving down to zero.
3. Prefer foods whose fat comes mostly from nuts, seeds, fish or plant oils.
4. Judge the replacement, not just the removal. Ask what the food will be replaced with.

Fat quality is one part of the overall diet. A pattern rich in vegetables, whole grains, legumes and unsaturated fats, such as the Mediterranean diet, has shown cardiovascular benefit in large trials. The benefit comes from the whole pattern, not from any single nutrient.

### five_phase_correspondences (0.8s)

## Five-Phase Correspondences

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the **Five-Phase correspondences** are a classification scheme that sorts body parts, flavors, colors, seasons, and other observable features into five groups: Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water. Items in the same group are said to be related. The table is a filing system. TCM uses it as a mnemonic and a clinical checklist, not as a measured law of physiology.

### The core table

| Phase | Organ (yin / yang) | Flavor | Color | Season | Tissue | Sense organ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood | Liver / Gallbladder | Sour | Green | Spring | Tendons | Eyes |
| Fire | Heart / Small Intestine | Bitter | Red | Summer | Vessels | Tongue |
| Earth | Spleen / Stomach | Sweet | Yellow | Late summer | Muscle | Mouth |
| Metal | Lung / Large Intestine | Pungent | White | Autumn | Skin | Nose |
| Water | Kidney / Bladder | Salty | Black or dark blue | Winter | Bones | Ears |

In TCM, an "organ" such as the Spleen names a functional system, not only the anatomical organ of the same name in biomedicine. Do not equate the two when reading the table.

### Worked example

A patient reports red, irritated eyes, frequent irritability, and symptoms that worsen every spring. A practitioner using the table reasons in three steps:

1. **Cluster the signs.** Eyes (sense organ), spring (season), and irritability (an emotion TCM links to Wood) all point to the Wood row.
2. **Name the implicated system.** TCM would say the Liver system is involved.
3. **Choose a response.** The table suggests asking about sour foods, green vegetables, and spring routines. It also suggests checking the tendons, for example for cramps or stiffness.

Notice that the table generates a hypothesis. It does not confirm anything. Red eyes and seasonal worsening have well-established biomedical explanations, such as allergic conjunctivitis from spring pollen, and those should be ruled out first.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** A student wants to use the table to plan a "winter-appropriate" diet. How should they proceed, and what should they check?

**Solution.**

1. *Read the row.* Winter corresponds to Water, with the salty flavor, dark colors, and the Kidney system. A table-driven plan would favor dark foods such as black beans, seaweed, and dark berries.
2. *Test the implied advice against nutrition science.* Black beans supply protein and fiber, and dark berries supply anthocyanins, so those suggestions are harmless. The salty flavor is different. The adult recommendation is under about 2,300 mg of sodium per day, and most people already exceed it. Eating more salty food because winter is "salty season" would be poor advice, especially with hypertension.
3. *Decide.* Use the table for variety and seasonal eating, and use measured quantities for decisions that carry risk.

**Takeaway.** The correspondences are a memory aid for organizing observations. Dietary and clinical decisions should rest on measured quantities such as sodium intake, blood pressure, and lab values. Controlled trials have not shown that the table's links, for example between flavors and organs, predict health outcomes.

### Check your understanding

1. Which row would a practitioner consult for a patient with chronic dry skin and a runny nose in autumn?
2. Name one correspondence in the table that has a direct biomedical counterpart, and one that does not.

*(Answers: 1. Metal, because skin, nose, and autumn all fall in that row. 2. Answers vary. For example, the Kidney system's link to bones loosely parallels the kidney's role in vitamin D activation and calcium balance. The link between the salty flavor and the Water phase has no established physiological counterpart.)*

### micronutrients_and_hydration (0.6s)

## Micronutrients and Hydration

**Definition.** Micronutrients are vitamins and minerals. The body needs them in milligrams or micrograms per day, compared with the hundreds of grams of carbohydrate, fat and protein it needs. They supply no usable energy. Instead they act as **co-factors**: helper molecules that enzymes need to work. Examples are B vitamins in energy metabolism, iron in hemoglobin, calcium in muscle contraction, and sodium and potassium in nerve signalling. Water is needed in much larger amounts, but it belongs here because it is the medium in which these reactions happen. It makes up roughly 50–60% of adult body mass. It carries nutrients, removes waste, and helps regulate temperature through sweat.

Two ideas help in solving problems:

- **Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)** are stored in body fat, so excess can accumulate to toxic levels.
- **Water-soluble vitamins (B-complex, C)** are mostly excreted in urine, so they need regular intake.

Minerals are divided into macrominerals (calcium, sodium, potassium, magnesium), needed at hundreds of milligrams per day, and trace minerals (iron, zinc, iodine), needed at a few milligrams or less.

**Worked example: sweat loss during exercise.** A 70 kg runner weighs 70.0 kg before a one-hour run and 68.9 kg after. During the run they drank 0.5 L of water. Fluid lost is the change in body mass plus the fluid consumed, taking 1 kg of water as about 1 L:

$$
\text{sweat loss} = (70.0 - 68.9)\ \text{kg} + 0.5\ \text{kg} = 1.6\ \text{kg} \approx 1.6\ \text{L}
$$

The runner's body mass fell by $\frac{1.1}{70.0} \approx 1.6\%$. Performance and thinking often begin to decline at around 2% loss, so this runner is close to that point. A common recommendation is to replace about 1.25–1.5 L of fluid for each kilogram lost, over the hours after exercise. Sweat also carries sodium, so a long, heavy sweat is best replaced with fluid that contains some sodium, not plain water alone.

**Problem-solving application.** Consider a vegan student who feels fatigued, and whose blood test shows low hemoglobin and low vitamin B12. Work through it in steps:

1. Identify the function. Iron is needed to make hemoglobin, and B12 is needed for red blood cell formation and nerve function.
2. Identify the dietary source. B12 occurs naturally almost only in animal foods, so a vegan diet needs fortified foods or a supplement. Plant iron (non-heme) is absorbed less well than iron from meat.
3. Choose the fix. Pair iron-rich plants such as lentils with vitamin C foods, which improve absorption, and take a B12 supplement or eat fortified foods.
4. Check the risk. Fat-soluble vitamins and iron can be harmful in excess, so supplement to a measured deficit and not "just in case."

A similar approach works for any case. Name the nutrient's role, find its dietary source, check for factors that block or boost absorption, and compare the intake with the recommended range.

**Context note.** In TCM, thirst, sweating and fluid balance are described in terms of body "dampness" and "fluids." These terms are not measured quantities, and in practice hydration is judged by body-mass change, urine color and thirst.

### spleen_transformation_transport (0.5s)

## The Spleen Transforms and Transports (脾主运化)

**Definition.** In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the Spleen (脾) is described by its function more than by the anatomical organ of the same name. TCM holds that the Spleen governs *transformation and transport* (运化) in two linked jobs:

1. **Transforming food and drink.** The Spleen is said to break ingested food and fluid down into usable "essence" (水谷精微), which is then raised to the Lung and Heart to be turned into qi and blood.
2. **Transporting fluids.** The Spleen is said to move water through the body, so that it nourishes tissue and does not pool as dampness (湿) or phlegm (痰).

This work is said to depend on Spleen yang (from Chapter 5), the warming, activating aspect of the Spleen. In TCM, cold or raw foods, irregular meals, overthinking, and prolonged illness are thought to weaken Spleen yang, so transformation and transport falter.

**Mapping to modern physiology.** The idea loosely parallels digestion, nutrient absorption, and fluid balance, but it is a different framework. It does not map one-to-one onto the anatomical spleen, which filters blood and stores immune cells and plays little part in digestion. The TCM pattern is a way of organizing symptoms, and the following scenario shows how a practitioner reasons with it.

**Worked example.** A 20-year-old student reports four weeks of the following:

| Observation | TCM reading |
|---|---|
| Bloating after meals, loose stools | Transformation weakened |
| Tiredness, poor appetite | Too little essence reaching qi and blood |
| Heavy limbs, puffy ankles by evening | Fluids not transported, collecting as dampness |
| Cold hands, craving warm drinks | Spleen yang is low |
| Pale, swollen tongue with tooth marks | Classic sign in TCM of Spleen qi/yang deficiency with dampness |

The student's habits: skipped breakfast, iced coffee at midday, a large late dinner, exam stress.

**Problem-solving application.** TCM reasoning proceeds in three steps.

1. **Cluster the signs.** Digestive symptoms, fatigue, and fluid retention together suggest Spleen yang deficiency with dampness, not three separate problems.
2. **Find the strain on the system.** Cold drinks, skipped meals, and a heavy late meal each overload a "warming" digestive process.
3. **Choose a response that fits the pattern.** TCM counseling would favor regular, warm, cooked meals, less iced food, and a lighter dinner. Some practitioners add herbs such as *ginger* or *Atractylodes*.

**Checking the reasoning.** Evidence matters here. Regular meals, less late-night eating, and reduced ultra-processed food have real support for digestive comfort. The Spleen yang explanation itself has not been validated by controlled trials, and a modern clinician would first rule out other causes of these symptoms, such as celiac disease, thyroid disease, anemia, or kidney problems. Puffy ankles together with fatigue can signal heart, liver, or kidney disease, so persistent swelling should be medically evaluated and not just treated with diet.

**Takeaway.** "Transformation and transport" is TCM's way of linking digestion, energy, and fluid handling in one organ system. Using it well means grouping symptoms into a pattern, identifying the habits that burden it, and adjusting those habits. It also means recognizing when symptoms call for conventional testing.

### yin_yang (0.9s)

## Yin and Yang (阴阳)

**Definition.** In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), yin and yang describe pairs of complementary opposites that are defined in relation to each other. Yin is associated with cool, quiet, restful, and nourishing qualities. Yang is associated with warm, active, and energizing qualities. TCM holds that no quality exists alone: "cool" only makes sense next to "warm," and rest only makes sense next to activity. Health, in this framework, is a shifting pattern in which neither side stays fixed or runs unchecked.

This is a descriptive framework, not a measured physical law, so it has no formula. Its practical value is as a vocabulary for organizing observations. Modern physiology does not recognize yin and yang as physical entities, but it does measure the daily rhythms the framework points toward, which makes the comparison useful.

**Worked example: one day.** Consider a typical 24-hour cycle and sort what a person experiences into the two categories:

| Time | Condition | TCM description | Measurable counterpart |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 a.m. | Asleep, cool | Yin predominant | Core temperature near its minimum, about 36.5 °C; resting heart rate about 55 bpm |
| 10 a.m. | Working, alert | Yang rising | Core temperature about 37 °C; heart rate 70–80 bpm |
| 4 p.m. | Exercising | Yang at peak | Heart rate 120–150 bpm; heat output sharply higher |
| 10 p.m. | Winding down | Yang yielding to yin | Heart rate and temperature falling toward baseline |

Notice that each state turns into the other. Activity raises temperature and heart rate, which prompts sweating and later fatigue, and rest then restores capacity for activity. TCM describes this as yin and yang transforming into each other. Physiology describes the same loop with feedback mechanisms, such as the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the nervous system.

**Problem-solving application.** In TCM, imbalance is described by which side is relatively excessive or deficient. Suppose a student reports feeling restless and warm at night, sleeping poorly, and having a dry mouth after weeks of late-night studying with little rest. TCM would call this "yin deficiency with relative yang excess," meaning too little cooling and restorative capacity.

Work through it in steps:

1. **Identify the qualities.** Warmth, restlessness, and poor sleep are yang-type qualities. A lack of rest is the yin-type shortfall.
2. **Name the pattern.** Too much activity and too little recovery.
3. **Choose a response.** The suggestion is to restore the deficient side: a consistent bedtime, less late-evening stimulation, and adequate fluids.
4. **Check against physiology.** These steps match standard sleep-hygiene advice, and they do not depend on the yin-yang framing to work.

That last step is the key habit. The framework can help you organize observations and generate sensible questions, but a claim such as "this herb tonifies yin" needs separate clinical evidence. Persistent symptoms, such as night sweats with weight loss, call for medical evaluation rather than a framework-based self-diagnosis.

**Takeaway.** Yin and yang give a descriptive language for paired, changing states such as rest and activity. Use it to structure observations, and verify any treatment claim with measurable evidence.

### energy_balance (0.8s)

## Energy Balance

**Energy balance** compares the energy you take in from food with the energy your body uses. Both sides are measured in kilocalories (kcal).

**Intake** is the energy in what you eat and drink: carbohydrate and protein provide about 4 kcal per gram, fat about 9 kcal per gram, and alcohol about 7 kcal per gram.

**Expenditure** has three main parts:

- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR):** the energy needed at complete rest to keep the heart beating, the lungs working and the body temperature stable. It is usually the largest share, roughly 60–70% of daily use.
- **Thermic effect of food:** the energy spent digesting, absorbing and storing nutrients, about 10% of intake.
- **Activity:** both deliberate exercise and everyday movement such as walking, standing and fidgeting.

When intake equals expenditure, body weight stays roughly stable. When intake is higher, the surplus is stored, mostly as fat. When it is lower, the body draws on its stores. A common rule of thumb is that about 7,700 kcal corresponds to 1 kg of body fat. It is an approximation, because the body adapts as weight changes.

### Worked example

Estimate BMR with the Mifflin–St Jeor equation. For a 70 kg man, 175 cm tall, age 20:

$$\text{BMR} = 10(70) + 6.25(175) - 5(20) + 5 = 1699 \text{ kcal/day}$$

An activity multiplier of 1.55 (moderately active) gives total daily energy expenditure (TDEE), which already includes the thermic effect of food and activity:

$$\text{TDEE} \approx 1699 \times 1.55 \approx 2634 \text{ kcal/day}$$

If he eats 2,200 kcal per day, the daily balance is:

$$2200 - 2634 = -434 \text{ kcal/day}$$

### Problem-solving application

**Question:** How long would it take him to lose 2 kg of fat at this deficit?

1. Energy to be removed: $2 \times 7700 = 15{,}400$ kcal.
2. Time at the current deficit: $\frac{15400}{434} \approx 35.5$ days, or about 5 weeks.

**Check the assumptions.** As he loses weight, his BMR falls, so the deficit shrinks. Rework the estimate after a few weeks with his new weight. A deficit of 400–500 kcal per day is generally considered sustainable, because it loses fat with less loss of muscle and less hunger than aggressive dieting. Real results also vary because activity multipliers are rough estimates and food labels can be off by 10–20%.

**Try it yourself:** A 60 kg, 165 cm, 18-year-old woman has TDEE of 2,100 kcal. (Her Mifflin–St Jeor equation ends in $-161$ instead of $+5$.) She eats 2,400 kcal per day. Predict her monthly weight change.

- Daily surplus: $2400 - 2100 = 300$ kcal.
- Over 30 days: $300 \times 30 = 9000$ kcal.
- Weight change: $\frac{9000}{7700} \approx 1.2$ kg gained per month, if nothing else changes.

In practice, the method is to estimate expenditure, track intake, compare the two, and adjust based on measured weight over several weeks.

### four_natures_siqi (0.8s)

## The Four Natures (Siqi, 四气)

**Definition.** In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the *four natures* (四气, *siqi*) describe the thermal tendency a food or herb is said to have on the body. The scale has five grades: **cold, cool, neutral, warm, hot**. The label does not refer to serving temperature. Iced ginger tea is still "warming," and a hot bowl of watermelon soup is still "cooling." TCM holds that a food's nature is a stable property of the food itself, observed through the effects people report after eating it.

| Nature | Typical examples | Traditional use |
|---|---|---|
| Cold | bitter melon, seaweed | for "heat" signs such as fever, thirst, irritability |
| Cool | watermelon, cucumber, pear | milder version of the above |
| Neutral | rice, potato, pork | suitable for most people |
| Warm | ginger, lamb, cinnamon | for "cold" signs such as chills, cold hands |
| Hot | dried chili, black pepper (strong) | short-term use, small amounts |

**Worked example.** A student has a sore throat, flushed face, and strong thirst after a week of late nights and spicy takeout. In TCM, these signs fit a "heat" pattern. The classification suggests choosing cooling foods (pear, watermelon, cucumber) and reducing hot foods (chili, fried lamb). Another student, who is often chilled and has cold hands in an air-conditioned library, shows a "cold" pattern. Here the suggestion reverses: ginger tea, cooked oats, and warm soups.

The rule is *opposition*: use a food whose nature counters the pattern, and use neutral foods when no clear pattern appears.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose you are planning meals for a week for someone with "cold" signs. Work in three steps:

1. **Identify the pattern** from reported signs (chills, pale complexion, preference for hot drinks → cold; thirst, red face, irritability → heat).
2. **Select foods** from the opposing side, and keep a base of neutral staples such as rice so the diet stays balanced.
3. **Check for cooking effects.** TCM also treats preparation as shifting nature: stir-frying with ginger is said to warm a dish, while steaming or raw preparation is said to be cooler.

Notice what this system does *not* do. It does not use measured quantities such as kcal, temperature in degrees, or blood markers. Modern nutrition science has no measurement that corresponds to "cold" or "hot" nature. Watermelon is cooling in TCM, yet its physiological content is mostly water (about 91%), with roughly 30 kcal per 100 g. Some real effects overlap with the tradition: capsaicin in chili does trigger heat receptors and sweating, and water-rich fruits aid hydration. But the five-grade scale itself has not been validated by controlled trials, so treat it as a traditional framework for thinking about food, not as a substitute for medical care. A persistent fever, for example, needs clinical evaluation, not a pear.

**Check your understanding.** Classify iced ginger tea, then explain which of its two features (ginger, ice) the four natures refer to. *(Answer: the ginger. It is warm in nature even when served cold.)*

### whole_vs_ultra_processed_food (0.7s)

## Whole vs. Ultra-Processed Food

**Definition.** The NOVA classification sorts foods by the *extent and purpose of industrial processing* rather than by nutrient content. It has four groups:

| Group | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Unprocessed or minimally processed | Edible parts of plants or animals, altered only by drying, freezing, grinding or pasteurizing | Fruit, vegetables, eggs, plain milk, dried beans, fresh meat |
| 2. Processed culinary ingredients | Substances extracted from group 1 foods, used in cooking | Oil, butter, sugar, salt |
| 3. Processed foods | Groups 1 and 2 combined by simple methods | Canned vegetables, cheese, fresh bread |
| 4. Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) | Industrial formulations made mostly from extracted substances (refined starch, sugar, fats, protein isolates) plus additives such as emulsifiers, flavorings, and sweeteners | Soft drinks, packaged snacks, instant noodles, reconstituted meat products |

A practical test for group 4: the ingredient list contains items you would not find in a home kitchen, such as maltodextrin, hydrogenated oil, or "natural flavor."

**Why UPFs are linked to overeating.** UPFs are typically energy-dense, soft, and low in fiber and water. They are easy to eat quickly, so fullness signals arrive after more energy has already been consumed. In a 2019 inpatient trial (Hall et al.), 20 adults were given either an ultra-processed or an unprocessed diet for two weeks, with meals matched for presented calories, sugar, fat, fiber, and macronutrients. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about 500 kcal/day more and gained about 0.9 kg; on the unprocessed diet they lost about 0.9 kg.

**Worked example.** Using the trial's figures, estimate the extra energy intake over 14 days:

$$500\ \text{kcal/day} \times 14\ \text{days} = 7000\ \text{kcal}$$

Body fat stores roughly 7700 kcal per kilogram, a common rule of thumb. So 7000 kcal corresponds to about $\frac{7000}{7700} \approx 0.9$ kg of fat, which is close to the observed weight difference. The rule of thumb is approximate, but here it agrees with the measurement.

**Problem-solving application.** Classify a typical breakfast and find a substitution.

*Meal:* sweetened breakfast cereal with flavored milk, and a fruit-flavored yogurt drink.

1. Read the labels. The cereal lists refined grain flour, sugar, and flavorings (group 4). The flavored milk and yogurt drink contain sweeteners, thickeners, and flavorings (group 4).
2. Replace with the nearest group 1 or 3 equivalents: plain oats cooked in water or milk, fresh fruit, and plain yogurt.
3. Estimate the change. If the original meal is 550 kcal and the swap is 400 kcal, you save 150 kcal per day, or about 1050 kcal per week.

Some caution is warranted: NOVA is a classification of processing, not a ranking of nutrients. Some group 4 foods (certain wholegrain breads or fortified plant milks) may be nutritionally reasonable, and some group 1 foods are not healthy in every quantity. Observational studies link high UPF intake to obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, but they cannot fully separate processing from overall diet quality. The 2019 trial was small and short, so it shows a plausible mechanism rather than long-term proof.

**Takeaway.** Reading ingredient lists, not just calorie counts, is a practical way to spot ultra-processed foods and reduce their share of your diet.

### dampness_and_phlegm (0.8s)

## Dampness and Phlegm (湿与痰)

**Definition.** In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), *dampness* (湿, shī) and *phlegm* (痰浊, tán zhuó) are pathological accumulations of fluid and "turbid" material in the body. TCM holds that the Spleen's role is to transform food and drink into usable nourishment and to move fluids along. When the Spleen is overloaded, for example by a steady intake of greasy, sweet, rich or cold food, the fluids are not transformed properly and pool. Dampness is the thinner, more diffuse form. Phlegm is its thicker, denser product. TCM uses "phlegm" more broadly than Western medicine does. It covers visible sputum, and it also covers lumps, a foggy head and a coated tongue that TCM reads as signs of the same process.

Some authors compare this picture with metabolic overload: intake that exceeds what the body can process and clear. The comparison is an analogy, not an equivalence. "Dampness" is not a measurable substance, and no laboratory test identifies it.

**Characteristic signs in TCM.**

| Feature | Dampness | Phlegm |
|---|---|---|
| Sensation | Heavy limbs, bloating, sluggishness | Chest fullness, foggy thinking |
| Digestion | Loose stools, poor appetite | Nausea, a sticky feeling in the mouth |
| Tongue | Greasy, thick coating | Greasy coating, often swollen tongue |
| Typical trigger | Cold drinks, sweets, damp climate | Long-standing dampness, rich diet |

**Worked example.** A 20-year-old student eats instant noodles, fried chicken and bubble tea most days. Their intake is about 2,800 kcal per day, while their estimated expenditure is about 2,200 kcal per day. They report afternoon heaviness, bloating after meals, and soft stools. A TCM practitioner would call this "Spleen unable to transform dampness."

The energy surplus is ordinary arithmetic:

$$2800 - 2200 = 600 \text{ kcal/day}$$

Since about 7,700 kcal corresponds to roughly 1 kg of body fat, this surplus would add about 1 kg every $\frac{7700}{600} \approx 13$ days if it were sustained. This is a rough estimate. Real weight change is smaller because expenditure rises as weight increases. TCM does not use this calculation. It describes the same pattern in terms of dampness. A physiologist would instead point to a calorie surplus, a high intake of fat and sugar, and low fiber, any of which can cause bloating and sluggishness.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose you want to act on this pattern. A practical approach has three steps:

1. **Record.** Keep a three-day food and symptom log, noting when heaviness and bloating appear.
2. **Adjust the diet.** The TCM advice is to reduce greasy, sweet and cold foods and to eat cooked, simpler meals. This overlaps with mainstream guidance to cut added sugar and saturated fat and to eat more fiber.
3. **Add movement.** Regular walking increases expenditure and is the most reliable part of the advice.

Then compare your log after two weeks. If symptoms improve, the changes helped, whichever framework you use to explain it. Persistent bloating, a lasting cough with sputum, unexplained weight change or fatigue can have medical causes such as thyroid disease, diabetes, celiac disease or respiratory infection. Those need a clinician, not a TCM label.

**Evidence note.** The concepts of dampness and phlegm have no validated biological measure. The dietary advice is sensible mainly because it matches established nutrition guidance.

### five_flavors_wuwei (0.7s)

## The Five Flavors (Wuwei, 五味)

**Definition.** In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), foods and herbs are classified by five flavors (五味, *wuwei*): sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, and salty. TCM holds that each flavor has a characteristic action and an affinity for one organ system. Here "flavor" is a therapeutic category, not only what the tongue detects. TCM also holds that taking any flavor in excess harms its own organ.

| Flavor | Organ | Action | Typical example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sour (酸) | Liver | Astringes (holds in, firms up) | Vinegar, hawthorn |
| Bitter (苦) | Heart | Drains (clears heat, dries) | Bitter melon, coptis |
| Sweet (甘) | Spleen | Tonifies (supplements, moderates) | Jujube, honey |
| Pungent (辛) | Lung | Disperses (moves outward) | Ginger, scallion |
| Salty (咸) | Kidney | Softens (breaks up hardness) | Seaweed, sea salt |

An "organ" in TCM names a functional system, not only the anatomical organ of the same name. The Spleen, for example, covers digestion and the transformation of food into usable energy.

**Worked example.** Suppose a person is described this way: after a heavy meal they feel hot and restless, and their tongue is coated. A TCM practitioner would look for a bitter, draining food. A cup of bitter-melon tea fits. Compare a person with weak digestion, a poor appetite and fatigue after eating. Here the practitioner would choose a sweet, tonifying food such as jujube porridge. The reasoning has three steps:

1. Identify the pattern (excess heat or deficiency).
2. Choose the action that counters it (drain or tonify).
3. Pick the flavor that carries that action.

**Problem-solving application.** Now apply the same reasoning to a day's menu. A person eats a salty ramen lunch, a salty snack, and cured meat for dinner. TCM would call this an excess of salty, which it says burdens the Kidney. A TCM-style adjustment would add other flavors for variety, such as a sour pickled vegetable or a pungent ginger soup, and would cut the salty items.

Modern physiology gives a firmer reason for the same advice. Sodium intake is measured in milligrams, and for most adults guidelines advise staying below about 2,300 mg per day, roughly one teaspoon of table salt. A single instant noodle bowl can supply 1,500 to 2,000 mg. Adding a snack and cured meat can push the daily total past the limit. High sodium intake raises blood pressure in many people and strains the kidneys, so here the TCM warning about salty and Kidney matches an established finding.

Be careful with the rest of the system. The mapping of flavors to specific organs, and the claim that each flavor's excess harms a particular organ, are traditional frameworks. Controlled clinical studies have not validated them as general rules. Treat the table as a way to understand TCM dietary reasoning, not as a substitute for medical advice. Moderation and variety are sound guidance either way.

### gut_microbiome (0.6s)

## The Gut Microbiome

The **gut microbiome** is the community of trillions of microbes, mostly bacteria, that live in the large intestine. Human cells and gut microbes are present in roughly comparable numbers. Together the microbes carry far more genes than the human genome does. Their most important job is **fermentation**. Human enzymes cannot digest most dietary fiber, so it reaches the colon intact. There, bacteria break it down and release **short-chain fatty acids** (SCFAs), mainly acetate, propionate and butyrate.

SCFAs matter for three reasons:

- **Digestion:** Butyrate is the main fuel for the cells lining the colon.
- **Immunity:** A large share of the body's immune tissue sits along the gut wall. It learns to tolerate food and harmless microbes while still responding to pathogens.
- **Metabolism:** SCFAs are absorbed and contribute some energy. They also act as signals that affect appetite, insulin response and fat storage.

A microbiome with many different species (high diversity) is generally associated with better health. Low diversity is associated with inflammatory bowel disease, obesity and recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection.

### Worked example

Suppose a person raises daily fiber intake from 12 g to 30 g. Colonic fermentation of fiber yields roughly 2 kcal per gram, which is about half the 4 kcal per gram of digestible carbohydrate. The extra fermentable fiber contributes about

$$(30 - 12)\ \text{g} \times 2\ \tfrac{\text{kcal}}{\text{g}} = 36\ \text{kcal/day}$$

Real yields vary, because not all fiber is fully fermented. The more important change is that fiber-fermenting species gain more food, SCFA output rises, and stool bulk increases.

A few days of a very different diet can measurably shift microbial composition. That shift usually reverses when the old diet returns, so lasting change requires lasting dietary change.

### Problem-solving application

**Scenario:** After a 10-day course of broad-spectrum antibiotics, a patient has loose stools and bloating. What is a sensible plan?

1. **Diagnose the mechanism.** Antibiotics kill susceptible species indiscriminately, lowering diversity and reducing fermentation. Less fiber is broken down, and opportunistic microbes can expand.
2. **Rebuild with food.** Add varied fermentable fiber (legumes, oats, vegetables, fruit), increasing gradually over several days to limit gas. Include fermented foods such as yogurt with live cultures.
3. **Judge the evidence.** Dietary fiber's effect on SCFA production is well established. Evidence for commercial probiotic supplements is mixed and depends on the strain and the condition. They help in some cases, such as antibiotic-associated diarrhea, but they are not a general cure.
4. **Know the limits.** Fever, blood in the stool, or diarrhea lasting beyond a few days needs medical evaluation. It may signal *C. difficile* infection, which requires specific treatment.

**Takeaway:** The microbiome responds mainly to what it is fed. Diverse fiber, introduced gradually, is the most reliable lever an individual controls.

### circadian_clock (0.6s)

## The Circadian Clock

The **circadian clock** is the body's internal timer, which runs on a cycle of roughly 24 hours. (The word comes from the Latin *circa diem*, "about a day.") Its master clock is a small cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus called the suprachiasmatic nucleus. The clock is set mainly by light. Specialized cells in the retina detect light, especially blue light, and signal the master clock, which then coordinates timers in nearly every organ. The clock schedules sleep, body temperature, hormone release, and digestive activity. For example, melatonin rises in the evening, cortisol peaks shortly after waking, and body temperature reaches its lowest point in the early morning.

Without light cues, the human clock runs slightly longer than 24 hours, on average about 24.2 hours. Daily light exposure resets it, so it does not drift.

### Worked example

Suppose a student's clock runs at 24.2 hours per cycle and gets no light cues. Each day, their internal "morning" falls 0.2 hours, or 12 minutes, later than the day before.

$$0.2\ \text{h/day} \times 60\ \text{min/h} = 12\ \text{min/day}$$

After 10 days, the drift is $10 \times 12 = 120$ minutes, or 2 hours. In real life, morning light shifts the clock earlier by about this amount each day, which keeps it aligned with the sun.

### Problem-solving application

Consider a student who normally sleeps from 11 pm to 7 am. They fly east across 6 time zones. On arrival, local time is 6 hours ahead of their body's clock, so their clock "thinks" it is 6 hours earlier than it is. They will feel sleepy and alert at the wrong local times. This mismatch is **jet lag**.

Most people can shift their clock by about 1 to 1.5 hours per day with well-timed light. Taking 1 hour per day as a conservative rate, this student needs about 6 days to adapt. Eastward travel is usually harder than westward travel. Traveling east requires moving the clock *earlier*, which works against its natural tendency to run long, while traveling west requires moving it *later*.

The timing of light matters as much as the amount:

| Light exposure | Effect on the clock |
|---|---|
| Morning, after the body's temperature minimum | Shifts the clock earlier |
| Evening, before the body's temperature minimum | Shifts the clock later |
| Bright light late at night | Delays the clock and suppresses melatonin |

To adapt after flying east, the student should seek bright light in the local morning and avoid bright light during the hours when their body clock still reads "night." Both rules push the clock in the needed direction.

The same logic applies without travel. A student who stays up late on screens and sleeps in on weekends delays their clock. This pattern, sometimes called "social jet lag," can leave them groggy on Monday morning. Keeping a consistent wake time and getting morning light helps anchor the clock.

### Check your understanding

A person's clock runs at 24.5 hours in constant dim light. By how much does their internal day drift later each day? What daily cue would correct this?

*Answer:* The drift is 0.5 h = 30 minutes per day. Morning light exposure would shift the clock earlier each day and offset the drift.

### fiber_microbiome_axis (0.6s)

## The Fiber–Microbiome Axis

**Definition.** Dietary fiber is plant carbohydrate that human digestive enzymes cannot break down. It passes intact to the colon, where trillions of resident bacteria ferment it. The main products are short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs): acetate, propionate, and butyrate. The fiber–microbiome axis is this chain of events: fiber intake, then bacterial fermentation, then SCFAs, then effects on the gut lining, inflammation, and appetite.

Each SCFA has a different job:

| SCFA | Main role |
|---|---|
| Butyrate | Primary fuel for colon-lining cells; supports the gut barrier |
| Propionate | Taken up by the liver; linked to appetite signaling and glucose regulation |
| Acetate | Enters the bloodstream; used as fuel by tissues and involved in appetite signaling |

SCFAs also lower the pH of the colon. This favors beneficial bacteria and discourages some pathogens. They help regulate inflammation by acting on immune cells, and they stimulate gut hormones such as GLP-1 and PYY that promote fullness.

**Worked example.** Typical adult fiber intake in the United States is about 15 g per day. Dietary guidelines recommend about 14 g per 1,000 kcal. For a 2,000 kcal diet, the target is:

$$\frac{14\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{kcal}} \times 2000\ \text{kcal} = 28\ \text{g}$$

That is nearly double the typical intake. Here is a day that reaches the target:

- Breakfast: oatmeal with a sliced pear (about 8 g)
- Lunch: lentil soup and a side salad (about 10 g)
- Snack: an apple with skin (about 4 g)
- Dinner: brown rice with broccoli and black beans (about 9 g)

The total is about 31 g. The mix also matters. Oats and beans supply soluble, fermentable fiber, which feeds SCFA producers readily. Whole grains and vegetables add other fiber types that feed other bacterial groups. In general, a more varied plant intake supports a more diverse microbiome.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose a person eats 12 g of fiber per day and has gas, irregular stools, and frequent hunger between meals. How should they raise fiber intake?

1. **Set a target.** At 2,000 kcal, aim for about 28 g.
2. **Increase gradually.** Adding roughly 3–5 g per week gives the bacterial community time to adapt. Jumping from 12 g to 28 g overnight often causes bloating, because fermentation produces gas along with SCFAs.
3. **Diversify sources.** Rotate legumes, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts, and seeds instead of relying on one supplement.
4. **Add fluid.** Fiber holds water, so drink enough to keep stools soft.
5. **Reassess.** After several weeks, check stool regularity and between-meal hunger.

Some people with irritable bowel syndrome or small intestinal bacterial overgrowth react poorly to highly fermentable fibers. They should adjust the types and amounts with a clinician. SCFAs are one mechanism among several. Human studies show clear benefits of high-fiber diets for bowel health and cardiometabolic risk. The finer details of how individual bacterial species mediate these effects are still being studied.

**Key idea:** Fiber feeds bacteria, and bacteria return SCFAs that help maintain the gut barrier, calm inflammation, and signal fullness. Raising fiber gradually and from varied plant sources is the practical way to support this process.

### five_colors_foods (0.6s)

## The Five Colors of Food (五色)

**Definition.** The five colors (五色) are a Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) scheme for sorting foods by color and linking each color to an organ system. In TCM, green foods are associated with the Liver, red with the Heart, yellow with the Spleen, white with the Lung, and black with the Kidney. In practice the scheme works as a variety cue: if your plate or your week contains all five colors, you are unlikely to be eating the same few foods repeatedly. TCM's organ links are traditional categories, not physiological findings, so use the scheme as a prompt for variety and not as a way to treat disease. The practical benefit is real for a separate reason. Plant pigments mark different groups of nutrients and compounds, which is why modern advice to "eat the rainbow" lands in the same place.

**What the colors tend to indicate nutritionally.**

| TCM color | Example foods | Typical nutrients |
|---|---|---|
| Green (Liver) | spinach, broccoli, bok choy | folate, vitamin K, lutein, fiber |
| Red (Heart) | tomato, red pepper, strawberry | vitamin C, lycopene, anthocyanins |
| Yellow (Spleen) | corn, pumpkin, carrot, millet | beta-carotene, carbohydrate, fiber |
| White (Lung) | daikon, cauliflower, onion, tofu | potassium, allicin compounds, protein (tofu) |
| Black (Kidney) | black beans, black sesame, shiitake | fiber, protein, iron, anthocyanins |

Color is a rough guide only. A carrot is orange but is grouped with yellow, and a white potato and a white sugar cube are both "white" with very different nutritional value.

**Worked example.** Suppose a student eats this dinner: stir-fried bok choy, tomato and egg, steamed corn, and white rice. Count the colors: green (bok choy), red (tomato), yellow (corn and egg yolk), white (rice). That makes four of five. Black is missing. The fix is cheap: add a tablespoon of black sesame seeds, or swap half the rice for black beans. Black beans also add about 7 g of fiber per half cup (cooked), raising the meal's fiber and protein.

**Problem-solving application.** Use the cue to audit a weekly menu.

1. List every vegetable, fruit, legume, and whole grain you ate this week.
2. Tag each item with one of the five colors.
3. Count how many *different* items appear under each color.
4. Pick the color with the fewest entries and add two new foods from it next week.

Say a week's list has seven green items, five red, four yellow, three white, and zero black. The audit shows the gap is black foods, so next week's additions might be black beans and shiitake mushrooms. This is the same logic as a nutrition tracker flagging a low nutrient, but it needs no app and no calorie math.

**Limits.** The color rule does not replace portion and energy considerations. A plate of five colors from fried foods and sweets is still a poor diet. Treat color as a check on variety, applied after the basics of adequate protein, fiber, and total energy are met.

### food_as_medicine_shiliao (0.8s)

## Food as Medicine (Shiliao, 食疗)

**Definition.** *Shiliao* (食疗, "food therapy") rests on the idea of *yaoshi tongyuan* (药食同源, "medicine and food share an origin"). In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), many everyday foods are credited with gentle actions on the body, such as warming, moistening, or supporting digestion. These actions are used in small amounts, matched to the season and the person's constitution. Shiliao is a dietary habit. It is not a replacement for diagnosis or treatment. Where a food has real pharmacology, it can interact with prescribed drugs.

**Common examples.**

| Food | TCM description | Everyday use | Modern note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ginger (生姜) | Warming; "releases the exterior" | Tea at the start of a cold, cold weather | Gingerols have modest evidence against nausea |
| Chinese yam (山药) | Supports the Spleen and digestion | Congee, soups | Starchy and mucilaginous; a gentle carbohydrate |
| Jujube (红枣) | Nourishes blood, calms the mind | Sweet teas, stews | About 3 kcal per gram dried, so mostly sugar |
| Goji (枸杞) | Nourishes Liver and eyes | A pinch in soups or tea | Rich in carotenoids; may interact with warfarin |
| Mung bean (绿豆) | Clears heat | Summer soup | A legume with protein and fiber |

TCM usually describes foods as warming or cooling, and shiliao favors cooling foods in summer and warming ones in winter.

**Worked example.** A 20-year-old student feels chilled and has a scratchy throat at the first sign of a cold. A shiliao approach might be ginger tea: 3 slices of fresh ginger, about 5 g, simmered in 300 mL of water for 10 minutes, with a little brown sugar. 1. **Identify the goal.** Is it comfort, digestion, or a symptom of a possible illness? Serious or persistent symptoms need medical evaluation first.
2. **Check safety.** Look for allergies, pregnancy, diabetes, and medications. Goji can raise warfarin's effect. Jujube and honey add sugar, which matters for blood glucose. Large amounts of ginger may increase bleeding risk alongside anticoagulants.
3. **Check the dose.** Culinary amounts are the point. Concentrated extracts and supplements are a different category with different risks.
4. **Estimate the energy.** A bowl of mung bean soup with 6 dried jujubes (about 3 g each, so roughly 18 g × 3 kcal/g ≈ 54 kcal from jujube) is light. Sweetened versions can quickly become a dessert.
5. **Judge the evidence.** Ginger for nausea has clinical support. Many other claims, such as "nourishing blood," come from TCM tradition and are not established by controlled trials.

**Practice.** A friend with type 2 diabetes who takes warfarin asks about a daily jujube-and-goji tea. Which checks apply? Sugar load from the jujube and the goji–warfarin interaction both apply, so the friend should ask their physician or pharmacist first.

### protein_across_life (0.6s)

## Protein Across the Lifespan

**Definition.** Protein supplies the amino acids the body uses to build and repair muscle, enzymes, and other tissues. Muscle protein is constantly being broken down and rebuilt. Muscle grows or is maintained when synthesis keeps pace with breakdown. Two things influence this balance: the *total* amount of protein eaten per day, and how that protein is *spread* across meals. A single meal can stimulate muscle protein synthesis only up to a limit, so a large dose at dinner does not make up for small doses earlier in the day.

Protein needs also change with age. The standard adult recommendation (RDA) is 0.8 g of protein per kg of body weight per day. Many researchers suggest that adults over about 50 do better with 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day. Aging muscle becomes less responsive to a given dose of protein, a change called anabolic resistance. Together with lower activity levels, this contributes to **sarcopenia**, the gradual loss of muscle mass and strength with age. Sarcopenia raises the risk of falls, fractures, and loss of independence. Needs are also higher during growth in childhood and adolescence, and during pregnancy and lactation.

**Worked example.** A 70 kg adult, age 62, wants to follow 1.2 g/kg/day and spread protein over three meals.

Daily target:

$$70\ \text{kg} \times 1.2\ \frac{\text{g}}{\text{kg}} = 84\ \text{g}$$

Per meal:

$$\frac{84\ \text{g}}{3} = 28\ \text{g}$$

A common per-meal target for older adults is about 25–30 g, so this plan fits. For comparison, the RDA gives $70 \times 0.8 = 56$ g per day, which is about 19 g per meal.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose this person currently eats the following:

| Meal | Foods | Protein |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Toast, jam, coffee | 4 g |
| Lunch | Salad with 60 g chicken | 16 g |
| Dinner | Salmon (150 g), rice, vegetables | 38 g |
| **Total** | | **58 g** |

The total meets the RDA but falls short of the 84 g target. It is also heavily weighted toward dinner, and breakfast gives almost no stimulus to muscle synthesis. To fix both problems, the plan can:

1. Add 3 eggs and a cup of Greek yogurt at breakfast: about +24 g, giving 28 g.
2. Increase lunch chicken to about 120 g, which adds about 15 g and brings lunch to roughly 31 g.
3. Keep dinner at about 38 g, or reduce it slightly.

The new daily total is about 97 g, and each meal supplies more than 25 g. This is above the 84 g target, so dinner could be trimmed a little.

Some practical points apply across age groups. Children and teenagers need adequate protein for growth, which most balanced diets provide. For older adults, evidence indicates that combining higher protein intake with resistance exercise (such as weight training or bodyweight work) preserves muscle better than either one alone. People with kidney disease should consult a clinician before raising protein intake, because the recommendation differs for them.

**Check yourself.** A 55 kg woman, age 68, aims for 1.2 g/kg/day over three meals. What is her daily target and per-meal amount? (Answer: 66 g per day, or 22 g per meal. She might raise breakfast and lunch slightly and eat a bit less at dinner, or add a protein-rich snack.)

### balanced_plate (0.6s)

## The Balanced Plate

A **balanced plate** is a visual rule for building a meal. Fill about half the plate with vegetables and fruit, about a quarter with whole grains, and about a quarter with protein. Add a small portion of healthy fats, such as olive oil, nuts, or avocado. Because it works by proportion of space, you don't need to weigh or count anything. You only need to look at the plate.

The rule has a second check: variety. Different plant pigments come with different nutrients, so aim for several colors across the meal. In TCM, a meal is also described in terms of five colors (green, red, yellow, white, black), and the same habit of "eat many colors" is a practical shortcut here. The nutrition reason is that a varied plate is more likely to supply a wide range of vitamins, minerals, and fiber.

### Worked example

Suppose you eat a 600 kcal dinner of salmon, brown rice, broccoli, and carrots, cooked in olive oil.

- **Half plate (vegetables and fruit):** steamed broccoli and sliced carrots, plus a few orange slices. Roughly 100 kcal.
- **Quarter plate (whole grains):** brown rice. Roughly 200 kcal for about one cup cooked.
- **Quarter plate (protein):** a salmon fillet. Roughly 220 kcal.
- **Healthy fat:** one teaspoon of olive oil. About 40 kcal.

Notice that the plate is half vegetables by *volume*, not by calories. Vegetables are bulky but low in energy, so they fill half the plate while supplying a small share of the energy. That is the point: you get fiber, potassium, and vitamins without a large calorie load.

Color check: green (broccoli), orange (carrot, orange), pink (salmon), and tan (brown rice). That is four distinct colors. Adding black beans or purple cabbage would add a fifth.

### Problem-solving application

**Problem.** A student's usual lunch is a large bowl of white pasta with a little cheese. How can they apply the balanced plate to fix it without eating much more food?

**Step 1: Audit the proportions.** The plate is nearly all refined grain, with little vegetable and little protein.

**Step 2: Resize.** Cut the pasta to a quarter of the bowl and use a whole-grain pasta. Fill half with sautéed spinach, tomatoes, and peppers. Use the last quarter for chicken, lentils, or tofu.

**Step 3: Add fat deliberately.** Use a teaspoon or two of olive oil instead of the extra cheese.

**Step 4: Color check.** The meal is now green, red, and tan, with the protein adding a fourth color.

**Adjusting the template.** The proportions are a starting point. An endurance athlete might enlarge the grain portion, and a person managing blood glucose might shrink it and enlarge the vegetables. The method stays the same: audit the plate, resize the sections, and check the colors.

## Eating for Your Constitution (体质)

**Definition.** In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), a *constitution* (体质, *tǐzhì*) is a person's relatively stable pattern of body tendencies, such as how warm or cold they run, how easily they tire, and how they handle fluids and digestion. A widely used modern classification divides people into nine types: balanced, qi-deficient, yang-deficient, yin-deficient, phlegm-damp, damp-heat, blood-stasis, qi-stagnation, and special-diathesis (allergic). TCM holds that food has its own "nature," such as warming, cooling, or neutral, so the same food can help one constitution and aggravate another. The nine-type questionnaire is a standardized screening tool in China. Controlled evidence that matching food to constitution improves health outcomes is limited, so treat it as a framework for self-observation, not a diagnosis.

**Worked example.** Two roommates, Ana and Ben, both eat the same lunch: a bowl of raw cucumber and watermelon salad with iced green tea, about 350 kcal.

| | Ana | Ben |
|---|---|---|
| Typical pattern | Cold hands and feet, loose stools, prefers hot drinks | Feels hot, flushed face, oily skin, bitter taste in the mouth |
| TCM type | Yang-deficient | Damp-heat |
| Effect of the lunch | Feels bloated and chilled afterward | Feels refreshed |
| TCM reasoning | Cooling foods burden a body already short of warmth | Cooling, fluid-clearing foods ease heat |

Same food, opposite responses. In TCM terms, Ana would be steered toward warm cooked dishes such as ginger-and-lamb soup or congee with cinnamon. Ben would be steered away from greasy, fried, or spicy food (such as hot pot) and toward mung beans, bitter melon, and barley.

**Problem-solving application.** Use a four-step routine:

1. **Observe.** For two weeks, log what you eat and how you feel 1–3 hours later (energy, digestion, temperature, sleep). A simple notebook works.
2. **Group the patterns.** Look for repeated links, such as "cold drinks, then cramping" or "fried food, then breakouts and heaviness."
3. **Match to a type.** Compare your pattern with the nine descriptions. Many people fit more than one, and TCM allows for a main type plus secondary tendencies.
4. **Adjust one variable at a time.** Change a single item, such as swapping iced drinks for warm ones, for a week and compare your log.

*Practice problem.* Mei often feels tired after meals, catches colds easily, and has a weak voice. Her diet is mostly raw salads and smoothies. Which type does this suggest, and what is one change to test?

*Answer.* The pattern fits qi deficiency. TCM would suggest cooked, easily digested foods such as rice porridge, sweet potato, and chicken soup. She can test replacing one raw smoothie a day with warm porridge and track her energy.

**Caution.** A constitution is not a medical diagnosis. Persistent fatigue, chronic diarrhea, or unexplained weight change needs a physician's evaluation. Food allergies must be managed by conventional medical rules, whatever your TCM type. Dietary changes should also keep total energy and nutrient intake adequate.

### junk_load (0.6s)

## Junk Load

**Junk load** (劣质食物, "junk training") is a way of describing food that forces the digestive system to do a lot of work for little nourishment. The analogy is to a gym session in which you train with poor form under a heavy but useless load: effort is spent, strain accumulates, and no useful adaptation results. Nutrition science supports the core idea in measurable terms. Ultra-processed, low-fiber, high-sugar foods tend to deliver many kilocalories (kcal) with few micronutrients, little protein, and little fiber.

The comparison is a teaching metaphor, not a physiological mechanism. The digestive tract does not "train" the way a muscle does, and the useful quantity to compare is nutrient density: nutrition delivered per kcal consumed.

### Worked example

Compare two 200 kcal snacks.

| | Snack A: soda and candy | Snack B: apple and 20 g almonds |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | 200 kcal | about 200 kcal |
| Fiber | 0 g | about 6 g |
| Protein | 0 g | about 4 g |
| Micronutrients | almost none | vitamin C, vitamin E, magnesium, potassium |
| Effect on blood glucose | fast spike, then drop | slower, flatter rise |

Both snacks supply the same energy, but only Snack B supplies materials the body can use for repair and regulation. Snack A is the "heavy but useless load." It adds kcal while leaving the body's nutrient needs unmet. It also encourages further eating, because it satisfies little hunger, so the day's total intake climbs.

In TCM, the Spleen and Stomach are said to "work hard" to process greasy, sweet, and cold foods that yield little nourishment. This is a traditional framing of the same observation. The modern account is in terms of nutrient density, satiety, and blood glucose response.

### Problem-solving application

Suppose a student eats 2,200 kcal per day, and 700 kcal of it comes from sugary drinks and packaged snacks with almost no nutrients.

1. **Find the junk share.** The share of daily energy is $\frac{700}{2200} \approx 0.32$, so about 32%.
2. **Judge it.** One commonly cited guideline, from the World Health Organization, recommends keeping free sugars below 10% of total energy. That is about 220 kcal on this diet. Free sugars are only part of the 700 kcal, but the junk share is clearly high.
3. **Swap, don't just cut.** Replace half of the junk kcal (350 kcal) with foods that carry nutrients: legumes, vegetables, whole grains, fruit, yogurt. Total energy stays the same, but the same 2,200 kcal now supplies more fiber, protein, and micronutrients.
4. **Check the result.** Track hunger, energy through the afternoon, and, if relevant, a food log or blood glucose readings over a week.

**Key takeaway:** judge food by what it delivers per kcal, not by kcal alone. A useful habit is to ask of each meal, "What nutrients and fullness am I getting for this energy?" Replacing junk load with nutrient-dense food is usually more sustainable than simply eating less.

### meal_timing_chrononutrition (0.6s)

## Meal Timing and Chrononutrition

**Definition.** Chrononutrition is the study of *when* we eat, not only what or how much. Human metabolism follows a circadian rhythm, a roughly 24-hour cycle driven by a master clock in the brain and by clocks in the liver, pancreas, gut, and fat tissue. Light sets the master clock. Eating times help set the peripheral clocks. In controlled studies, the same meal eaten in the evening usually produces a higher and longer blood glucose rise than it does in the morning, because insulin sensitivity is highest earlier in the day and falls toward night. Melatonin released in the evening also dampens insulin secretion.

In TCM, the late-night eating concern is described as "forcing the organs to work overtime." This is a metaphor, not a measured mechanism. The physiological version is the clock mismatch described above. Human trials do support that mismatch, but they are small and short-term. Evidence from long-term outcomes is weaker.

**Worked example.** Suppose a lab gives the same 600 kcal meal to one volunteer at two times, and measures blood glucose in mg/dL.

| Meal time | Fasting glucose | Peak glucose | Rise |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8:00 a.m. | 90 | 135 | 45 |
| 9:00 p.m. | 90 | 160 | 70 |

The rise is the peak minus the fasting value, so the morning rise is $135 - 90 = 45$ mg/dL and the evening rise is $160 - 90 = 70$ mg/dL. The evening response is $\frac{70 - 45}{45} \approx 56\%$ larger for an identical meal. (These numbers are illustrative, but the direction matches published findings.)

**Problem-solving application.** Consider a student who eats 2,000 kcal a day, with 200 kcal at breakfast, 500 at lunch, and 1,300 after 8:00 p.m. because of evening study. How should they adjust?

1. **Identify the problem.** Of the day's energy, $\frac{1300}{2000} = 65\%$ comes at the time of day when glucose handling is weakest. It also falls close to sleep. Lying down soon after a large meal can worsen reflux and disturb sleep.
2. **Shift, don't just cut.** Moving 500 kcal from the late meal to breakfast and a mid-afternoon snack gives roughly 400, 600, and 800 kcal. The daily total is unchanged, so any benefit comes from timing alone.
3. **Set a practical window.** Many studies of time-restricted eating use a 8 to 10 hour window, for example 8:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. A finishing time about 3 hours before bed is a reasonable target.
4. **Handle exceptions.** Shift workers, athletes training late, and people on glucose-lowering medication have different needs. Their timing should be planned with a clinician or dietitian rather than copied from a general rule.
5. **Check the result.** Track morning energy, sleep quality, and, if available, fasting glucose over 2 to 4 weeks. If nothing improves, the timing change may matter less for that person than total diet quality.

**Key takeaway.** Meal timing is a modifiable factor that shifts how the body handles the same food. It supports eating more of the day's energy earlier and avoiding large meals close to bedtime. It does not replace attention to total calories and food quality.

### warm_cooked_food_principle (0.7s)

## The Warm, Cooked Food Principle

**Definition.** Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) holds that the Spleen governs the "transformation and transport" of food, a role loosely comparable to digestion and nutrient absorption. TCM describes the Spleen as depending on yang, a warming, activating quality. The warm, cooked food principle says that habitually eating cold, raw or iced food drains Spleen yang, while warm, cooked, easily digested meals protect it. Typical examples are congee (rice porridge), soups, stewed grains and steamed vegetables. In this view, symptoms of a "weak Spleen" include bloating, loose stools, fatigue and poor appetite after cold meals. Modern physiology has no organ or measurement that matches Spleen yang. The principle can be tested only through its practical claims about digestibility and comfort.

**Worked example.** Compare two lunches that each supply about 450 kcal.

| | Meal A | Meal B |
|---|---|---|
| Food | Raw kale salad, cold cuts, iced drink | Chicken and vegetable soup with rice |
| Serving temperature | about 5 °C | about 60 °C |
| Chewing effort | high (raw fiber) | low (softened by cooking) |

Cooking does have documented effects. Heat softens plant cell walls, gelatinizes starch and denatures proteins, so enzymes can reach these foods more easily. Cooking also destroys some heat-sensitive vitamins, such as vitamin C, and it makes others, such as lycopene in tomatoes, easier to absorb. Meal B is therefore easier to digest, which matches TCM's emphasis. Whether cold temperature itself harms digestion is not well established. Some people with irritable bowel syndrome report that cold drinks worsen their symptoms.

**Problem-solving application.** A student reports feeling bloated and sluggish after the raw salads and iced smoothies they eat at lunch. They want to test the principle on themselves. You can structure the trial like this:

1. **Baseline.** Record meals, temperature, and symptoms (bloating, stool consistency, energy on a 1–5 scale) for one week.
2. **Change one variable.** For the next week, keep total energy roughly the same, but replace the cold items with warm, cooked equivalents: oatmeal or congee at breakfast, soup or steamed vegetables at lunch.
3. **Compare.** If symptoms improve, the change helps this person. This is a personal result and does not prove TCM's explanation. If nothing changes, the principle has no practical value for them.
4. **Check the trade-offs.** Cooking leaches water-soluble nutrients into the broth, so drink the soup. Keep some raw foods if they are well tolerated, since many raw fruits and vegetables supply fiber and vitamin C. Anyone with persistent diarrhea, weight loss or blood in the stool should see a clinician. A dietary rule is no substitute for diagnosis.

This approach keeps the idea testable. The student gets a practical, low-risk experiment, and the TCM framework serves as a hypothesis to check against their own results.

### five_phase_meal_design (0.8s)

## Five-Phase Meal Design

**Definition.** Five-phase meal design plans a week of meals through two lenses at once. The first is modern nutrition: a balanced plate, mostly whole foods, adequate protein and fiber, and sensible meal timing. The second is the Five Phases framework of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), which sorts foods by color and flavor: green/sour, red/bitter, yellow/sweet, white/pungent, and black/salty. TCM also recommends warm, cooked foods and adjusting the diet to one's constitution. The lenses mostly agree in practice. Eating a variety of colors reliably increases the range of vitamins, minerals, and plant compounds you get. The claim that each flavor or color supports a specific organ system has not been established by clinical evidence, so treat it as a planning checklist for variety, not a medical prescription.

**Worked example.** Build one day for a 2,000 kcal target using the plate model: half vegetables and fruit, a quarter protein, a quarter whole grains or starch.

| Meal | Foods | Color/flavor coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast (about 450 kcal) | Warm oat porridge, walnuts, sliced pear, black sesame | White, black, sweet |
| Lunch (about 700 kcal) | Brown rice, stir-fried bok choy, tofu, carrots, a little rice vinegar | Green/sour, red, yellow |
| Dinner (about 650 kcal) | Steamed fish, sweet potato, sautéed kale with garlic, miso soup | Yellow/sweet, green, pungent, salty |
| Snack (about 200 kcal) | Red apple with almond butter | Red, sweet |

Checking the day: green appears at lunch and dinner, red in the carrots and apple, yellow in the sweet potato, white in the oats and garlic, black in the sesame. Protein is spread across all meals. Total energy is 450 + 700 + 650 + 200 = 2,000 kcal.

**Problem-solving application.** Planning a week is a constraint-satisfaction task. Follow these steps:

1. **Fix the nutritional constraints first.** Set an energy target, a protein goal (commonly 0.8–1.2 g per kg of body weight for healthy adults), and a fiber goal (about 25–35 g per day).
2. **Rotate the colors.** Do not repeat the same vegetables daily. Aim for all five colors at least several times across the week, not necessarily in every meal.
3. **Keep flavors moderate.** Salt in particular should stay within health guidelines (under about 2,300 mg of sodium per day), even though TCM lists salty as a flavor to include.
4. **Prefer warm, cooked foods where it suits you.** Cooking softens fiber and can make some nutrients, such as lycopene in tomatoes, easier to absorb. Others, such as vitamin C, decline with heat, so keep some raw foods.
5. **Adjust for constitution.** In TCM, someone who often feels cold might favor ginger tea and soups, while someone who often feels overheated might choose more steamed greens and fewer fried dishes. Check any such adjustment against real needs: a person with diabetes still tracks carbohydrates and blood glucose, whatever their TCM profile.
6. **Time meals sensibly.** Regular meals, with the largest ones earlier in the day, suit most schedules. Shift them for training, shift work, or medication.

**Check yourself.** A plan with all five colors but 3,000 kcal for a person who needs 2,000 fails the nutritional lens. Where the two lenses conflict, the measurable one takes priority.
