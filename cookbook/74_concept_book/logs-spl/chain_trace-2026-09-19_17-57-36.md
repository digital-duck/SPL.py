### gradient_descent (13.7s)

## Gradient Descent

Given a differentiable loss function $L(\theta)$ over parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^n$, gradient descent minimizes $L$ by repeatedly stepping in the direction of steepest decrease. The gradient $\nabla L(\theta)$ points toward the steepest *increase* of $L$ at $\theta$, so moving along $-\nabla L(\theta)$ locally decreases the loss fastest. This gives the update rule

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \, \nabla L(\theta_t),
$$

where $\eta > 0$ is the learning rate, controlling step size. This rule follows directly from a first-order Taylor expansion: $L(\theta_t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_t)) \approx L(\theta_t) - \eta \|\nabla L(\theta_t)\|^2$, which is less than $L(\theta_t)$ whenever the gradient is nonzero and $\eta$ is small enough — the theoretical justification for why the step reduces loss.

**Worked example.** Suppose $L(\theta) = (\theta - 3)^2$, a simple one-dimensional quadratic with minimum at $\theta = 3$. Then $\nabla L(\theta) = 2(\theta - 3)$. Starting at $\theta_0 = 0$ with $\eta = 0.1$:

$$
\theta_1 = 0 - 0.1 \cdot 2(0-3) = 0.6, \quad \theta_2 = 0.6 - 0.1\cdot 2(0.6-3) = 1.08.
$$

Each step shrinks the distance to $\theta = 3$ by a factor of $(1 - 2\eta) = 0.8$, so $\theta_t \to 3$ geometrically. If $\eta$ is too large — say $\eta = 1.1$ — the factor $|1-2\eta| = 1.2 > 1$, and the iterates diverge instead of converging. This illustrates a general fact: for a quadratic with curvature $c$ (here $c = 2$), convergence requires $\eta < 2/c$, tying step size directly to the function's second-order behavior.

**Problem-solving application.** In practice, $L$ is a high-dimensional, non-convex loss over millions of neural-network weights, and computing the exact gradient over an entire dataset is expensive. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) approximates $\nabla L(\theta)$ using a small random batch of data at each step, trading exact direction for speed; momentum and adaptive methods (e.g., Adam) further adjust the effective step using past gradients to escape shallow regions and dampen oscillation. Choosing $\eta$ remains the central practical decision: too small wastes compute, too large risks divergence, and schedules that shrink $\eta$ over training are the standard remedy.

### softmax (10.4s)

## Softmax

Softmax converts a vector of real numbers into a probability distribution — a set of nonnegative values that sum to exactly one. Given a vector $\mathbf{z} = (z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_n)$, the softmax function is defined component-wise as

$$
\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} e^{z_j}}
$$

The exponential guarantees every output is positive, even if some $z_i$ are negative, and dividing by the sum of all exponentials forces the outputs to sum to one. Because it preserves the relative ordering of the inputs while amplifying the gap between large and small values, softmax is the standard way to turn a neural network's raw output scores ("logits") into class probabilities.

**Worked example.** Suppose a classifier outputs logits $\mathbf{z} = (2.0, 1.0, 0.1)$ for three classes. Compute $e^{2.0} \approx 7.389$, $e^{1.0} \approx 2.718$, $e^{0.1} \approx 1.105$, giving a sum of about $11.212$. Dividing each term by this sum yields probabilities approximately $(0.659, 0.242, 0.099)$ — they sum to $1$, and the class with the highest logit gets the highest probability, but every class still receives some nonzero share.

**Problem-solving application.** A key numerical issue arises when logits are large: $e^{z_i}$ can overflow floating-point precision before the division ever happens. The standard fix is the *shift trick* — subtract the maximum logit from every entry before exponentiating:

$$
\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_i = \frac{e^{z_i - \max_j z_j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} e^{z_j - \max_j z_j}}
$$

This is mathematically identical to the original formula, since the shift cancels in the ratio, but it keeps the largest exponent at $e^0 = 1$, preventing overflow. When training a classifier, softmax is almost always paired with cross-entropy loss; their gradients combine into a remarkably simple expression, $\hat{y}_i - y_i$ (predicted probability minus true label), which is why the two are implemented as a single fused operation in most deep learning libraries rather than composed separately.

### training_objective (16.7s)

## Training Objective

A training objective is the specific loss function that gradient descent minimizes to fit a transformer's weights. For a language model, the dominant choice is next-token prediction: given a sequence of tokens $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T$, the model must predict each token from the ones before it. The transformer's final layer produces, for each position $t$, a vector of raw scores over the vocabulary, which the softmax function converts into a probability distribution $\hat{y}_t$. The training objective compares this distribution to the actual next token $x_{t+1}$ using cross-entropy loss:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \log \hat{y}_t[x_{t+1}]
$$

Here $\hat{y}_t[x_{t+1}]$ is the probability the model assigned to the correct next token. Because $-\log(p)$ grows large as $p \to 0$ and shrinks toward zero as $p \to 1$, this loss penalizes confident wrong predictions heavily and rewards confident correct ones. Minimizing $\mathcal{L}$ is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood the model assigns to the actual training data.

**Worked example.** Suppose the model sees "The cat sat on the" and the true next token is "mat." Softmax converts the scores at that position into probabilities, say $\hat{y}[\text{mat}] = 0.2$. The loss contribution is $-\log(0.2) \approx 1.61$. If training improves the model so that $\hat{y}[\text{mat}] = 0.8$, the loss drops to $-\log(0.8) \approx 0.22$. Summed and averaged across every position in every training sequence, this quantity is exactly what gradient descent differentiates and reduces at each update step.

**Problem-solving application.** The objective's shape determines what behavior the model learns. Because cross-entropy only rewards matching the training distribution, a model trained purely on next-token prediction will mimic patterns in its data — including biases or errors — without any built-in notion of "correctness" beyond likelihood. This is why downstream techniques like instruction tuning or reinforcement learning from human feedback modify or add to the base training objective: predicting the next token well is necessary for fluent generation, but the loss itself never distinguishes a helpful answer from a merely plausible one. Recognizing this gap is essential when reasoning about why a model behaves as it does.
