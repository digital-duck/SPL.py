### agri_rev_1740s (16.0s)

## Agri Rev 1740S

Britain's Agricultural Revolution refers to a set of changes in land ownership, crop management, and livestock breeding between roughly 1740 and 1780 that raised farm output per worker so sharply that far fewer people were needed to feed the population. The two pillars were enclosure and four-field (Norfolk) crop rotation. Enclosure acts, passed by Parliament in the thousands during the eighteenth century, converted open medieval strip-fields and shared common land into fenced, privately held plots. This let landowners consolidate holdings, drain marshland, and manage soil systematically rather than negotiating with dozens of smallholders each season. Four-field rotation—wheat, turnips, barley, clover, cycled year to year—replaced the old three-field system that had required leaving a third of land fallow. Turnips and clover restored soil nitrogen and also provided winter fodder, which meant farmers like Robert Bakewell could selectively breed sheep and cattle for meat and wool year-round instead of slaughtering most herds each autumn for lack of feed. Bakewell's Leicester sheep and Longhorn cattle roughly doubled in average weight within a few decades through controlled breeding.

Consider the mechanism concretely: before enclosure, a village of subsistence farmers might support itself with little surplus, since fallow land and dispersed strips limited yields. After enclosure and rotation, the same acreage produced a marketable surplus of grain and meat, but required fewer hands to work it—commons that had supported grazing and gleaning for the rural poor were now fenced and often consolidated into fewer large farms. This displaced smallholders and cottagers, who lost customary rights to graze animals or gather fuel on common land.

The problem-solving question this raises for economic history is one of labor supply: where did displaced rural workers go? Historians estimate England's agricultural workforce share fell from roughly 60% around 1700 to under 40% by 1800, even as population grew. That freed labor pool became available for wage work exactly as textile mills and factories began recruiting in the 1760s–1780s. Without enclosure's surplus food and displaced labor arriving together, the factory system emerging in the next section would have had crops but no workers to spare.

### seven_years_war_1756 (14.6s)

## Seven Years War 1756

By the mid-eighteenth century, Britain and France had fought three wars in seventy years without settling who would dominate global trade, North America, and India. The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) resolved that question decisively. It began in Europe as a contest between Prussia's Frederick the Great and an Austrian-led coalition backed by France and Russia, but the war's real stakes lay overseas: British and French forces clashed in the Ohio Valley, on the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec, in the Caribbean sugar islands, off the coast of West Africa, and across the Indian subcontinent, where Robert Clive's victory at Plassey in 1757 gave the British East India Company effective control of Bengal. Because fighting spanned five continents, historians often call it the first true "world war."

The outcome, sealed by the 1763 Treaty of Paris, transformed the balance of global power. Britain took all of French Canada, Spanish Florida, and most French territory east of the Mississippi, while consolidating dominance in India — laying the foundation for the British Empire's nineteenth-century reach. France retained a few Caribbean sugar islands, valuable but strategically diminished, and abandoned its North American ambitions almost entirely.

Victory came at a staggering financial cost to both sides, but France's collapse was more consequential. The war roughly doubled France's national debt, pushing the monarchy toward chronic insolvency that no minister — Turgot, Necker, or Calonne — could fully repair. This fiscal crisis, worsened by France's decision to fund the American Revolution partly for revenge against Britain, forced Louis XVI to convene the Estates-General in 1789 to address bankruptcy — the meeting that ignited the French Revolution. Britain, though also deeply indebted, financed its debt through a more stable system of parliamentary taxation and credit, and instead faced a narrower crisis: taxing American colonists to cover war costs, which triggered the resentments leading to the American Revolution.

A useful problem-solving exercise: given a state's war debt roughly doubles and its tax-collection system remains rigid and unreformed, predict which political outcome is more likely — incremental reform or systemic collapse. The 1756–1763 war demonstrates how a shared financial shock can produce sharply divergent political trajectories depending on a state's institutional capacity to adapt.

## Adam Smith 1776

In 1776, Scottish philosopher Adam Smith published *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*, a work written in the shadow of two very different upheavals. The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) had left Britain with a vast, debt-financed empire and a pressing need to rethink how national wealth was actually created — not merely seized through colonial monopoly and war. At the same time, the Agricultural Revolution of the 1740s had already shown, in the English countryside, what Smith would generalize into theory: that specialization and improved methods could multiply output from the same land and labor. Smith took these lessons and built a systematic argument against mercantilism, the prevailing doctrine that a nation's wealth was measured by the gold and silver it hoarded through tightly controlled trade.

Smith's central claims were threefold. First, division of labor — breaking production into specialized tasks — dramatically increases output; his famous example of a pin factory showed that ten workers, each performing one step of pin-making, could produce thousands of times more than ten generalists working alone. Second, individuals pursuing their own self-interest in a competitive market, guided by what Smith called an "invisible hand," tend to allocate resources efficiently without central direction. Third, government should therefore step back from micromanaging trade — abolishing tariffs, subsidies, and monopolies — and let supply and demand set prices and wages.

The practical test of these ideas came seventy years later. British landowners had long been protected by the Corn Laws (tariffs on imported grain that kept domestic prices high), a textbook mercantilist policy. By the 1840s, industrialists and reformers invoked Smith's free-trade logic directly against this protectionism, arguing that cheaper imported grain would lower food costs, free up capital for manufacturing, and expand markets abroad. Parliament repealed the Corn Laws in 1846, marking Britain's decisive turn toward free trade and industrial capitalism — the opening event of the era this book traces next. Smith's 1776 treatise thus did not just describe markets; it supplied the intellectual justification that policymakers cited, sometimes word for word, when dismantling a century-old system of trade protection.
