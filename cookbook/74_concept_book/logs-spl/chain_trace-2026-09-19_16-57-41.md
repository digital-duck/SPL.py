## Vector

A vector is an ordered list of $n$ real numbers, written as $\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \rangle$. Mathematically, we consider vectors as elements of the $n$-dimensional Euclidean space, denoted $\mathbb{R}^n$. In computational linguistics and machine learning, a vector is used to translate abstract data—such as the meaning of a word (a token)—into a numerical format that algorithms can process. These representations are known as *embeddings*. Each dimension within the vector often corresponds to a specific feature or latent property of the data set (e.g., whether the word relates to nature, time, or action).

To determine the relationship between two vectors, $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, we rely on fundamental concepts from linear algebra: the dot product and the cosine similarity. The **dot product** calculates a scalar value that measures how much the two vectors point in the same direction. For two $n$-dimensional vectors $\mathbf{A} = \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$ and $\mathbf{B} = \langle b_1, \dots, b_n \rangle$, the dot product is defined as:

$$\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i$$

## Embedding

An embedding is a foundational concept that solves the problem of representing discrete, high-dimensional categorical data—such as words or unique identifiers—within a continuous, low-dimensional vector space ($\mathbb{R}^d$). Mathematically, an embedding $E$ is represented as a matrix where each row corresponds to a token (or word) in the vocabulary $V$. If $|V|$ is the size of the vocabulary and we choose a dimension $d$, the embedding matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times d}$ acts as an injective function $\text{Emb}: V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$. Crucially, the goal of this mapping is not merely to assign coordinates but to ensure that the geometric relationships between vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ reflect the semantic and syntactic relationships between the tokens they represent.

Suppose we have a vocabulary $V = \{\text{"King"}, \text{"Queen"}, \text{"Man"}, \text{"Woman"}\}$, and we map these four tokens into $\mathbb{R}^5$. The embedding matrix $E$ stores the coordinates for each token. Because this representation is dense, small changes in the input word (like changing "man" to "woman") should result in predictable, continuous movements in the vector space.
