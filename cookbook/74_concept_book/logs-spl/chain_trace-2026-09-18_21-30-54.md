## Rise Of Hitler 1933

Imagine a classroom where the teacher steps down, and instead of picking someone by vote, a new rule quietly appears: whoever is in charge can now cancel votes entirely. That's close to what happened in Germany in January 1933.

Adolf Hitler didn't seize power in a midnight raid — he was legally appointed Chancellor, the top government job, by the aging president. Germany's democracy, called the Weimar Republic, was struggling: high unemployment, political street fights, and citizens desperate for stability. Hitler promised order.

Then came the fast part. Within weeks, a fire damaged the parliament building. Hitler's government used it as an excuse to pass emergency laws suspending civil liberties. Soon after, a law let his cabinet make laws without parliament's approval at all — legally ending the checks that had kept one person from ruling alone.

This matters because it shows how a democracy can be dismantled using its *own* legal tools, one emergency measure at a time, rather than by breaking the rules outright.

**Now you try:** Ask an adult: what rule exists in your school, club, or country that's meant to stop one person from having *all* the power? Why do you think that rule was made?

### remilitarization_1936 (0.0s)

## Remilitarization 1936

Imagine you promised a friend you'd never step onto their side of the yard again after a big fight, and you agreed a grown-up would keep watch. What happens if you take one tiny step over the line — and nobody says anything?

In 1936, Hitler marched German soldiers into the Rhineland, a region Germany had promised to keep empty of troops after World War I. It was a direct, public rule-breaking. Britain and France, the "grown-ups" watching, did nothing. Two years later, Germany absorbed Austria entirely — again, no response.

Here's the intuition: broken promises are cheap to make and cheap to ignore — but only the first few times. Each unpunished violation changes what everyone believes is *possible* to get away with next. This is the same pattern behind why small compromises in any system — a budget rule, a game's rules, a safety check — tend to escalate once the first one goes unenforced.

**Now you try:** Think of a rule at home or school. What might happen if it was broken once and nobody noticed?

### invasion_of_poland_1939 (0.0s)

## Invasion Of Poland 1939

Picture Hitler's generals studying maps in 1939. Poland sits between Germany and the Soviet Union — flat, open, no mountains to hide behind. Germany had spent three years rebuilding its army (from remilitarizing the Rhineland in 1936), and now that army needed a target.

Here's the intuition: dictators who rebuild weapons eventually use them, because armies that never fight don't prove anything to anyone — least of all to the dictator who built them. On September 1, 1939, German tanks and planes attacked Poland in a fast, coordinated strike — a new kind of warfare using speed and combined forces, later called *Blitzkrieg* ("lightning war").

This time, the rest of Europe didn't just watch. Britain and France had promised to protect Poland. Two days later, on September 3, 1939, they declared war on Germany — turning one invasion into a world war.

**Now you try:** Draw a simple timeline with three dots: 1936 (rebuild army) → Sept 1, 1939 (invade Poland) → Sept 3, 1939 (world war begins). What do you notice about how each event makes the next one almost unavoidable?

### fall_of_france_1940 (0.0s)

## Fall Of France 1940

Picture a wall of dominoes — but instead of falling one after another slowly, someone knocks them all down in six weeks. That's France in 1940. Germany had already conquered Poland with a new kind of fast, coordinated attack — tanks, planes, and radios working together instead of armies marching in slow lines. Now Germany aimed that same speed at France, sweeping through a forest everyone thought was "too thick" for tanks, and suddenly appearing behind France's strongest defenses.

Why does this feel surprising? Because France had built a huge wall of forts (the Maginot Line) expecting a slow, old-fashioned war. Speed beat strength. Trapped British and French soldiers had to escape by boat from the beaches of Dunkirk — hundreds of small ships ferrying soldiers home. By June, France gave up.

**Minimal formalisation:** think of it as a chain: surprise route $\rightarrow$ broken defense $\rightarrow$ trapped army $\rightarrow$ retreat $\rightarrow$ surrender. Each link causes the next.

**Now you try:** Can you think of another situation — a game, a story — where going around an obstacle beat trying to smash through it?

### battle_of_britain_1940 (0.0s)

## Battle Of Britain 1940

Picture two chess players, but one can only see a few squares of the board at a time. In summer 1940, Germany planned to invade Britain — but first it needed control of the sky. So Nazi bombers came in waves, trying to destroy Britain's air force on the ground. The trick was: Britain had radar towers watching the coast, invisible eyes that saw the enemy coming before it arrived.

That's the intuition worth sitting with: information changes a fight before a single shot is fired. A smaller force that *knows where to look* can outmaneuver a bigger force that's searching blind. British fighter pilots didn't patrol the whole sky hoping to get lucky — radar told them exactly when and where to rise and meet the bombers.

By September, the Luftwaffe had lost too many planes and pilots to keep going. Germany quietly shelved its invasion plan. It was the first time Hitler's war machine had been stopped.

Now you try: if you were defending a castle and could see attackers coming from far away, would you spread your guards everywhere, or wait and send them exactly where needed?

### operation_barbarossa_1941 (8.7s)

## Operation Barbarossa 1941

Imagine you've won a big game, but instead of celebrating, you immediately pick a fight with someone twice your size — while your first opponent is still on the field. That's what Germany did in June 1941, launching a surprise attack on the Soviet Union while Britain remained unbeaten across the Channel.

Why does this feel like a mistake even before we check a map? Because fighting two opponents at once means splitting your strength in half — or worse. Armies, like people, have only so much energy, so many soldiers, so much fuel. Germany hoped a fast knockout would let it beat the Soviets before Britain could recover. But the Soviet Union was enormous — vast distances, brutal winters, huge population.

We can think of this as a resource-allocation problem: total military capacity $C$ must now cover two fronts, so each front receives less than $C$ instead of the full amount. Splitting a fixed resource across more competing demands shrinks what's available anywhere.

Now you try: if you had a fixed amount of allowance to spend, would you start a second shopping trip before finishing the first?
