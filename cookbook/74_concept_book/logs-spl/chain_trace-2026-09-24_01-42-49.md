## Parent Structure

**Definition.** In Sage, every mathematical object $x$ carries a distinguished attribute $x.parent()$, an object $P$ called its *parent*, which is itself an instance of a subclass of the `Parent` class in the category-theoretic hierarchy `sage.structure.parent`. The parent encodes the algebraic structure — the set together with its operations — to which $x$ is asserted to belong: $\mathbb{Z}$ (`ZZ`), $\mathbb{Q}$ (`QQ`), the finite field $\mathbb{F}_7$ (`GF(7)`), the symbolic ring `SR`, a polynomial ring $R[x]$, a matrix space $\mathrm{Mat}_{m\times n}(R)$, and so on. Formally, Sage's coercion model realizes a fragment of category theory: each parent $P$ is associated to a category $\mathcal{C}$ (rings, fields, groups, modules, …), and arithmetic between elements of parents $P$ and $Q$ is only well-defined once a *coercion morphism* $P \to R$ or $Q \to R$ into some common parent $R$ has been established, via `sage.structure.coerce`.

**Theorem (uniqueness and canonicity of parents).** Sage's parent structure satisfies a *unique parent axiom*: for any given mathematical structure with fixed defining data, the parent object is a singleton up to `==` and often `is` (via a global cache, `UniqueRepresentation`). That is, `GF(7) is GF(7)` returns `True`, and two elements $a, b$ with $a.parent() \equiv b.parent()$ (structurally identical constructor arguments) are guaranteed interoperable without ambiguity. *Proof sketch:* `UniqueRepresentation` overrides `__classcall__` to hash the constructor arguments and consult a weak-value cache; if a parent with identical arguments already exists, the cached instance is returned instead of instantiating anew. This guarantees that parent identity (`is`), and not merely equality (`==`), can be used as a fast structural test — an essential invariant for the coercion model's performance, since exact structural comparison would otherwise require re-deriving canonical forms at each operation. $\blacksquare$

**Literature context.** The design descends from Magma's type/parent system and from Axiom/Aldor's categorical hierarchy, and is documented formally in the Sage coercion model reference and in Bosma–Cannon's original design papers for computer algebra category hierarches. What remains open, and is an active area of Sage development, is the completeness and soundness of the coercion *graph*: with an increasing number of parents (p-adics, number fields, quotient rings, tensor products), the discovery of coercion paths becomes a nontrivial search problem, and cases of ambiguous or missing coercions (e.g., between two number fields with no canonical embedding) surface regularly as reported issues.

**Suggested investigation.** Construct three parents — $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, and $\mathbb{F}_5[x]$ — and systematically test which pairs support automatic coercion of a nonzero-degree element via `x.parent().has_coerce_map_from(y.parent())`. Extend to $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$ and observe the failure mode when no canonical map exists. Trace the actual coercion path found (if any) using `sage.structure.coerce.py`'s `_richcmp_` and `get_action` internals with verbose coercion tracing enabled (`Parent._coerce_map_from_` debugging hooks).

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state precisely which coercion tests succeeded and failed, give the categorical justification (ring homomorphism existence) for each success, and for each failure explain why no canonical morphism exists (as opposed to one merely not being implemented) — distinguishing mathematical obstruction from implementation gap.

## Symbolic Variable

**Definition.** A symbolic variable is an element $x$ of a free commutative algebra (typically $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, $\mathbb{R}[x]$, or a polynomial ring in several indeterminates over a base ring $R$) that is *uninterpreted*: no evaluation map $\phi: R[x] \to R$ is fixed a priori. Formally, `var('x')` in a computer algebra system (CAS) such as SageMath or a symbolic-manipulation library instantiates a generator of a free $R$-algebra, distinguished from a *numeric* variable — a name bound to a single element of $R$ — precisely by the absence of this binding. The symbol $x$ is not "a number we don't know yet" in the probabilistic sense; it is a formal placeholder satisfying only the axioms of the ring it generates (commutativity, distributivity, etc.), nothing more.

**Theorem (Universal property of the polynomial ring).** Let $R$ be a commutative ring. The polynomial ring $R[x]$, together with the inclusion $\iota: R \hookrightarrow R[x]$ and the distinguished element $x \in R[x]$, satisfies: for every commutative $R$-algebra $A$ and every element $a \in A$, there exists a unique $R$-algebra homomorphism $\varepsilon_a: R[x] \to A$ with $\varepsilon_a(x) = a$.

*Proof.* Existence: define $\varepsilon_a\left(\sum_{i=0}^n c_i x^i\right) = \sum_{i=0}^n c_i a^i$; this is well-defined since every element of $R[x]$ has a unique representation as a finite sum of monomials with coefficients in $R$, and one checks directly that $\varepsilon_a$ respects addition and multiplication because $A$ is commutative. Uniqueness: any $R$-algebra homomorphism $\psi$ with $\psi(x) = a$ must satisfy $\psi(c_i x^i) = c_i \psi(x)^i = c_i a^i$ by $R$-linearity and multiplicativity, forcing $\psi = \varepsilon_a$ on every monomial, hence on all of $R[x]$ by additivity. $\blacksquare$

This is precisely why `var('x')` grants access to "every number at once": $x$ is the *generic point* of $\mathbb{A}^1_R$, and any specific number $a$ is recovered by the evaluation homomorphism $\varepsilon_a$ — substitution. Algebra with $x$ (simplification, factoring, differentiation as a formal $R$-derivation) is computation performed once, in $R[x]$, that commutes with every subsequent specialization $\varepsilon_a$; this is the sense in which "algebra precedes arithmetic."

**Literature context.** The universal property above is standard commutative algebra (Atiyah–Macdonald, Lang); its computational incarnation is the subject of computer algebra (von zur Gathen & Gerhard). What remains genuinely active research is efficient *representation* of symbolic expressions (DAG sharing, canonical forms modulo associativity/commutativity — the word problem for expression equivalence is undecidable in general once transcendental functions are admitted, per Richardson's theorem) and the interplay between symbolic and numeric computation (interval arithmetic, hybrid symbolic-numeric algorithms).

**Suggested investigation.** Implement $\varepsilon_a$ explicitly in a CAS (Sage or SymPy) for a nontrivial $A$ (e.g. $A = M_n(R)$, matrices) and verify the homomorphism property computationally on random polynomials and matrices; then explore where naive symbolic simplification fails to detect $A$-specific identities (e.g. Cayley–Hamilton) that a numeric substitution would trivially satisfy for tested values but that require the ring structure of $A$ to prove in general.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the universal property precisely, give the proof, then present the computational experiment with explicit code and output, and discuss at least one case where symbolic and numeric approaches to the same identity diverge in what they can certify (numeric: verified at sampled points; symbolic: proved for all $a \in A$).

### exact_rational (0.0s)

## Exact Rational

**Definition.** The type $\mathbb{Q}_{\text{exact}}$ represents a rational number as a pair $(n, d) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ with $\gcd(n,d) = 1$, interpreted as the value $n/d \in \mathbb{Q}$. Arithmetic operations are defined by the field structure of $\mathbb{Q}$ and a normalization map $\rho: \mathbb{Z} \times (\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}) \to \mathbb{Q}_{\text{exact}}$,
$$
\rho(a,b) = \left(\frac{a/g}{\,\text{sgn}(b)\,},\ \frac{|b|/g}{1}\right), \qquad g = \gcd(a,b).
$$
Decimal or floating-point representations are not part of the type; they arise only under an explicit coercion $\delta_k: \mathbb{Q}_{\text{exact}} \to \mathbb{R}$ truncated to $k$ digits, applied on output, never internally.

**Theorem (closure under the four operations).** Let $x = n_1/d_1$, $y = n_2/d_2$ be in lowest terms. Then
$$
x+y = \rho(n_1 d_2 + n_2 d_1,\ d_1 d_2), \quad x \cdot y = \rho(n_1 n_2,\ d_1 d_2), \quad x/y = \rho(n_1 d_2,\ n_2 d_1)\ (y\neq 0),
$$
and each result, after normalization by $\rho$, is again an element of $\mathbb{Q}_{\text{exact}}$ with a *unique* representative pair. Uniqueness follows immediately from $\gcd(n,d)=1$ combined with a fixed sign convention on $d>0$: any two coprime pairs representing the same rational differ by a unit $(-1,-1)$, which the sign convention eliminates.

*Proof sketch.* Closure is the standard verification that $\mathbb{Q}$ is a field under the usual fraction arithmetic; the substance here is that $\rho$ is total and computable via Euclid's algorithm, so no operation can silently leave lowest-terms form or introduce rounding. Uniqueness of the coprime representative is the fundamental theorem of arithmetic applied to $n/g, d/g$. $\blacksquare$

**Literature context.** This construction is the algebraic-datatype analogue of the classical field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}$ (Hungerford, *Algebra*, Ch. III); its computational realization is the subject of exact/symbolic arithmetic systems (GMP's `mpq`, Python's `fractions.Fraction`, Lisp's rational tower). The open engineering question is not existence but *complexity*: repeated addition without intermediate GCD reduction causes numerator/denominator bit-length to grow, in the worst case, linearly per operation, giving quadratic-or-worse blowup over $k$ operations — a phenomenon studied under "coefficient growth" in computer algebra (von zur Gathen & Gerhard, *Modern Computer Algebra*). Whether a given implementation reduces eagerly (every operation) or lazily (only before comparison/output) is a genuine design tradeoff, not a settled matter.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement $\mathbb{Q}_{\text{exact}}$ two ways — eager reduction vs. lazy reduction — and empirically measure numerator bit-length growth over a random walk of $10^4$ additions/multiplications on rationals with small initial denominators (e.g. summing $1/1, 1/2, \dots$ as in the harmonic series). Compare wall-clock time against `Fraction` in an existing library.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the normalization invariant precisely, prove it is preserved by each operation, and present the growth-rate measurement with a fitted asymptotic (linear vs. superlinear) alongside the theoretical worst-case bound, explicitly flagging any gap between the two.

## Symbolic Expression

**Definition.** In Sage, the Symbolic Ring $SR$ is a commutative ring whose elements are formal expression trees built from symbolic variables (see [[symbolic_variable]]), numeric literals, and function/operator nodes (arithmetic operators, elementary and special functions), closed under composition. Formally, $SR$ is the free structure generated by an alphabet of symbols $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ under the signature $\Sigma = \{+, \cdot, -, /, {}^{\wedge}, \sin, \exp, \log, \Gamma, \ldots\}$, modulo no relations beyond the syntactic ones imposed by the parser — i.e., $SR$ is (up to normalization by Maxima/Pynac's simplification routines) the term algebra $T_\Sigma(X)$ over variable set $X$. An element $e \in SR$ carries its construction history exactly: $e = f(g(x), h(y))$ is stored as a directed acyclic graph (a *parent structure*, see [[parent_structure]]) with $f, g, h \in \Sigma$ and leaves $x, y \in X$, not as a floating-point approximation of its value.

**Theorem (structural uniqueness up to evaluation order).** Two symbolic expressions $e_1, e_2 \in SR$ are syntactically distinct in general even when they are semantically equal as functions of their free variables (e.g., $\sin^2(x) + \cos^2(x)$ and $1$), because $SR$ performs no automatic theorem-proving simplification — only canonicalization at the level of associativity/commutativity normal forms maintained by the underlying CAS kernel (Pynac, itself a C++ reimplementation of GiNaC's expression-tree engine). Consequently equality testing in $SR$ splits into two distinct operations: `bool(e1 == e2)`, which invokes symbolic simplification and possibly numerical sampling as a heuristic, and `(e1 - e2).is_zero()` or `.simplify_full()`, which attempts exact reduction. Neither is a decision procedure in general, since the equivalence problem for expressions involving $\exp$, $\log$, and algebraic operations is undecidable (Richardson's theorem, 1968) even for finite closed forms.

**Literature context.** Richardson's undecidability result is the standard reference bounding what any symbolic engine — Sage, Mathematica, Maple — can guarantee about expression equivalence; it explains why $SR$ deliberately exposes the raw expression tree rather than pretending to a canonical form. The design of Pynac/GiNaC follows the term-rewriting and computer-algebra tradition surveyed in von zur Gathen–Gerhard's *Modern Computer Algebra*; open questions remain in simplification heuristics (which rewrite rules to apply, in what order, to approach — never guarantee — a "simplest" form) and in efficient common-subexpression sharing across large expression DAGs, an active concern in symbolic differentiation and automatic differentiation research.

**Suggested investigation.** Construct a family of expressions known to be equal (e.g., trigonometric identities, telescoping sums, or instances of Ramanujan-type series) and empirically test: (1) does `bool(e1==e2)` succeed, (2) does `.simplify_full()` succeed, (3) how does runtime scale with expression-tree depth? Compare Sage's success rate against a direct call to Maxima and to a CAS using a different simplification strategy (e.g., Mathematica's `FullSimplify`).

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should report the DAG size (node count) of each test expression, distinguish failures due to timeout from failures due to genuine simplification incompleteness, and state explicitly which equivalences were verified exactly versus only numerically (interval sampling), since this distinction is the crux of what "symbolic" guarantees mean in practice.

## Exact Linear Algebra

**Definition.** Let $\mathbb{F}$ be an exact field of characteristic $0$ implemented with unbounded-precision rational or algebraic arithmetic (as with `QQ` or `QQbar` in Sage). Exact linear algebra is the study of matrix computations — row reduction, kernel and image computation, characteristic and minimal polynomials, eigenvalues, canonical forms — performed entirely within $\mathbb{F}$, so that every intermediate and final quantity is a provably correct element of $\mathbb{F}$, not a floating-point approximation subject to rounding error or ill-conditioning.

**Theorem (Correctness and complexity of exact Gaussian elimination).** Let $A \in \mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}$ with entries of bit-length at most $h$. There is an algorithm computing the reduced row-echelon form (RREF) of $A$ using exact rational arithmetic in time polynomial in $n$ and $h$, and the RREF is unique and exact.

*Proof sketch.* Fraction-free Gaussian elimination (the Bareiss algorithm) performs elimination by tracking determinants of leading principal minors, replacing each pivot division by an exact division guaranteed to have zero remainder — a consequence of the Sylvester identity relating successive minors. This controls *intermediate expression swell*: naive fraction arithmetic on rationals can cause numerator/denominator bit-length to grow exponentially in $n$ without such control, since each elimination step multiplies denominators. Bareiss's method keeps the bit-length of intermediate entries polynomially bounded (specifically $O(n h)$ under mild assumptions), giving an overall complexity polynomial in $n$ and $h$, in contrast to naive fraction-arithmetic elimination which is correct but not polynomially bounded without such control. Uniqueness of RREF is a standard linear-algebra fact independent of the field's representation. $\blacksquare$

**Literature context.** The complexity landscape for exact linear algebra is still evolving. Bareiss's 1968 algorithm remains foundational, but faster asymptotic methods exist: Storjohann's work reduces exact linear system solving and rank/kernel computation to essentially the same complexity as matrix multiplication ($O(n^\omega)$ arithmetic operations with bit-complexity overhead), and p-adic/Chinese Remainder lifting techniques (Dixon's algorithm, 1982) solve $Ax=b$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ by lifting a solution modulo a prime and reconstructing rationals via continued fractions, avoiding intermediate swell entirely. Exact eigenvalue computation is fundamentally different from the numerical case: eigenvalues of a rational matrix are algebraic numbers, roots of the characteristic polynomial, and are generally *not* expressible by radicals for $n \geq 5$ (Abel–Ruffini), so "exact" eigenvalues require symbolic representation via algebraic number fields (e.g. `QQbar`) or isolating intervals with certified refinement (as in `AlgebraicRealField`). An open and actively researched question is finding the practical complexity crossover: for which matrix sizes and sparsity patterns do certified numerical methods (interval arithmetic, iterative refinement) outperform genuinely symbolic ones, and how do modular/CRT techniques scale for very large sparse systems relevant to lattice cryptography and integer programming.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement (in Sage or another CAS) naive fraction-arithmetic Gaussian elimination and Bareiss's fraction-free variant on a family of $n\times n$ rational matrices with controlled entry size; empirically measure the growth in numerator/denominator bit-length as a function of $n$ for each method, and compare wall-clock time against Sage's built-in RREF (which uses more sophisticated modular methods).

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state the theoretical bit-length bound for Bareiss's method, present measured bit-length and timing data confirming or contradicting it, and discuss at what matrix size (if any) naive elimination becomes practically infeasible due to swell.

## Simplification

**Definition.** Let $E$ be the set of symbolic expression trees over a field $\mathbb{K}$ (typically $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{Q}(x_1,\dots,x_n)$) built from constants, symbols, and operators $\{+,-,\times,\div,{}^{\wedge}, \sin,\cos,\exp,\log,\dots\}$. A *simplification strategy* is a rewriting relation $\to_S \subseteq E \times E$ generated by a finite (or schema-indexed) set of identities $S$, each of the form $L(\vec x) \equiv R(\vec x)$ valid on a specified domain. `simplify_full(e)` computes a representative of the equivalence class $[e]_{\to_S^*}$ under the reflexive-transitive closure of $\to_S$, subject to a termination order (e.g., a reduction ordering on tree size or a Knuth–Bendix weight) that guarantees $\to_S$ terminates and, ideally, confluence, so the result is canonical. Separately, `is_zero(e)` decides membership of $e$ in the ideal-theoretic or transcendental sense: whether $e \equiv 0$ as a *function* (not merely as a syntax tree) on its domain — the semantic question underlying the *equation object* built by `expr == 0`, which is a **proposition**, not yet a **truth value**.

**Theorem (undecidability of the zero-equivalence problem).** For the class of elementary expressions built from $+,\times,\exp,\sin$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, the predicate "$e \equiv 0$" is undecidable in general (Richardson's theorem, 1968), even though it is decidable for purely algebraic (polynomial/rational) subexpressions via Gröbner basis membership testing, and for expressions involving only $\exp$ and polynomials via Ritt–Risch-style differential algebra when no integer-relation ambiguity (e.g. $e^{i\pi}+1$) is present.

*Proof sketch.* Richardson embeds an instance of the halting problem (or, more directly, undecidability of integer-linear-combination vanishing, $\sum n_i \log p_i = 0$) into deciding whether a specific closed-form expression involving $\sin$, $\exp$, absolute value, and integers is identically zero on $\mathbb{R}$. Reduction preserves computability class, so no algorithm can decide zero-equivalence for the full elementary class. $\blacksquare$

**Literature context.** `simplify_full()` is therefore necessarily a *heuristic normalizer*, not a decision procedure — it applies confluent, terminating rewrite systems where available (polynomial normal form via distributive/collect laws, trigonometric reduction via Chebyshev/product-to-sum identities, log/exp collapsing under branch-cut side conditions) and falls back to syntactic canonicalization elsewhere. `is_zero()` typically layers several decidable subroutines — CAS Gröbner-basis ideal membership (Buchberger 1965), Risch-style differential-field zero tests (Risch 1969), and numerical/interval sampling as a fast heuristic pre-filter (never a proof) — before returning "unknown." Open problems include: tight decidable fragments beyond purely algebraic/exp-log towers (e.g. mixed trigonometric-exponential with algebraic relations), and confluence certificates for domain-restricted trig rewriting under branch assumptions.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a minimal rewrite system for trigonometric identities (double-angle, product-to-sum) as a term-rewriting system; prove termination via a weight function and test confluence on critical pairs (Knuth–Bendix completion). Separately, construct expressions where `is_zero()` returns `unknown` (e.g. $e^{i\pi} + \cos(0)$-style constructions with hidden algebraic relations among logarithms of primes) and characterize which decidable subroutine, if any, resolves them.

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state precisely which subclass of expressions the implemented rewrite system decides confluently, give the termination order and a confluence proof (or counterexample), and report empirically how often `is_zero()` returns `unknown` versus a definite answer on a benchmark corpus, distinguishing numerical near-zero false positives from true undecidable instances.

## Spl Verifier Ladder

**Definition.** The SPL verifier ladder is a staged verification pipeline $\mathcal{L} = (V_0, V_1, \ldots, V_n)$ in which a candidate mathematical claim $\phi$, generated by a language model or search procedure, is passed through successively stronger checkers $V_i$. Each $V_i$ either rejects $\phi$, passes it unchanged to $V_{i+1}$, or — for the terminal rungs — stamps $\phi$ with a certificate $c_i \in \{\texttt{numeric\_checked}, \texttt{symbolic\_verified}, \texttt{machine\_verified}\}$. Sage occupies the symbolic rung: given $\phi$ expressed as a polynomial identity, ODE, or algebraic relation, Sage's computer algebra system (built on Singular, PARI/GP, GAP, and Maxima) either proves $\phi$ by symbolic normalization — reducing both sides of an equation to a canonical form and checking syntactic equality of the normal forms — or produces a counterexample via randomized evaluation over a finite field, following the Schwartz–Zippel paradigm.

**Theorem (soundness of the symbolic rung).** Let $\phi: f(x_1,\ldots,x_k) = g(x_1,\ldots,x_k)$ be a claimed polynomial identity over a field $F$ with $\deg f, \deg g \le d$. If Sage's `.simplify_full()` / `.is_zero()` normalization certifies $f - g \equiv 0$ as a formal polynomial, then $\phi$ holds identically on $F^k$. Conversely, if Sage instead evaluates $f-g$ at $m$ random points drawn from a subset $S \subset F$, $|S| \ge dm/\epsilon$, and finds no zero, then $\Pr[\phi \text{ false yet all evaluations zero}] \le \epsilon$ by the Schwartz–Zippel lemma. Thus the certificate `machine_verified` produced by full symbolic normalization is *sound with probability 1*, while a certificate from evaluation-only checking is sound with high probability, not certainty — a distinction the ladder must track explicitly, since collapsing the two into one label overstates the guarantee.

**Literature context.** The general question of when a computer algebra system's zero-test is complete (always terminates and correctly decides $f\equiv 0$) is the classical polynomial identity testing (PIT) problem; deterministic polynomial-time PIT for general arithmetic circuits remains open and is closely tied to circuit lower bounds (Kabanets–Impagliazzo 2004). SPL's ladder sidesteps full PIT by restricting rung inputs to expression classes (rational functions, hypergeometric sums, D-finite sequences) where Zeilberger's algorithm and Gosper's algorithm give *complete* decision procedures — this is the substantive research boundary: which sub-languages of the generated claims admit complete symbolic certification versus only probabilistic evaluation.

**Suggested investigation.** Take a corpus of $N$ generated identities (e.g., binomial-sum claims in the Ramanujan-style spotlight material). Classify each by expression class, run Sage's `bool()`/`simplify_full()` and separately a Schwartz–Zippel evaluator with fixed $\epsilon$, and measure: (a) the fraction where symbolic and evaluation-based verdicts disagree, (b) wall-clock cost of symbolic normalization as a function of $\deg f$, (c) whether disagreements correlate with a specific generator failure mode (sign errors, off-by-one indices).

**Report-writing note.** The write-up should state, per identity class, which certificate level was achievable and why, report the empirical PIT failure/false-positive rate against the theoretical $\epsilon$ bound, and give at least one worked counterexample where evaluation-only checking would have wrongly certified a false identity.
