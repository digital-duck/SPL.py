### scalar_multiplication (0.0s)

## Scalar Multiplication

A vector carries two pieces of information: a direction and a magnitude. Scalar multiplication changes the magnitude — and possibly reverses the direction — while leaving the line of action untouched. Formally, given a vector $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n)$ and a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the product $c\mathbf{v}$ is defined componentwise:

$$
c\mathbf{v} = (cv_1, cv_2, \ldots, cv_n)
$$

Geometrically, $|c|$ stretches or shrinks the vector's length by that factor, and the sign of $c$ determines orientation: $c > 0$ preserves direction, $c < 0$ reverses it, and $c = 0$ collapses the vector to the zero vector regardless of its original direction.

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{v} = (3, -2)$. Then $2\mathbf{v} = (6, -4)$ — same direction, twice the length. And $-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v} = (-1.5, 1)$ — opposite direction, half the length. Plotting all three on the $xy$-plane confirms they lie on the same line through the origin, differing only in how far along that line (and in which direction) each point sits.

Scalar multiplication obeys two algebraic properties that make it indispensable for building more complex vector operations: distributivity over vector addition, $c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$, and distributivity over scalar addition, $(c + d)\mathbf{v} = c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{v}$. These properties are what make vector spaces "linear" — they guarantee that scaling and combining vectors interact predictably, which is the foundation for concepts like linear combinations and linear independence introduced later.

**Problem-solving application.** Scalar multiplication is the tool for normalizing vectors — rescaling a vector to unit length without changing its direction. Given $\mathbf{v} = (3, 4)$ with magnitude $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, the unit vector in the same direction is

$$
\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{5}(3,4) = (0.6, 0.8)
$$

This technique appears constantly in applications: normalizing feature vectors before computing cosine similarity in machine learning, converting a force vector into a pure direction for physics simulations, or rescaling pixel-intensity vectors in image processing. Whenever a problem asks "which way does this point, independent of how large it is?", scalar multiplication by $1/\|\mathbf{v}\|$ is the operation that answers it.

### vector_addition (0.0s)

## Vector Addition

Vector addition is the operation that combines two vectors in a vector space $V$ to produce a third vector, also in $V$. Formally, addition is a function $+: V \times V \to V$, so for any $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$, the sum $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is guaranteed to be a member of $V$ — this closure property is what makes $V$ a self-contained algebraic structure rather than just a collection of objects. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, addition is defined componentwise:

$$
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n) + (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n) = (u_1+v_1,\ u_2+v_2,\ \dots,\ u_n+v_n)
$$

This operation satisfies commutativity ($\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}$), associativity ($(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w})$), an identity element $\mathbf{0}$, and an inverse $-\mathbf{u}$ for every vector — the axioms that, together with scalar multiplication, define a vector space.

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{u} = (2, -1, 3)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (-1, 4, 0)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Adding componentwise:

$$
\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (2 + (-1),\ -1 + 4,\ 3 + 0) = (1, 3, 3)
$$

Geometrically, this is the "tip-to-tail" rule: place the tail of $\mathbf{v}$ at the tip of $\mathbf{u}$, and the sum is the vector from the origin to the new tip. Equivalently, it's the diagonal of the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

**Problem-solving application.** Vector addition is the mechanism behind superposition: a physical displacement, force, or velocity that results from multiple simultaneous influences is the vector sum of the individual contributions. Suppose a drone experiences wind pushing it by $\mathbf{w} = (3, 1, 0)$ meters per second and its own engines produce thrust velocity $\mathbf{t} = (-1, 2, 4)$ meters per second. Its net velocity is $\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{t} = (2, 3, 4)$ meters per second — a single vector capturing the combined effect, computed without needing to track the two influences separately once summed. This same closure guarantee is why algorithms that iteratively update a state vector (gradient descent steps, physics simulations, signal accumulation) can safely keep adding update vectors indefinitely: the result never leaves the space you're working in.

### linear_combination (0.0s)

## Linear Combination

A **linear combination** of vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ is any vector formed by scaling each one and adding the results:

$$
w = \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \cdots + \alpha_n v_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i \, v_i
$$

Here the $\alpha_i$ are scalars (real or complex numbers), and each term $\alpha_i v_i$ is itself built from the two operations you already know: scalar multiplication (stretching or shrinking $v_i$) and vector addition (chaining the results tip-to-tail). This is not a new concept layered on top of those two — it *is* those two, applied repeatedly. That is why it is called the atomic operation of linear algebra: matrix-vector products, spans, subspaces, rank, and solutions to linear systems are all just linear combinations viewed from different angles.

**Worked example.** Let $v_1 = (1, 0)$ and $v_2 = (0, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The vector $w = (3, -2)$ is the linear combination $3v_1 + (-2)v_2$, since

$$
3(1,0) + (-2)(0,1) = (3,0) + (0,-2) = (3,-2).
$$

In fact *every* vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is some linear combination of $v_1$ and $v_2$ — this is exactly what it means to say $\{v_1, v_2\}$ spans $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Problem-solving application.** Linear combinations turn geometric questions into algebraic ones. Suppose you're asked whether $(4, 1, 5)$ lies in the plane through the origin spanned by $u = (1, 1, 1)$ and $v = (1, 0, 2)$. The question "is this point in the plane?" becomes "do scalars $\alpha, \beta$ exist with $\alpha u + \beta v = (4,1,5)$?" Writing out coordinates gives a system:

$$
\alpha + \beta = 4, \qquad \alpha = 1, \qquad \alpha + 2\beta = 5.
$$

From the second equation $\alpha = 1$; substituting into the first gives $\beta = 3$; checking the third, $1 + 2(3) = 7 \ne 5$ — no solution, so $(4,1,5)$ is *not* in the plane. This pattern — converting a geometric membership question into a solvable (or unsolvable) linear system — is the mechanism behind checking spans, testing linear independence, and eventually solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ for any matrix $A$, since the columns of $A$ combined via $\mathbf{x}$'s entries are nothing but a linear combination.

### linear_map (0.0s)

## Linear Map

A linear map is a function $f: V \to W$ between two vector spaces that preserves the structure of linear combinations: for any vectors $u, v \in V$ and any scalars $\alpha, \beta$,

$$f(\alpha u + \beta v) = \alpha f(u) + \beta f(v).$$

This single condition packages two familiar properties — additivity, $f(u+v) = f(u) + f(v)$, and homogeneity, $f(\alpha u) = \alpha f(u)$ — into one requirement. A map that satisfies it never distorts the "linear" relationships between vectors: scaling an input scales the output by the same factor, and combining inputs combines outputs the same way. This is what allows a linear map to be fully described by its action on a basis, and, in finite dimensions, to be represented by a matrix.

**Worked example.** Let $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be defined by $f(x, y) = (2x + y,\ x - y)$. Check linearity by testing the defining condition directly. Take $u = (x_1, y_1)$, $v = (x_2, y_2)$, and scalars $\alpha, \beta$:

$$f(\alpha u + \beta v) = f(\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2,\ \alpha y_1 + \beta y_2) = \big(2(\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2) + (\alpha y_1 + \beta y_2),\ (\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2) - (\alpha y_1 + \beta y_2)\big).$$

Expanding and regrouping terms by $\alpha$ and $\beta$ shows this equals $\alpha f(u) + \beta f(v)$ exactly, so $f$ is linear. Note that $g(x, y) = (2x + 1, y)$ is *not* linear, because $g(0,0) = (1,0) \neq (0,0)$ — every linear map must send the zero vector to zero, a quick first test to rule out non-candidates.

**Problem-solving application.** Once a map's linearity is confirmed, it can be represented by a matrix acting on coordinate vectors: the example above corresponds to $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, obtained by reading off the coefficients of $f$ applied to the standard basis vectors $e_1 = (1,0)$ and $e_2 = (0,1)$. This is the practical payoff of linearity: rather than evaluating $f$ pointwise, you multiply the matrix by any input vector. In applications — computer graphics transformations, systems of differential equations, or data transformations in machine learning — verifying linearity first justifies replacing a general function with matrix arithmetic, turning an abstract mapping problem into concrete linear algebra.

### eigenpair (0.0s)

## Eigenpair

A linear map $f$ takes a vector space and stretches, rotates, or reflects it — but along certain special directions, the action collapses to something much simpler: pure scaling. An **eigenpair** $(\lambda, v)$, with $v \neq 0$, satisfies

$$
f(v) = \lambda \odot v,
$$

where $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue** and $v$ the **eigenvector**. The map sends $v$ to a multiple of itself — no change in direction, only in length (and sign, if $\lambda < 0$). Since $f$ is often represented by a matrix $A$, this is usually written $Av = \lambda v$.

**Worked example.** Let

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Try $v = (1,1)^T$: $Av = (2+1,\ 1+2)^T = (3,3)^T = 3(1,1)^T$. So $(\lambda, v) = (3, (1,1)^T)$ is an eigenpair. Try $v = (1,-1)^T$: $Av = (2-1,\ 1-2)^T = (1,-1)^T = 1\cdot(1,-1)^T$, giving the eigenpair $(1, (1,-1)^T)$. Notice $A$ has no obvious geometric symmetry axis until you find these directions — they are hidden invariants of the map.

**Finding eigenpairs systematically.** Rearranging $Av = \lambda v$ gives $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$. Since $v \neq 0$, the matrix $A - \lambda I$ must be singular, so $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. This is the **characteristic equation**; its roots are the eigenvalues. For the matrix above,

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0 \implies \lambda = 3 \text{ or } \lambda = 1,
$$

matching the pairs found by inspection. Once $\lambda$ is known, substitute back into $(A-\lambda I)v = 0$ and solve the resulting linear system for $v$.

**Why it matters for problem-solving.** Eigenpairs let you decompose a complicated repeated transformation — such as $A^n$ for large $n$, used in population models, Markov chains, and vibration analysis — into simple scalar powers $\lambda^n$ along each eigenvector direction, avoiding direct matrix multiplication entirely.

### pagerank (11.0s)

## Pagerank

PageRank ranks nodes in a directed graph — originally, web pages linked by hyperlinks — by treating a link as a vote of importance, weighted by the importance of the page casting it. Formally, let $A$ be the $n \times n$ link matrix where $A_{ij} = 1/d_j$ if page $j$ links to page $i$ (and $0$ otherwise), with $d_j$ the out-degree of page $j$. Then $A$ is column-stochastic: each column sums to $1$, distributing page $j$'s "vote" equally among the pages it links to. The PageRank vector $\mathbf{r}$ is defined as the stationary distribution satisfying $A\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}$ — that is, $\mathbf{r}$ is the dominant eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $1$.

Two problems threaten this construction: pages with no outgoing links (dangling nodes) make $A$ not properly stochastic, and disconnected clusters can trap probability mass, preventing convergence to a unique ranking. Google's fix is the damped matrix

$$
M = d\,A + \frac{1-d}{n}\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T,
$$

where $\mathbf{1}$ is the all-ones vector and $d \approx 0.85$ is the damping factor. This models a "random surfer" who follows links with probability $d$ and jumps to a uniformly random page with probability $1-d$. Because $M$ is now a positive, column-stochastic matrix, the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees a unique dominant eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$ with a positive eigenvector — exactly the $\mathbf{r}$ we want, computable and stable regardless of graph structure.

**Worked example.** Consider a 4-page network: page 1 links to 2 and 3; page 2 links to 3; page 3 links to 1; page 4 links to 1, 2, 3 (dangling nowhere, self-contained). Rather than solving the eigenvector equation directly, compute $\mathbf{r}$ via the power method: start with $\mathbf{r}^{(0)} = \frac{1}{4}\mathbf{1}$, then iterate $\mathbf{r}^{(k+1)} = M\mathbf{r}^{(k)}$. Convergence is geometric, governed by the ratio $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|$ of the second-largest to largest eigenvalue — damping shrinks $\lambda_2$, which is precisely why $d < 1$ guarantees fast, reliable convergence in practice, typically within 50–100 iterations for real web-scale graphs.

**Application.** The same eigenvector machinery ranks nodes in citation networks, social-influence graphs, and recommendation systems: any setting where "importance" should propagate recursively through directed relationships rather than being read off local degree alone.
