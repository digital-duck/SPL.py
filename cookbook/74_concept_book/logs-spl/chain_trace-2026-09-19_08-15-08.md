## Stress

Stress is the body's nonspecific, stereotyped physiological response to any demand for adaptation — physical, psychological, or environmental. The word "nonspecific" is key: whether the stressor is a predator, an exam, a burn, or an argument, the underlying physiological machinery is largely the same. This idea was formalized by Hans Selye, who described the **General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)**, a three-stage response: alarm, resistance, and exhaustion.

**Alarm** is the immediate mobilization phase. The hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system and triggers the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The adrenal medulla releases epinephrine and norepinephrine within seconds, producing the classic "fight-or-flight" signs — elevated heart rate, dilated pupils, redirected blood flow toward skeletal muscle. Slower-acting, the adrenal cortex releases cortisol, which raises blood glucose and suppresses non-urgent processes like digestion and immune activity, conserving energy for the perceived threat.

**Resistance** follows if the stressor persists. The body maintains an elevated state of readiness — cortisol stays high, glucose mobilization continues — allowing sustained coping, but at a metabolic cost. Systems not essential to immediate survival (reproductive, digestive, immune) remain dampened.

**Exhaustion** occurs when the stressor is chronic and resources are depleted. The adaptive systems that were protective in the short term become damaging: chronically elevated cortisol contributes to immune suppression, hypertension, impaired memory formation, and increased vulnerability to disease. This is why *acute* stress (a brief exam) and *chronic* stress (ongoing financial insecurity) have such different health consequences despite sharing the same initial alarm response.

Consider a concrete problem: a student pulls repeated all-nighters before finals week. Night one triggers alarm — cortisol spikes, alertness increases, performance may even improve short-term. By night four, the student is in resistance — sustained but fragile — showing irritability and slower recall. By the following week, exhaustion sets in: the student gets sick, a direct consequence of immune suppression from prolonged cortisol exposure. Applying GAS to this scenario explains not just why finals week is exhausting, but why the crash afterward is a predictable physiological outcome, not a personal failing — and why interventions (sleep, pacing, recovery periods) work by interrupting the transition from resistance into exhaustion rather than eliminating the alarm response itself.

## Model Validity

When researchers induce chronic stress in a mouse to study depression, a natural question arises: does this actually model human depression, or just create a stressed mouse? **Model validity** is the framework for answering that question, built from three distinct criteria.

**Face validity** asks whether the model's symptoms resemble the human disorder's symptoms. A chronically stressed rat that stops grooming, avoids sugar water, and withdraws from social contact *looks like* it has depressive symptoms—reduced pleasure-seeking (anhedonia), self-neglect, social withdrawal. This is validity by surface resemblance, and it's the easiest to establish but the weakest on its own, since a behavior can resemble a disorder without sharing its cause.

**Construct validity** asks a deeper question: does the model share the underlying biological or psychological mechanism with the human condition? If chronic stress in humans dysregulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis—elevating cortisol and altering hippocampal neuroplasticity—then a valid mouse model should show the same HPA dysregulation and hippocampal changes, not just similar behavior. Construct validity is what separates a model that *explains* a disorder from one that merely *mimics* it.

**Predictive validity** asks whether the model responds to treatment the way the human disorder does. If a compound relieves depressive-like behavior in the stressed mouse only when it belongs to a drug class that also works in human patients—and fails when it belongs to a class that doesn't—the model has predictive power. This criterion matters most for drug development, since it determines whether a result in animals will translate to the clinic.

**Worked application:** Suppose a lab develops a new "learned helplessness" stress paradigm and wants to justify using it to screen antidepressants. They should establish all three criteria before treating results as meaningful: (1) confirm the mice show anhedonia and passivity resembling depressive symptoms (face validity); (2) confirm HPA axis and neurotrophic markers mirror human depression's known biology (construct validity); (3) confirm the model responds to established antidepressants (e.g., SSRIs) but not to drugs known to be ineffective in humans (predictive validity). A model strong in one criterion but weak in others—say, behaviorally convincing but pharmacologically unresponsive—cannot be trusted to generate translatable findings, which is precisely why all three criteria, not just one, must be evaluated together.

## Animal Models Of Stress

Animal models of stress are experimental systems in which researchers expose a species — most often rats or mice — to controlled physical, psychological, or social stressors in order to study the mechanisms and consequences of the stress response in a way that would be unethical or impossible to manipulate directly in humans. The core logic is comparative: because the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and its major hormone, cortisol in humans (corticosterone in rodents), are conserved across mammals, a stressor applied to an animal under known conditions lets researchers isolate cause and effect — something naturalistic human studies, which are always confounded by uncontrolled life history, cannot do.

**Worked example.** Consider the classic *social defeat* paradigm: an experimental mouse is placed in the home cage of a larger, more aggressive resident mouse for a brief period daily over ten days, then housed nearby with sensory but not physical contact. Researchers measure corticosterone levels via blood sampling, weigh adrenal glands post-mortem (chronic stress enlarges them), and run behavioral assays like the social interaction test, where a "stressed" mouse avoids a novel conspecific — a proxy for the social withdrawal seen in human depression. Because researchers control the exact number, duration, and intensity of defeats, they can establish a dose-response relationship between stress exposure and physiological or behavioral outcome, and can test interventions (e.g., an antidepressant) against a placebo group under otherwise identical conditions.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose you are designing a study to test whether a candidate drug blocks stress-induced weight loss. You need at least three groups: unstressed + vehicle, stressed + vehicle, and stressed + drug, ensuring the stress protocol (say, restraint stress for one hour daily) is standardized in duration and time of day, since corticosterone follows a circadian rhythm and stressing animals at different times introduces a confound. You would also need a large enough sample size per group to detect a meaningful effect size against biological variability — a power calculation, not just intuition, should set that number. This design mirrors the logic used in real translational research: an animal model's value lies not in perfectly replicating human experience, but in isolating one mechanistic variable — HPA axis activation, in this case — cleanly enough that a candidate intervention's effect can be attributed with confidence, before it is ever tested in a human trial.
