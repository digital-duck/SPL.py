### 人 (5.3s)

## 人

Stand up and look at your shadow. Two legs, spread apart, holding you steady on the ground. That's the whole idea behind this character.

Thousands of years ago, someone wanted to draw the word for "person." They didn't draw a face or a body — they drew the simplest thing that makes a person a person: two legs walking. One stroke sweeps down and out like a front leg stepping forward, and another sweeps down and out the other way, like a back leg trailing behind. Together they look like a little stick figure caught mid-stride.

That's why 人 shows up inside so many other characters — whenever a word involves a person doing something, standing somewhere, or being part of a group, you'll often find this walking figure tucked inside it, quietly doing its job.

**Now you try:** Stand with your feet apart like the character, then write 人 in the air with two strokes. Can you feel your own body becoming the shape you're drawing?

### 木 (7.4s)

## 木

Picture a little tree drawn with just a few strokes: one line for the trunk standing tall, two lines for branches reaching out to the sides, and one line for roots digging down. That whole tiny drawing is 木, and it simply means "tree" or "wood."

Here's the fun part: this one small tree-brick gets used in two very different jobs. Sometimes it brings its *meaning* — the idea of trees or wood — into a bigger character. Sometimes it only brings its *sound*, lending the way it's pronounced without caring about trees at all. Think of it like an actor who sometimes plays "the tree character" and sometimes just lends their voice for a cartoon.

$$
\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = 林 \;(\text{forest, meaning: trees})
$$

$$
\underbrace{木}_{\text{tree, sound only}} + \underbrace{water}_{} = 沐 \;(\text{to bathe, sound from 木})
$$

Now you try: look at 休 (a person resting against a tree). Does 木 there give meaning or sound? Draw your own tiny tree and imagine what job it could do in a new character you invent!

### 女 (6.4s)

## 女

Long ago, someone wanted to draw a person kneeling gracefully, hands folded, sitting back on their heels — a pose of quiet composure. That drawing became the character 女, meaning "woman."

Look at its shape: a curved stroke sweeps down like a bent knee, crossed by two strokes that could be folded arms. It doesn't look like a modern stick figure standing tall — it captures a moment of stillness, someone settled and calm.

What makes 女 special isn't just that it means "woman" on its own. It becomes a building block — a radical — hiding inside many other characters about family and relationships. When you see 女 tucked into a bigger character, it's often a clue that the character has something to do with a mother, a sister, a wife, or peace within a household.

**Now you try**: Next time you spot a character with 女 folded inside it, pause and guess — does it feel like it's about family, care, or connection? Let the kneeling figure be your hint.

### 子 (6.0s)

## 子

Imagine a tiny bundle wrapped snugly in a blanket, tucked in tight, with just a little head peeking out. That's exactly what the character 子 once looked like — a picture of a swaddled baby, arms bundled at its sides.

Why does this feel right? Because babies really are wrapped up that way in many cultures — cozy, protected, small. The character keeps that memory of a wrapped infant, even though today it looks more like a simple shape than a picture.

But 子 grew up to do two jobs. By itself, it means "child" or "son." Attached to the end of other words, it becomes a quiet helper — a suffix that turns things into nouns, almost like adding "-y" or "-let" in English, giving a word a friendly, familiar feel: think of "little box" becoming "box-y-thing," a named object you could pick up and hold.

$$
\underbrace{子}_{\text{swaddled infant}} = \text{"child"} \quad \text{or} \quad \underbrace{\text{word} + 子}_{\text{noun-maker}} = \text{a named thing}
$$

**Now you try:** Can you think of an English word that adds a small ending to name something, the way 子 does?

### 宀 (7.0s)

## 宀

Imagine you are drawing a little tent, or the roof of a tiny house, seen from the outside. Just a peaked cover, nothing underneath yet. That shape — a rooftop with slanted eaves — is 宀.

By itself, 宀 does not tell a whole story. It is like an empty umbrella waiting for someone to stand beneath it. But the moment you put something under that roof, the character starts to mean "settled," "sheltered," or "at home." A pig under the roof becomes a family's house. A woman under the roof becomes peace and calm. The roof radical is a hint whispering: "something here is protected, indoors, or belongs to a home."

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{\text{something}}_{\text{sheltered}} = \text{a settled, housed idea}
$$

**Now you try:** Draw a roof shape like a little tent. Then imagine putting your favorite toy under it. What word might that create — a toy house, a toy shelter?

### 从 (7.0s)

## 从

Imagine you're walking to school, and your little brother trots right behind you, matching your steps. That's the whole idea packed into one character.

Look at 从: it's just one person, 人, walking behind another person, 人. No tricks, no hidden parts — the picture *is* the meaning. When someone follows, walks behind, or goes along with another, this is the character that shows it.

$$\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{人}_{\text{person walking behind}} = 从 \; (\text{follow})$$

That's why 从 feels right for "follow" — you can almost see the second person's footsteps landing just after the first one's, step by step, never quite catching up, always trailing along.

**Now you try:** Draw two stick figures, one behind the other, both mid-step. Then write 从 next to your drawing. Can you think of a time you followed someone — a parent, a friend, a leader in a line? Say the sentence "I follow my teacher" and picture 从 walking it out.

### 日 (7.4s)

## 日

Long ago, someone looked up at the sky and saw a bright circle with a tiny mark inside it, like a dot of light glowing in the middle. That was the sun! They wanted to draw it so anyone could recognize it — but circles are hard to paint neatly with a brush, so scribes gently squared off the edges, turning the round sun into a tidy box shape.

Why does this feel right? Think about drawing a smiley face with straight lines instead of curves — it still looks like a face, just a little more blocky. The character 日 keeps the sun's roundness in spirit: a boxy outline holding a single stroke inside, like the sun's own glow captured in a frame.

This shape isn't just "sun" — it's also a seed. Many characters about brightness, days, and time grow out of it, because the sun gives us both light and the rhythm of days.

Now you try: Draw a circle, then draw a dot in the middle. Now try squaring the circle into a box, keeping the dot. Can you see the sun in your new shape?

### 月 (5.8s)

## 月

Look up at the night sky when the moon is thin — not round like a coin, but curved like a little boat, or a fingernail clipping. That curved shape is exactly what the character 月 draws. The two short strokes inside aren't decoration — some say they picture the shadows and craters people imagined seeing on its glowing face.

Here's the surprising part: 月 leads a double life. Sometimes it's really the moon, like in 明 (bright — moon plus sun, two lights together!). But often, tucked inside a character about the body, that same crescent shape is a disguise for "flesh" or "meat" — like in 肚 (belly) or 胖 (plump). Same picture, two totally different jobs, depending on which character it's hiding inside.

$$
\underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} \;=\; \underbrace{月}_{\text{flesh (in body words)}}
$$

Now you try: Next time you see 月 in a character, ask — is this word about the *sky*, or about a *body*? Let the meaning around it be your clue.

### 林 (7.2s)

## 林

Imagine one tree standing alone in a field. Now imagine a second tree standing right next to it. What do you call that? Not "two trees" — something new. You call it woods.

That's the trick of this character. Chinese doesn't always invent a brand-new symbol for every idea. Sometimes it just takes something you already know and doubles it, and the doubling itself becomes the new meaning. One tree, 木, is just a tree. Two trees together feel like more than a tree plus a tree — they feel like a little forest, a shady patch of woods.

$$\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 林 \; (lín, \text{ woods})$$

This is one of the simplest building rules in the whole writing system: repeat a piece, and you intensify or multiply its meaning.

**Now you try:** If one 木 is a tree, what do you think three trees stacked together might mean? (Hint: even *more* trees — a real forest!)

### 休 (6.4s)

## 休

Imagine you have been running all day, chasing your friends, climbing, laughing. Your legs feel heavy. What do you do? You find a big tree, and you lean your back right against it, and you just... stop.

That is exactly the picture of this character. On one side stands a person, 人, simply standing. Right next to it grows a tree, 木. Put them shoulder to shoulder, and you get 休 — a person leaning on a tree. Two small pictures snap together, and suddenly they mean something new: rest.

Notice that neither picture alone means "rest." A person alone is just a person. A tree alone is just a tree. But a person *against* a tree — that pose itself tells the whole story, without needing any extra words.

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 休 \ (rest)$$

Now you try: Draw a stick figure leaning against a tree trunk. Can you see 休 hiding inside your drawing?

### 众 (5.5s)

## 众

Picture three friends standing close together on a playground — not just two, but three, shoulder to shoulder. That third person changes everything. Two people is just a pair, but three people starts to feel like a group, a gathering, a crowd.

You already know 人 means "person," standing tall like two legs. And you've seen what happens when a tree becomes a forest: one tree, then two, then three, and suddenly it's 森. The same trick works here! Take one person, add another, add a third — and instead of just "some people," you get the feeling of a whole crowd, humanity together.

$$\underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} + \underbrace{人}_{person} = \underbrace{众}_{crowd}$$

Three is the magic number for "many" in Chinese characters — not four, not ten, just three, because three already feels like "more than a few."

**Now you try:** Can you think of another word where three of something suddenly feels like "a lot," even though it's really just three?

### 好 (5.3s)

## 好

Imagine peeking into a home three thousand years ago. What would make that home feel complete, safe, full of life? Ancient scribes had an answer: picture a mother with her child by her side. That image — togetherness, care, a family whole — became the character for "good."

Look closely and you can see the two people still standing there today: 女 (woman) on the left, 子 (child) on the right, side by side.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 好 \ (\text{hǎo, "good"})$$

Why would this feel like "good" to someone long ago? Because a mother and child together meant safety, love, and continuity — the next generation was cared for. That feeling of things being right became the word for goodness itself.

**Now you try:** Next time you see 好, picture that mother and child. Can you think of another pair of people or things that, together, would also feel like "good" to you?

### 字 (5.6s)

## 字

Imagine a tiny child brought safely inside a house, tucked under a roof, out of the rain and cold. That is the picture behind this character. Long ago, when people needed a word for "written character," they looked for something that already felt true — and a child growing up under a roof felt exactly right, because that is how new characters come into the world too: sheltered by older pieces, cared for until they can stand on their own.

Every character starts this way — small pieces combine, and something new is "born and raised" from them, just like a child growing up in a home. That is why this very word became the name for the whole writing system's family trees: ZiNets, or "character networks."

$$
\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof, shelter}} + \underbrace{子}_{\text{child}} = 字 \quad \text{(character — a child raised under a roof)}
$$

Now you try: think of a character you already know. Can you spot a "roof" piece protecting something underneath?

### 安 (6.0s)

## 安

Imagine you're a little girl, and outside it's dark and the wind is howling. But you're inside your house, under a strong roof, warm and safe. Nothing can reach you there. That cozy, protected feeling — that's exactly what this character captures.

Look at the character: there's a roof shape on top, covering a woman underneath. A person sheltered under a roof — that's the whole idea of peace and safety in one picture. It doesn't need to explain itself with words, because the picture already tells the story: shelter makes you calm.

$$\underbrace{宀}_{\text{roof}} + \underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} = 安 \text{ (peace, safe, settled)}$$

You'll see this character everywhere once you know it. In 平安 (píng ān), it means "safe and sound" — like when your family says it after a long trip. In 安全 (ān quán), it means "safety" — like wearing a helmet on your bike.

**Now you try:** Next time you feel warm and safe at home, picture the roof over your head — that's 安!

### 明 (5.5s)

## 明

Imagine it's nighttime, and you're scared of the dark under your blanket. Then your mom turns on the lamp — and suddenly you can see your toys, your books, everything! Now imagine two lamps turned on at once. Even brighter, right?

That's exactly what this character is doing. Long ago, people looked at the two brightest things in the sky — the sun during the day, and the moon at night — and thought: what if we put them side by side? Sun plus moon must mean the brightest kind of bright there is.

$$
\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} = \underbrace{明}_{\text{bright}}
$$

It's not just "a little light" — it's brightness itself, made from combining the two greatest sources of light we know. A smart idea is sometimes called "míng" too, because a clear, bright idea lights up your mind just like the sun and moon light up the sky!

**Now you try:** Can you think of two bright things you know? Draw them side by side — did you just invent your own "明"?

### 森 (8.7s)

## 森

Imagine you're standing in a small yard with one tree — nice and quiet. Now imagine a park with two trees close together, giving a little shade. Now imagine walking into a real forest, so thick with trees you can't see the sky. That last feeling — trees everywhere, all around you — is exactly what 森 means.

Here's the trick: the character doesn't just tell you "forest," it *shows* you. One tree is 木. Two trees stacked together, 林, already means "woods" or "grove." Add one more tree on top, and you get 森 — a whole forest. The more tree-bricks you stack, the bigger and denser the meaning grows. It's like the character is counting trees to help you feel how crowded the forest is!

$$
\underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} + \underbrace{木}_{tree} = 森 \text{ (forest)}
$$

$$
木 \xrightarrow{+木} 林 \xrightarrow{+木} 森
$$

**Now you try:** Can you think of another character where more copies of a small piece might mean "more" or "bigger"?

### semantic_composition (10.6s)

## Semantic Composition

Imagine you want to draw the idea of "rest." You can't draw a feeling — but you *can* draw a person leaning against a tree. That's exactly what ancient writers did: they took two simple pictures and pushed them together so their meanings would combine into something new.

This is called meaning-stacking. A person next to a tree doesn't mean "person" or "tree" anymore — together they mean *rest*, because a tree is a good place to lean and pause. Even the word "character" itself is built this way!

$$
\underbrace{人}_{\text{person}} + \underbrace{木}_{\text{tree}} = \underbrace{休}_{\text{rest}}
$$

Once you know this trick, you start seeing it everywhere. The sun next to the moon doesn't mean either one alone — together, two great lights make *bright*.

$$
\underbrace{日}_{\text{sun}} + \underbrace{月}_{\text{moon}} = \underbrace{明}_{\text{bright}}
$$

**Now you try:** Three trees together make 森 (forest), and three people together make 众 (crowd). Can you guess what a woman under a roof might mean?

### heritage_and_classroom (5.9s)

## Heritage And Classroom

Imagine a girl named Mei who speaks Chinese at home but has never learned to read it. Her grandmother writes a word on paper: 妈 (mother). Instead of asking Mei to memorize this shape like a random doodle, she points to its two bricks. On the left is 女, the "woman" brick — it shows up in words about mothers, sisters, and daughters. On the right is 马, the "horse" brick — here it just hints at the sound "ma." Mei's grandmother is teaching the same way builders teach: show the bricks first, then the wall.

$$\underbrace{女}_{\text{woman}} + \underbrace{马}_{\text{sound: ma}} = 妈 \text{ (mother)}$$

This structural path works for anyone — a heritage learner reconnecting with a grandparent's language, or a whole classroom starting from zero. Once you know a handful of bricks, hundreds of characters stop looking scary. A computer can even generate practice lessons using bricks you already know, in whatever language explains them best to you.

**Now you try:** Take a character you've seen before. Can you spot two smaller pieces inside it — one that hints at meaning, one that hints at sound?
