### difference_quotient (0.1s)

## Difference Quotient

**Definition.** The *difference quotient* of a function $f$ at $x$ with step size $h$ is

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}, \quad h \neq 0.$$

Geometrically, this is the slope of the **secant line** — the straight line connecting the two points $(x,\, f(x))$ and $(x+h,\, f(x+h))$ on the graph of $f$. It answers the question: *how fast is $f$ changing, on average, over the interval from $x$ to $x+h$?* As $h$ shrinks toward zero, the secant approaches the tangent line at $x$, and the difference quotient approaches the derivative $f'(x)$. Mastering this expression is the essential prerequisite for understanding all of differential calculus.

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$. Compute the difference quotient and simplify.

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}$$

Expand the numerator:

$$(x+h)^2 - x^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2$$

Divide by $h$ (valid since $h\neq 0$):

$$\frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h$$

So the difference quotient for $f(x)=x^2$ simplifies to $2x+h$. As $h \to 0$, this approaches $2x$ — which is exactly the derivative $f'(x)=2x$ you would get from the power rule.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose you need to estimate the instantaneous speed of a falling object whose height (in meters) is modeled by $h(t) = -4.9t^2 + 20$ at $t=1$ second. You don't yet know calculus rules, but you can evaluate the difference quotient with a small step, say $\Delta t = 0.01$:

$$\frac{h(1.01)-h(1)}{0.01} = \frac{(-4.9(1.01)^2+20)-(-4.9(1)^2+20)}{0.01}$$

Compute: $h(1.01) = -4.9(1.0201)+20 \approx 14.9015$, $h(1)=15.1$, so

$$\frac{14.9015 - 15.1}{0.01} = \frac{-0.1985}{0.01} \approx -9.85 \text{ m/s.}$$

The negative sign confirms the object is falling. The exact answer $-4.9(2)(1) = -9.8$ m/s emerges in the limit — showing that the difference quotient is a computable, numerical bridge between average rates of change and instantaneous ones. Whenever you see a derivative, remember it is just a difference quotient with $h$ taken to its limit.

### limit (0.2s)

## Limit

A **limit** describes the value a function approaches as its input gets arbitrarily close to some point — not necessarily the value *at* that point. Formally, we write

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

and mean: for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. In plain terms: we can make $f(x)$ as close to $L$ as we like by keeping $x$ close enough to $a$. The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition is not just bookkeeping — it is what allows limits to exist at points where the function is undefined or discontinuous, which is exactly the situation that makes limits useful.

**Worked example.** Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$$

At $x = 1$, the denominator is zero, so $f(1)$ is undefined. But we can factor:

$$\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x + 1, \quad x \neq 1.$$

As $x \to 1$, $f(x)$ approaches $1 + 1 = 2$. The limit exists and equals 2 even though the function has a hole at that point. The graph would look like the line $y = x + 1$ with a single missing dot at $(1, 2)$.

**Problem-solving application.** Limits are the engine behind derivatives and integrals. When you compute a derivative,

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$

you are asking what the difference quotient *approaches* as $h$ shrinks, not what it equals at $h = 0$ (where it is $\tfrac{0}{0}$). The practical move is always the same: simplify algebraically to cancel the offending factor, then evaluate. For instance, $f(x) = x^2$ gives

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(2a + h) = 2a.$$

When algebra alone does not cancel the singularity, tools like L'Hôpital's rule or Taylor series take over. The key habit: before reaching for those tools, always try factoring or rationalizing — most limit problems at this level collapse with one algebraic step.

### derivative_at_a_point (0.2s)

## Derivative At A Point

**Definition.** The derivative of a function $f$ at a specific point $x = a$ measures the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at that exact location. Geometrically, it is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $(a, f(a))$. Formally:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

This limit, if it exists, gives a single number — not a function, but a slope. The expression $\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ is called the **difference quotient**; it is the slope of a secant line connecting $(a, f(a))$ to a nearby point. As $h \to 0$, the secant line approaches the tangent line, and the difference quotient approaches the derivative.

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$. Find $f'(3)$.

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 3^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h}$$

$$= \lim_{h \to 0} (6 + h) = 6$$

So the slope of the tangent to $y = x^2$ at $x = 3$ is $6$. The tangent line itself is $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$, or $y = 6x - 9$.

**Problem-Solving Application.** Derivatives at a point appear whenever you need a local rate — not an average over an interval, but what is happening *right now*.

Suppose a car's position (in meters) is $s(t) = 4t^2 + 2t$ and you want the instantaneous velocity at $t = 1$ second. Velocity is the derivative of position:

$$s'(1) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{s(1+h) - s(1)}{h}$$

Compute: $s(1) = 4(1)^2 + 2(1) = 6$, and $s(1+h) = 4(1+h)^2 + 2(1+h) = 4 + 8h + 4h^2 + 2 + 2h$. Then:

$$s'(1) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(6 + 10h + 4h^2) - 6}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(10 + 4h) = 10 \text{ m/s}$$

The key skill is setting up the difference quotient correctly, expanding carefully, and canceling the $h$ in the denominator before taking the limit. If you cannot cancel that $h$, the limit will be $\tfrac{0}{0}$ — a sign that algebra isn't finished yet.

### amount_of_change_formula (20.9s)

## Amount Of Change Formula

**Definition.** When a quantity changes continuously over time, its *total change* over an interval equals the definite integral of its rate of change. If $Q(t)$ is any differentiable function and $Q'(t)$ is its derivative, then:

$$Q(b) - Q(a) = \int_a^b Q'(t)\, dt$$

This is a direct consequence of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and is sometimes called the **Net Change Theorem**. The left side is simply the arithmetic difference — the ending value minus the starting value. The right side accumulates all the instantaneous rates of change across the interval. They are equal. That equality is the power of the formula: you can recover total change from rate-of-change data even when you never know $Q(t)$ explicitly.

**Worked example.** A car's velocity (in miles per hour) at time $t$ hours is given by $v(t) = 3t^2 - 6t + 4$. How far does the car travel between $t = 1$ and $t = 3$?

Distance is the amount of change in position $s(t)$, and $s'(t) = v(t)$, so:

$$\Delta s = \int_1^3 (3t^2 - 6t + 4)\, dt$$

Evaluate term by term:

$$= \left[ t^3 - 3t^2 + 4t \right]_1^3 = (27 - 27 + 12) - (1 - 3 + 4) = 12 - 2 = 10 \text{ miles}$$

The car travels 10 miles. Notice we never needed an explicit formula for $s(t)$ — just its derivative and two endpoints.

**Problem-solving application.** The formula applies whenever you know a *rate* and need a *total*. A water tank drains at $r(t) = 5 - 0.4t$ gallons per minute. How much water leaves the tank in the first 10 minutes?

$$\Delta W = \int_0^{10} (5 - 0.4t)\, dt = \left[ 5t - 0.2t^2 \right]_0^{10} = (50 - 20) - 0 = 30 \text{ gallons}$$

Key problem-solving steps:
1. Identify the quantity $Q$ whose change you need.
2. Confirm that the given function is $Q'(t)$, the rate of change of that quantity.
3. Set up and evaluate the definite integral over the specified interval.

Watch for sign: if $Q'(t) < 0$ on part of the interval, the integral correctly reduces the total, reflecting a decrease in $Q$. Always interpret the sign of the result in context before reporting it as a final answer.

### marginal_functions (19.8s)

## Marginal Functions

In economics and applied calculus, a **marginal function** is the derivative of a total function with respect to quantity. If $C(q)$ is the total cost of producing $q$ units, the **marginal cost** is $MC(q) = C'(q)$. It answers the question: *how much does it cost to produce one additional unit at the current output level?* The same idea applies to revenue and profit:

$$MR(q) = R'(q), \qquad MP(q) = P'(q) = R'(q) - C'(q)$$

Because the derivative gives an instantaneous rate of change, $MC(q_0)$ is the approximate cost of moving from $q_0$ to $q_0 + 1$ units — a linear estimate of the actual difference $C(q_0+1) - C(q_0)$.

---

**Worked example.** A firm's total cost function is

$$C(q) = 0.01q^3 - 0.6q^2 + 15q + 200$$

Find $MC(q)$ and evaluate it at $q = 20$.

Differentiate term by term:

$$MC(q) = 0.03q^2 - 1.2q + 15$$

At $q = 20$:

$$MC(20) = 0.03(400) - 1.2(20) + 15 = 12 - 24 + 15 = 3$$

Interpretation: when 20 units are already being produced, each additional unit costs approximately **\$3**. The actual incremental cost $C(21) - C(20)$ is $\$3.01$ — the derivative is an excellent approximation.

---

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose the firm sells each unit at a fixed price $p = 9$, so total revenue is $R(q) = 9q$ and marginal revenue is $MR(q) = 9$ (constant). To maximize profit, set $MR = MC$:

$$9 = 0.03q^2 - 1.2q + 15$$

$$0.03q^2 - 1.2q + 6 = 0 \implies q^2 - 40q + 200 = 0$$

$$q = \frac{40 \pm \sqrt{1600 - 800}}{2} = 20 \pm 10\sqrt{2}$$

The two candidates are $q \approx 5.86$ and $q \approx 34.14$. Check the second derivative of profit: $P''(q) = -MC'(q) = -(0.06q - 1.2)$, which is negative (concave down) at $q \approx 34$, confirming a maximum. The profit-maximizing output is approximately **34 units**.

The pattern is always the same: find where the marginal benefit equals the marginal cost, then verify it's a maximum rather than a minimum.
