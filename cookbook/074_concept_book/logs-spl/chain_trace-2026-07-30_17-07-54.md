## Audience

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a message space and $\mathcal{K}$ a knowledge space whose elements represent prior beliefs, vocabulary, and cognitive schemata. An *audience* $\mathcal{A}$ is a non-empty set of agents $\{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n\}$, each equipped with a knowledge profile $\kappa_i \in \mathcal{K}$. A *target audience* $\mathcal{A}^* \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ is the subset for whom a message $m \in \mathcal{M}$ is designed to be both received and decoded with minimal information loss. Formally, the *audience fit* of message $m$ with respect to agent $r_i$ is the functional

$$\phi(m, r_i) = 1 - \frac{H(m \mid \kappa_i)}{H(m)},$$

where $H(\cdot)$ denotes Shannon entropy and $H(m \mid \kappa_i)$ is the conditional entropy of the message given the agent's prior knowledge. A value $\phi = 1$ indicates perfect comprehension; $\phi = 0$ indicates total opacity.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a calculus instructor encodes a lecture $m$ that assumes knowledge of limits and derivatives. Consider two agents: $r_1$ (a first-year student who has completed pre-calculus, $\kappa_1$) and $r_2$ (a trained analyst, $\kappa_2$). Estimating entropy reductions, suppose $H(m) = 8$ bits, $H(m \mid \kappa_1) = 3$ bits, and $H(m \mid \kappa_2) = 0.5$ bits. Then

$$\phi(m, r_1) = 1 - \tfrac{3}{8} = 0.625, \qquad \phi(m, r_2) = 1 - \tfrac{0.5}{8} = 0.9375.$$

The lecture is well-matched to $r_2$ but delivers only 62.5% comprehension efficiency to $r_1$. A responsible author adjusts $m$ to raise $\phi(m, r_1)$ toward 1 without sacrificing precision for $r_2$.

**Key Theorem (Audience–Message Alignment).** For a fixed message $m$, the expected comprehension over a target audience $\mathcal{A}^*$ is maximised when the message encoding minimises $\mathbb{E}_{r \in \mathcal{A}^*}[H(m \mid \kappa_r)]$, equivalently when $m$ is constructed in the shared knowledge subspace $\bigcap_{r \in \mathcal{A}^*} \operatorname{supp}(\kappa_r)$.

*Proof sketch.* Since $H(m \mid \kappa_r) \geq 0$ for all $r$, minimising the expectation requires maximising overlap between the informational structure of $m$ and the common prior support. Any symbol in $m$ outside the intersection increases conditional entropy additively, reducing expected $\phi$. $\square$

```python
from sympy import symbols, log, simplify, Rational

H_m = 8          # total entropy of message (bits)
kappa_r1 = 3     # conditional entropy for student

print("phi(m, r2) =", phi(kappa_r2))   # 15/16
```

Running this cell confirms $\phi(m, r_1) = \tfrac{5}{8}$ and $\phi(m, r_2) = \tfrac{15}{16}$, making the alignment gap concrete and computable.

## Data Visualization Choice

**Definition.** A *data visualization* is a mapping $V: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{G}$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is a dataset with attributes of specified measurement levels (nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio) and $\mathcal{G}$ is a graphical representation drawn from a vocabulary of geometric primitives — position, length, area, angle, color hue, and color saturation. The *visualization choice problem* is the task of selecting the element of $\mathcal{G}$ that maximizes perceptual accuracy and communicative efficiency for a given analytic goal $Q$ (comparison, distribution, composition, or relationship).

The foundational constraint comes from Cleveland and McGill's 1984 ranking of perceptual accuracy. Formally, let $\epsilon_k$ denote the mean absolute decoding error when a reader recovers a numerical quantity encoded by channel $k$. The empirical ordering is

$$\epsilon_{\text{position}} < \epsilon_{\text{length}} < \epsilon_{\text{angle}} < \epsilon_{\text{area}} < \epsilon_{\text{color saturation}}.$$

Any principled visualization choice should prefer channels appearing earlier in this ordering, subject to the constraints imposed by the measurement level of the data.

**Worked Example.** Suppose $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is a continuous ratio-scale variable representing exam scores, and the goal $Q$ is to display its distribution. A pie chart encodes magnitudes as angles, placing it third in the ranking above. A histogram encodes frequency as bar length (second) and uses position on a common scale (first) for the score axis. The histogram therefore dominates the pie chart under the Cleveland–McGill criterion. Formally, if we denote the chosen visualization by $V^*$, then

$$V^* = \arg\min_{V \in \mathcal{G}} \epsilon(V, Q),$$

and for $Q = \text{distribution}$, the histogram achieves the minimum among standard chart types.

**Theorem (Measurement–Channel Compatibility).** A visualization is *valid* only if the geometric channel is at least as expressive as the measurement level of the encoded attribute: nominal data may use any channel, but ratio-scale data requires a channel that preserves the zero point and proportional distances, such as linear position.

**Lab Cell.**
```python
import sympy as sp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

# Simulate exam scores and compare histogram vs. pie chart
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
scores = rng.normal(loc=72, scale=12, size=200).clip(0, 100)
bins = np.arange(0, 101, 10)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))

ax1.hist(scores, bins=bins, edgecolor="black")
ax1.set_title("Histogram (position + length)")
ax1.set_xlabel("Score"); ax1.set_ylabel("Frequency")

counts, _ = np.histogram(scores, bins=bins)
ax2.pie(counts, labels=[f"{b}–{b+10}" for b in bins[:-1]], autopct="%1.0f%%")
ax2.set_title("Pie Chart (angle)")

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
# Observe that bin-to-bin comparisons are far easier in the histogram.
```

Running this cell makes the perceptual difference immediate: recovering that the $70$–$80$ bin is roughly $1.4\times$ larger than the $60$–$70$ bin requires mental arithmetic from the pie chart but is directly readable as a length ratio in the histogram.

## Executive Dashboard

**Definition.** An *executive dashboard* is a real-time aggregation surface that maps a high-dimensional state vector of an organization into a low-dimensional set of *key performance indicators* (KPIs) chosen to support decision-making at the strategic level. Formally, let $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denote the full operational state of a system at time $t$. A dashboard is a tuple $\mathcal{D} = (f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_k)$ where each $f_i : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is a summary functional and $k \ll n$. The projection $\mathbf{y}(t) = \bigl(f_1(\mathbf{x}(t)), \ldots, f_k(\mathbf{x}(t))\bigr)$ is the *dashboard state*. Effective design requires that $\mathbf{y}$ retains sufficient information for the decisions under consideration — a constraint formalised by information-theoretic sufficiency.

**Worked Example.** Consider a retail operation whose full state $\mathbf{x}$ includes daily unit sales $s_i$ for $n = 500$ SKUs. The executive needs only three indicators:

$$f_1(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i s_i \quad (\text{total revenue}), \qquad f_2(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\sum_i s_i - \mu_s}{\sigma_s} \quad (\text{standardised volume}), \qquad f_3(\mathbf{x}) = \max_i \frac{s_i - \hat{s}_i}{\hat{s}_i} \quad (\text{peak deviation}).$$

Here $p_i$ is unit price, $\mu_s$ and $\sigma_s$ are rolling mean and standard deviation of total volume, and $\hat{s}_i$ is a forecast. These three scalars compress 500 dimensions into an actionable signal.

**Key Theorem (Sufficient Statistic Reduction).** Let $\mathbf{x}$ follow a parametric family $p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)$. A statistic $T(\mathbf{x})$ is *sufficient* for $\theta$ if and only if the conditional distribution $p(\mathbf{x}\mid T(\mathbf{x}), \theta)$ does not depend on $\theta$ (Fisher–Neyman factorisation). A dashboard whose KPI vector is a sufficient statistic for the decision-relevant parameter $\theta$ incurs zero information loss relative to observing $\mathbf{x}$ in full. In practice, sufficiency is rarely exact; the design problem becomes minimising the *Fisher information loss* $\mathcal{I}(\theta; \mathbf{x}) - \mathcal{I}(\theta; \mathbf{y})$ subject to the cardinality constraint $k \leq k_{\max}$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

# Symbolic revenue KPI
n = 4
p, s = sp.symbols('p0:4'), sp.symbols('s0:4')
revenue = sum(p[i]*s[i] for i in range(n))
print("Revenue KPI:", revenue)

# Marginal contribution of SKU j
j = 2
print(f"∂revenue/∂s{j} =", sp.diff(revenue, s[j]))

# Standardised volume (symbolic mean μ, std σ)
mu, sigma = sp.symbols('mu sigma', positive=True)
total_vol = sum(s)
z_volume = (total_vol - mu) / sigma
print("Standardised volume:", sp.simplify(z_volume))
```

Running this cell confirms that the marginal revenue contribution of SKU $j$ equals its unit price $p_j$, illustrating how symbolic differentiation of dashboard functionals supports sensitivity analysis without numerical approximation.
