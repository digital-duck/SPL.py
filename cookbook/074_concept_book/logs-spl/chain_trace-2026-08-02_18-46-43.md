### derivative (0.1s)

## Derivative

The derivative of a function measures how fast its output changes relative to its input at any given point. More precisely, the derivative of $f$ at $x = a$ is the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to zero:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

Geometrically, this limit is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$. When the limit exists, $f$ is said to be **differentiable** at $a$. The derivative function $f'(x)$ gives you that slope at every point simultaneously.

**Worked Example.** Find the derivative of $f(x) = x^2$ at $x = 3$.

Apply the definition directly:

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(6 + h) = 6$$

So the slope of $y = x^2$ at $x = 3$ is $6$, and the tangent line there is $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$.

In practice, the power rule $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$ lets you skip the limit algebra: $f'(x) = 2x$, so $f'(3) = 6$ immediately.

**Problem-Solving Application.** A company's daily revenue (in thousands of dollars) is modeled by $R(x) = -2x^2 + 80x$, where $x$ is the number of units sold. To maximize revenue, you need the value of $x$ where the rate of change flips from positive to negative — that is, where $R'(x) = 0$.

Differentiate: $R'(x) = -4x + 80$. Setting this equal to zero gives $x = 20$. Since $R''(x) = -4 < 0$, the function is concave down, confirming this is a maximum. Selling 20 units yields peak daily revenue of $R(20) = -2(400) + 80(20) = \$800{,}000$.

This pattern — differentiate, set equal to zero, check concavity — is the core algorithm for optimization problems across physics, economics, and engineering. The derivative transforms a vague question ("where is the peak?") into a precise algebraic equation you can solve.

### antiderivative (18.4s)

## Antiderivative

**Definition.** A function $F(x)$ is called an *antiderivative* of $f(x)$ on an interval if $F'(x) = f(x)$ for every $x$ in that interval. Because the derivative of any constant is zero, antiderivatives are never unique: if $F(x)$ is one antiderivative, then $F(x) + C$ for any constant $C$ is another. The full family is written

$$\int f(x)\, dx = F(x) + C$$

and called the *indefinite integral* of $f$. The symbol $\int$ is an elongated "S" for summation — a visual hint at the deep connection to area that the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus makes precise.

**Core rules.** The power rule runs in reverse: since $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = n x^{n-1}$, we get

$$\int x^n\, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C, \quad n \neq -1.$$

Other essential antiderivatives follow from reversing known derivatives:

| $f(x)$ | $\int f(x)\, dx$ |
|---|---|
| $e^x$ | $e^x + C$ |
| $\cos x$ | $\sin x + C$ |
| $\sin x$ | $-\cos x + C$ |
| $\frac{1}{x}$ | $\ln|x| + C$ |

**Worked example.** Find $\int (3x^2 - 4x + 7)\, dx$.

Apply the power rule term by term:

$$\int 3x^2\, dx = x^3, \quad \int 4x\, dx = 2x^2, \quad \int 7\, dx = 7x.$$

So $\int (3x^2 - 4x + 7)\, dx = x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x + C$. Verify by differentiating: $\frac{d}{dx}(x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x + C) = 3x^2 - 4x + 7$. ✓

**Problem-solving application.** Antiderivatives appear whenever you know a *rate* and want the *accumulated quantity*. Suppose a particle moves along a line with velocity $v(t) = 6t - 2$ m/s and is at position $s = 4$ m when $t = 0$.

Find $s(t)$ by antidifferentiating: $s(t) = 3t^2 - 2t + C$. Apply the initial condition $s(0) = 4$:

$$3(0)^2 - 2(0) + C = 4 \implies C = 4.$$

Therefore $s(t) = 3t^2 - 2t + 4$. The arbitrary constant $C$ is pinned down the moment you supply one piece of known information — an *initial condition*. This pattern (antidifferentiate, then solve for $C$) is the core technique in every introductory differential equation.

### indefinite_integral (23.4s)

## Indefinite Integral

An indefinite integral is the reverse operation of differentiation. If $F'(x) = f(x)$, then $F$ is called an **antiderivative** of $f$, and the indefinite integral is written

$$\int f(x)\, dx = F(x) + C$$

where $C$ is an arbitrary constant called the **constant of integration**. The $+C$ is not a formality — it reflects the fact that infinitely many functions share the same derivative. For example, $x^2$, $x^2 + 5$, and $x^2 - 17$ all differentiate to $2x$, so $\int 2x\, dx = x^2 + C$ captures the entire family at once.

The foundational rule for polynomials is the **power rule for antiderivatives**: for any $n \neq -1$,

$$\int x^n\, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$$

This is the direct mirror of the differentiation power rule and handles the majority of introductory problems.

**Worked example.** Find $\displaystyle\int (3x^2 - 4x + 7)\, dx$.

Integration is linear, so you can integrate term by term:

$$\int 3x^2\, dx = 3 \cdot \frac{x^3}{3} = x^3, \qquad \int 4x\, dx = 4 \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} = 2x^2, \qquad \int 7\, dx = 7x$$

Combining with a single constant of integration:

$$\int (3x^2 - 4x + 7)\, dx = x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x + C$$

Always verify by differentiating the result: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x + C) = 3x^2 - 4x + 7$. ✓

**Problem-solving application.** The indefinite integral becomes fully useful when paired with an initial condition. Suppose a particle has velocity $v(t) = 6t^2 - 10t$ m/s and starts at position $s(0) = 4$ m. Find $s(t)$.

First, integrate velocity to get the position family:

$$s(t) = \int (6t^2 - 10t)\, dt = 2t^3 - 5t^2 + C$$

Then substitute the initial condition to pin down $C$:

$$s(0) = 2(0)^3 - 5(0)^2 + C = 4 \implies C = 4$$

So $s(t) = 2t^3 - 5t^2 + 4$.

This two-step pattern — integrate to get the general family, then use one known value to select the specific solution — recurs throughout physics, engineering, and economics. The indefinite integral gives you the full solution space; the initial condition narrows it to the one that fits your situation.

### initial_value_problem (19.4s)

## Initial Value Problem

A **differential equation** tells you how a quantity changes — it relates a function to its own derivative. But infinitely many functions can satisfy the same differential equation, differing only by a constant. An **initial value problem (IVP)** pins down one specific solution by pairing the differential equation with a single known fact: the value of the solution at a particular starting point.

Formally, the standard first-order IVP is:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x,\, y), \qquad y(x_0) = y_0$$

The equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)$ describes the rule of change; the **initial condition** $y(x_0) = y_0$ says "at $x = x_0$, the solution equals $y_0$." Together they determine a unique solution (whenever $f$ is sufficiently well-behaved — a guarantee given by the Existence and Uniqueness Theorem for ODEs).

**Worked example.** Suppose a population grows at a rate proportional to its current size, and there are 500 individuals at time $t = 0$. This gives the IVP:

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = 0.03\,P, \qquad P(0) = 500$$

Separating variables and integrating both sides:

$$\int \frac{dP}{P} = \int 0.03\, dt \implies \ln|P| = 0.03t + C \implies P(t) = Ae^{0.03t}$$

Applying the initial condition: $P(0) = A = 500$. The unique solution is:

$$P(t) = 500\,e^{0.03t}$$

Without $P(0) = 500$, every value of $A$ would be valid — the initial condition is what selects the one curve that matches reality.

**Problem-solving strategy.** When you encounter an IVP: (1) solve the differential equation to find the **general solution** (a family of curves parameterized by an arbitrary constant); (2) substitute the initial condition to **evaluate the constant**; (3) write the **particular solution**. Always verify by plugging back into both the equation and the initial condition.

IVPs appear wherever a system evolves from a known state: a cooling object with a measured starting temperature, a circuit switched on at $t = 0$, or a projectile launched at a known height. The differential equation encodes the physics; the initial condition encodes the setup. Solving the IVP means answering the question: *given where things started, where do they end up?*
