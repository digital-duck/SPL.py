### difference_quotient (0.0s)

## Difference Quotient

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval containing $x$ and $x + h$, where $h \neq 0$. The *difference quotient* of $f$ at $x$ with increment $h$ is the expression

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.$$

Geometrically, this quantity equals the slope of the secant line passing through the points $\bigl(x,\, f(x)\bigr)$ and $\bigl(x+h,\, f(x+h)\bigr)$ on the graph of $f$, measuring the average rate of change of $f$ over the interval $[x,\, x+h]$ (or $[x+h,\, x]$ when $h < 0$). Both of those ideas — secant slope and average rate of change — are assumed familiar; what is new here is the single algebraic expression that unifies them.

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$. Compute the difference quotient and simplify.

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h.$$

The factor $h$ cancels for all $h \neq 0$, leaving the expression $2x + h$, which is defined everywhere in the plane except along the line $h = 0$. Observe that as $h$ decreases toward zero, the value $2x + h$ approaches $2x$: the secant slopes converge, and their common limit is what calculus will later call the derivative. That step — passing to the limit — belongs to the next section; here the goal is simply to see how cleanly the difference quotient simplifies.

**Key Theorem** *(Existence of the Limit).* If the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ converges to a finite value $L$ as $h \to 0$ from both sides, then $L$ is unique and equals the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $\bigl(x, f(x)\bigr)$.

*Proof sketch.* Uniqueness follows directly from the uniqueness of limits: if two values $L_1$ and $L_2$ both satisfy the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ criterion for the same limit, then $|L_1 - L_2| < 2\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$, forcing $L_1 = L_2$. The geometric interpretation — that the limiting secant slope equals the tangent slope — follows because the tangent line is defined as precisely that limit.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, simplify, limit

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**2

dq = (f.subs(x, x + h) - f) / h
dq_simplified = simplify(dq)
print("Difference quotient:", dq_simplified)   # 2*x + h
print("Limit as h -> 0:",  limit(dq_simplified, h, 0))  # 2*x
```

Running this cell confirms the algebraic cancellation and previews the limiting value, grounding the formal definition in concrete symbolic computation.

### limit (0.0s)

## Limit

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on a punctured neighbourhood of a point $a \in \mathbb{R}$. We say that the *limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $a$* equals $L$, written

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L,$$

if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that

$$0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon.$$

The condition $0 < |x - a|$ is essential: the value $f(a)$ itself is irrelevant, or may not even exist. The definition formalises the intuition that $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by choosing $x$ sufficiently close (but not equal) to $a$.

---

**Worked example.** We verify $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2}(3x - 1) = 5$ directly from the definition.

Given $\varepsilon > 0$, we need $|3x - 1 - 5| < \varepsilon$, i.e., $3|x - 2| < \varepsilon$. Choose $\delta = \varepsilon/3$. Then

$$0 < |x - 2| < \delta \implies |f(x) - 5| = 3|x - 2| < 3\delta = \varepsilon. \quad \checkmark$$

The choice $\delta = \varepsilon/3$ was found by working backwards from the desired inequality — a standard technique in $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ proofs.

---

**Key theorem (Limit laws).** If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M$, then:

$$\lim_{x \to a}[f(x) + g(x)] = L + M, \qquad \lim_{x \to a}[f(x) \cdot g(x)] = L \cdot M,$$

and, provided $M \neq 0$,

$$\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}.$$

These laws reduce the verification of composite limits to simpler ones, eliminating the need for a fresh $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ argument each time.

---

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit, sin, oo

x = symbols('x')

# Fundamental trigonometric limit
print(limit(sin(x) / x, x, 0))          # 1

# Limit at infinity
print(limit((3*x**2 + 1) / (x**2 - 4), x, oo))  # 3

# One-sided limits
print(limit(abs(x) / x, x, 0, '+'))     # 1
print(limit(abs(x) / x, x, 0, '-'))     # -1
```

The last two calls illustrate that a two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits agree, connecting the symbolic machinery to the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition.

### derivative_at_a_point (0.0s)

## Derivative At A Point

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval containing $a$. The *derivative of $f$ at the point $a$*, denoted $f'(a)$, is defined as the limit

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists and is finite. When it does, $f$ is said to be *differentiable at $a$*. Geometrically, $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$.

---

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$. We compute $f'(3)$ directly from the definition.

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 3^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h}.$$

Factoring the numerator:

$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h(6 + h)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(6 + h) = 6.$$

Thus $f'(3) = 6$, meaning the tangent line to the parabola at $x = 3$ has slope $6$. The tangent line itself is $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$, or equivalently $y = 6x - 9$.

---

**Key Theorem** *(Differentiability implies continuity).* If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $f(a+h) - f(a) = \dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor converges to $f'(a)$ and the second to $0$, so the product tends to $0$. Hence $\lim_{h\to 0} f(a+h) = f(a)$. $\square$

The converse fails: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there, since the left- and right-hand limits of the difference quotient are $-1$ and $1$, respectively.

---

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**2
a = 3

# Derivative via limit definition
deriv_def = limit((f.subs(x, a + h) - f.subs(x, a)) / h, h, 0)

print(deriv_def)   # prints 6
```

### instantaneous_rate_of_change (0.0s)

## Instantaneous Rate Of Change

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function defined on an open interval containing $a$. The *instantaneous rate of change* of $f$ at $x = a$ is defined as the limit

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists. Geometrically, $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$. This quantity refines the *average rate of change* $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ by collapsing the interval $[a,b]$ to a single point through the limiting process.

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$ and $a = 3$. The average rate of change over $[3, 3+h]$ is

$$\frac{f(3+h)-f(3)}{h} = \frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = 6 + h.$$

Taking the limit as $h \to 0$ yields $f'(3) = 6$. Physically, if $f(t)$ measures position in metres at time $t$ seconds, then $f'(3) = 6\ \mathrm{m/s}$ is the velocity at the instant $t = 3$.

**Key Theorem (Differentiability Implies Continuity).** If $f'(a)$ exists, then $f$ is continuous at $a$. To see why, observe that

$$\lim_{h \to 0} [f(a+h) - f(a)] = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \cdot h = f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0,$$

so $\lim_{h\to 0} f(a+h) = f(a)$. The converse fails: continuity does not guarantee differentiability, as the function $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$ demonstrates.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit, diff, simplify

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**2

# Instantaneous rate of change at x=3 via definition
avg_rate = (f.subs(x, 3 + h) - f.subs(x, 3)) / h
iroc = limit(avg_rate, h, 0)
print("f'(3) via limit definition:", iroc)        # 6

# Verify with symbolic differentiation
deriv = diff(f, x)
print("f'(x) =", deriv, "  →  f'(3) =", deriv.subs(x, 3))  # 2x → 6
```

Both approaches confirm $f'(3) = 6$, illustrating that the limit definition and the differentiation rules are consistent.

### derivative_function (0.0s)

## Derivative Function

**Definition.** Let $f : (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval. The *derivative function* of $f$ is the function $f' : (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}$ whose value at each point $x \in (a, b)$ is defined by the limit

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists and is finite at every point of the interval. When the limit exists at a particular point $x_0$, the function $f$ is said to be *differentiable* at $x_0$, and $f'(x_0)$ equals the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $(x_0, f(x_0))$. The notation $\frac{df}{dx}$ is used interchangeably with $f'$.

**Worked Example.** Let $f(x) = x^3$. Applying the definition directly:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{3x^2 h + 3x h^2 + h^3}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \left(3x^2 + 3xh + h^2\right) = 3x^2.$$

Every term containing $h$ vanishes, leaving the derivative function $f'(x) = 3x^2$. At $x_0 = 2$, for instance, the tangent line has slope $f'(2) = 12$.

**Key Theorem (Differentiability implies Continuity).** If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0) = \dfrac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor converges to $f'(x_0)$ and the second to $0$, so the product tends to $0$, giving $\lim_{h\to 0} f(x_0+h) = f(x_0)$. $\square$

The converse fails: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there, since the left-hand and right-hand limits of the difference quotient are $-1$ and $+1$, respectively.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')
f = x**3
f_prime = sp.diff(f, x)          # derivative function
print(f_prime)                    # 3*x**2
print(f_prime.subs(x, 2))        # 12
sp.plot(f, f_prime, (x, -2, 2),
        legend=True, title="f and f'")
```

Running this cell displays both $f(x) = x^3$ and $f'(x) = 3x^2$, making the relationship between a function and its rate of change visually concrete.

### higher_order_derivatives (0.0s)

## Higher Order Derivatives

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a differentiable function on an open interval $I$. The *second derivative* of $f$, denoted $f''(x)$ or $\dfrac{d^2f}{dx^2}$, is the derivative of the first derivative:

$$f''(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f'(x+h) - f'(x)}{h}.$$

More generally, the *$n$-th order derivative* $f^{(n)}(x)$ is defined recursively by $f^{(0)} = f$ and $f^{(n)}(x) = \bigl(f^{(n-1)}\bigr)'(x)$ for $n \geq 1$, provided each successive derivative exists. The Leibniz notation for the $n$-th derivative is $\dfrac{d^n f}{dx^n}$.

**Worked Example.** Consider $f(x) = x^5 - 3x^3 + 2x$. Differentiating successively:

$$f'(x) = 5x^4 - 9x^2 + 2,$$
$$f''(x) = 20x^3 - 18x,$$
$$f'''(x) = 60x^2 - 18,$$
$$f^{(4)}(x) = 120x, \quad f^{(5)}(x) = 120, \quad f^{(n)}(x) = 0 \text{ for } n \geq 6.$$

This illustrates that every polynomial of degree $d$ vanishes identically after $d+1$ differentiations.

**Key Theorem (General Power Rule).** For $f(x) = x^n$ with $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ and $k \leq n$:

$$\frac{d^k}{dx^k}\bigl(x^n\bigr) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\, x^{n-k}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Applying the power rule $k$ times yields the falling factorial $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = \dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}$ as the coefficient, with exponent reduced to $n - k$ at each step. $\square$

Higher-order derivatives carry direct physical meaning: if $s(t)$ is position, then $s''(t)$ is acceleration and $s'''(t)$ is jerk. In analysis, the sign of $f''$ determines concavity, while the vanishing of all derivatives of order $\geq 2$ characterises linear functions.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')
f = x**5 - 3*x**3 + 2*x

for k in range(1, 7):
    dk = sp.diff(f, x, k)
    print(f"f^({k}) =", sp.simplify(dk))
```

Running this cell confirms the hand-computed derivatives above and shows $f^{(6)} = 0$, verifying the General Power Rule for degree-5 polynomials.

### velocity (0.0s)

## Velocity

**Definition.** Let a particle move along a straight line, and let $s(t)$ denote its position at time $t$, measured in metres from a fixed reference point. The *average velocity* over the interval $[t, t+h]$ is the ratio

$$\bar{v} = \frac{s(t+h) - s(t)}{h}.$$

The *instantaneous velocity* at time $t$ is the limit of this ratio as the interval shrinks to zero:

$$v(t) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{s(t+h) - s(t)}{h} = s'(t),$$

provided this limit exists. Velocity is therefore the derivative of position with respect to time; its SI unit is metres per second ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$). Note that velocity is a signed quantity: positive values indicate motion in the positive direction, negative values indicate motion in the opposite direction, and $v(t) = 0$ indicates the particle is momentarily at rest.

**Worked example.** Suppose $s(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 5$ (metres), $t \geq 0$ (seconds). Differentiating gives

$$v(t) = s'(t) = 6t - 12.$$

Setting $v(t) = 0$ yields $t = 2\,\mathrm{s}$, the instant at which the particle reverses direction. For $t < 2$ the velocity is negative (motion in the negative direction); for $t > 2$ it is positive. At $t = 3\,\mathrm{s}$, for instance, $v(3) = 6(3) - 12 = 6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

**Theorem (Mean Value Theorem for velocity).** If $s$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then there exists at least one instant $c \in (a, b)$ such that

$$v(c) = s'(c) = \frac{s(b) - s(a)}{b - a}.$$

In words, the instantaneous velocity equals the average velocity at some interior point of every finite time interval. This guarantees that the speedometer reading must pass through the average value—an intuitive but non-trivial fact.

**Lab cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

t = sp.Symbol('t', real=True)
s = 3*t**2 - 12*t + 5

v = sp.diff(s, t)
print("v(t) =", v)

t_rest = sp.solve(v, t)
print("Particle at rest when t =", t_rest)

print("v(3) =", v.subs(t, 3))
```

Running this cell confirms $v(t) = 6t - 12$, the rest instant $t = 2$, and $v(3) = 6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

### acceleration (0.0s)

## Acceleration

**Definition.** Let a particle move along a straight line with position function $x(t)$, where $t$ denotes time. The *acceleration* of the particle at time $t$ is the second derivative of position with respect to time:

$$a(t) = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = \frac{dv}{dt},$$

where $v(t) = dx/dt$ is the velocity. Equivalently, acceleration measures the instantaneous rate of change of velocity. In vector form in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the acceleration vector is $\mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{x}''(t)$, with SI units of metres per second squared ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$).

**Worked Example.** A particle's position is given by $x(t) = 3t^3 - 12t^2 + 9t$ metres, $t \geq 0$. Determine the acceleration at $t = 2\,\mathrm{s}$.

First compute the velocity:
$$v(t) = x'(t) = 9t^2 - 24t + 9.$$

Differentiating once more:
$$a(t) = v'(t) = 18t - 24.$$

Evaluating at $t = 2$:
$$a(2) = 18(2) - 24 = 36 - 24 = 12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.$$

The positive sign indicates the velocity is increasing at $t = 2\,\mathrm{s}$. Note that $v(2) = 9(4) - 48 + 9 = -3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; the particle moves in the negative direction but is slowing down (speed decreasing), since $a$ and $v$ have opposite signs.

**Key Theorem (Newton's Second Law).** For a particle of constant mass $m$, the net force $F$ acting on the particle and its acceleration are related by

$$F = ma.$$

This equation, the cornerstone of classical mechanics, asserts that acceleration is not an intrinsic property of motion but arises from an external cause. In particular, zero net force implies zero acceleration (Newton's First Law as a corollary), and the direction of acceleration coincides with the direction of the net force.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**
```python
import sympy as sp

t = sp.Symbol('t')
x = 3*t**3 - 12*t**2 + 9*t
v = sp.diff(x, t)
a = sp.diff(v, t)
print("v(t) =", v)
print("a(t) =", a)
print("a(2) =", a.subs(t, 2))   # expected: 12
```

Running this cell confirms $a(2) = 12$ and illustrates how symbolic differentiation eliminates arithmetic errors in multi-step kinematics problems.
