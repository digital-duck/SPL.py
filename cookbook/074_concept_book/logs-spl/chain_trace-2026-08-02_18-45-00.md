### limits_at_infinity (19.8s)

## Limits At Infinity

**Definition.** A limit at infinity describes how a function behaves as its input grows without bound. We write $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ to mean that $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to the value $L$ as $x$ increases without limit. Similarly, $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$ tracks behavior as $x$ decreases without bound. When such a limit exists and equals $L$, the horizontal line $y = L$ is called a **horizontal asymptote** of the graph.

**Key theorem.** For any rational exponent $n > 0$,

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{x^n} = 0$$

This single fact powers most limit-at-infinity calculations: any term with $x$ in the denominator vanishes as $x \to \infty$.

**Worked example.** Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5x + 1}{2x^2 + 4}$.

Direct substitution gives $\infty/\infty$, an indeterminate form. The standard technique is to **divide every term by the highest power of $x$ in the expression**, here $x^2$:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5x + 1}{2x^2 + 4} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3 - \dfrac{5}{x} + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{2 + \dfrac{4}{x^2}}$$

As $x \to \infty$, every term with $x$ in the denominator goes to zero:

$$= \frac{3 - 0 + 0}{2 + 0} = \frac{3}{2}$$

The function has a horizontal asymptote at $y = \tfrac{3}{2}$.

**Problem-solving pattern.** For rational functions, comparing the degrees of numerator and denominator gives a quick read:

- Degree of numerator $<$ degree of denominator: limit is $0$.
- Degrees equal: limit is the ratio of leading coefficients.
- Degree of numerator $>$ degree of denominator: limit is $\pm\infty$ (no horizontal asymptote).

For non-rational functions — exponentials, logarithms, or combinations — apply the same divide-by-highest-power idea, or reach for L'Hôpital's Rule when the form remains $\infty/\infty$ or $0/0$ after algebraic simplification.

**Practice.** Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sqrt{9x^2 + 1}}{x + 2}$. Hint: factor $x^2$ out of the square root carefully, noting that $\sqrt{x^2} = x$ for $x > 0$. The answer is $3$.

### derivative (0.2s)

## Derivative

The derivative of a function measures how fast its output changes relative to its input at any given point. More precisely, the derivative of $f$ at $x = a$ is the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to zero:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

Geometrically, this limit is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$. When the limit exists, $f$ is said to be **differentiable** at $a$. The derivative function $f'(x)$ gives you that slope at every point simultaneously.

**Worked Example.** Find the derivative of $f(x) = x^2$ at $x = 3$.

Apply the definition directly:

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(6 + h) = 6$$

So the slope of $y = x^2$ at $x = 3$ is $6$, and the tangent line there is $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$.

In practice, the power rule $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$ lets you skip the limit algebra: $f'(x) = 2x$, so $f'(3) = 6$ immediately.

**Problem-Solving Application.** A company's daily revenue (in thousands of dollars) is modeled by $R(x) = -2x^2 + 80x$, where $x$ is the number of units sold. To maximize revenue, you need the value of $x$ where the rate of change flips from positive to negative — that is, where $R'(x) = 0$.

Differentiate: $R'(x) = -4x + 80$. Setting this equal to zero gives $x = 20$. Since $R''(x) = -4 < 0$, the function is concave down, confirming this is a maximum. Selling 20 units yields peak daily revenue of $R(20) = -2(400) + 80(20) = \$800{,}000$.

This pattern — differentiate, set equal to zero, check concavity — is the core algorithm for optimization problems across physics, economics, and engineering. The derivative transforms a vague question ("where is the peak?") into a precise algebraic equation you can solve.

### indeterminate_forms (20.7s)

## Indeterminate Forms

When you evaluate a limit by direct substitution, you sometimes get an expression like $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$. These are called **indeterminate forms** — not because the limit fails to exist, but because the substituted expression alone carries no information about what the limit actually is. Two functions can both approach zero, yet their ratio might converge to $7$, blow up to $\infty$, or oscillate with no limit at all. The form $\frac{0}{0}$ tells you none of that; more work is required.

The seven standard indeterminate forms are:

$$\frac{0}{0}, \quad \frac{\infty}{\infty}, \quad 0 \cdot \infty, \quad \infty - \infty, \quad 0^0, \quad 1^\infty, \quad \infty^0$$

The primary tool for resolving the first two is **L'Hôpital's Rule**: if $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0$ (or both limits are $\pm\infty$), and $g'(x) \neq 0$ near $a$, then

$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

provided the right-hand limit exists.

**Worked example.** Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}$.

Direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, an indeterminate form. Apply L'Hôpital's Rule by differentiating numerator and denominator separately:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{1} = \cos 0 = 1$$

The limit is $1$ — a fact that underlies nearly every small-angle approximation in physics.

**Applying the concept.** The remaining forms require algebraic rewriting before L'Hôpital applies. For $0 \cdot \infty$, rewrite as a quotient: $f \cdot g = \frac{f}{1/g}$, which is now $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$. For exponential forms like $1^\infty$, take the natural log: if $L = \lim f(x)^{g(x)}$, then $\ln L = \lim g(x) \ln f(x)$, converting the exponent into a $0 \cdot \infty$ form you already know how to handle.

A common mistake is applying L'Hôpital's Rule when the limit is *not* indeterminate. Try $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x+1}{\sin x + 1}$: substitution gives $\frac{1}{1} = 1$ immediately. Differentiating anyway produces $\frac{1}{\cos 0} = 1$, accidentally correct here — but the rule is only valid when the indeterminate condition is met, so the habit of checking first is essential.

### lhopitals_rule (16.3s)

## L'Hôpital's Rule

When you evaluate a limit and the result takes the form $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$, you have reached an **indeterminate form** — the limit itself may still exist, but substitution alone cannot determine its value. L'Hôpital's Rule gives you a systematic way to resolve these cases.

**Theorem.** If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0$ (or both tend to $\pm\infty$), and if $g'(x) \neq 0$ near $a$, then

$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

provided the right-hand limit exists. The rule applies equally when $a$ is a finite number or $\pm\infty$.

The key idea: you are not differentiating a quotient (no quotient rule). You differentiate the numerator and denominator *separately*, then take the limit again.

---

**Worked example.** Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}$.

Direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$ — indeterminate. Apply L'Hôpital's Rule:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{1} = \cos 0 = 1$$

This confirms the famous result you may have seen used to derive other derivative formulas.

---

**Problem-solving application.** Some limits require the rule more than once, or need algebraic rewriting first. Consider $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^2}{e^x}$.

Substitution gives $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$. Apply once:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2x}{e^x}$$

Still $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$. Apply again:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2}{e^x} = 0$$

This confirms that exponential growth dominates polynomial growth — a qualitative insight made rigorous by the rule.

For forms like $0 \cdot \infty$ or $1^\infty$, rewrite the expression as a fraction before applying the rule. For example, $x \ln x$ as $x \to 0^+$ becomes $\frac{\ln x}{1/x}$, which is $\frac{-\infty}{\infty}$, and L'Hôpital's Rule then yields the limit $0$.

The practical skill is recognizing which form you have and whether a quick algebraic rearrangement is needed before differentiating.

### growth_rates (24.0s)

## Growth Rates

**Definition.** The *growth rate* of a function describes how quickly its output increases as the input grows. When comparing two functions $f(n)$ and $g(n)$, we say $f$ grows faster than $g$ if the ratio $f(n)/g(n) \to \infty$ as $n \to \infty$. The standard hierarchy, from slowest to fastest, is:

$$\log n \;\ll\; \sqrt{n} \;\ll\; n \;\ll\; n^2 \;\ll\; 2^n \;\ll\; n!$$

The double inequality $\ll$ means "grows strictly slower than in the limit." This ordering is not just theoretical — it determines whether an algorithm can handle a dataset of one million rows in a second or would take longer than the age of the universe.

**Worked example.** Suppose you have three algorithms solving the same problem, with running times $T_1(n) = 10\log_2 n$, $T_2(n) = 5n$, and $T_3(n) = 2^n$ (all in milliseconds). At $n = 20$:

- $T_1(20) = 10 \cdot \log_2 20 \approx 43\ \text{ms}$
- $T_2(20) = 100\ \text{ms}$
- $T_3(20) = 2^{20} \approx 1{,}048{,}576\ \text{ms} \approx 17\ \text{min}$

Scaling to $n = 40$: $T_1$ barely budges ($\approx 53\ \text{ms}$), $T_2$ doubles ($200\ \text{ms}$), and $T_3$ becomes $2^{40} \approx 12.7$ days. A constant-factor difference in the coefficient (10 vs 5) matters far less than which growth class you are in.

**Problem-solving application.** A database team needs to search a sorted list of $n = 10^6$ records. They are choosing between a linear scan $O(n)$ and binary search $O(\log n)$. To estimate the practical difference:

- Linear scan: $10^6$ comparisons
- Binary search: $\log_2(10^6) \approx 20$ comparisons

If each comparison takes $1\ \mu\text{s}$, the linear scan takes $\approx 1\ \text{s}$ while binary search takes $\approx 20\ \mu\text{s}$ — a 50,000$\times$ speedup. The decision rule is: **always identify the growth class first, then optimize constants**. No amount of micro-optimization to the linear scan closes the gap once $n$ is large.

When analyzing a new algorithm, ask: does doubling $n$ double the work (linear), square the work (quadratic), or add only one step (logarithmic)? That single question tells you more about scalability than any benchmark at small $n$.
