### sequence (0.1s)

## Sequence

Imagine you're saving money for a bicycle. Week one you have $5. Week two, $10. Week three, $15. You don't need a formula to see what's happening — your brain already feels the pattern. That feeling *is* the concept of a sequence.

A **sequence** is simply a list of numbers written in a definite order, where the position of each number matters. The first number isn't interchangeable with the second; order is everything. Think of your savings as a story: each chapter reveals the next number, and skipping a chapter breaks the narrative.

We write a sequence using subscripts to name each term by its position:

$$a_1, \; a_2, \; a_3, \; \ldots, \; a_n, \; \ldots$$

Here $a_n$ is the $n$-th term — the value at step $n$. In the savings story, $a_n = 5n$, so $a_1 = 5$, $a_2 = 10$, and so on. The rule $a_n = 5n$ is called the **general term** or **closed form** — a single expression that generates any term directly without listing all the ones before it.

Not every sequence grows so neatly. The sequence $1, -1, 1, -1, \ldots$ alternates forever and never settles. The sequence $1, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \ldots$ shrinks toward zero but never quite arrives. Each is a valid sequence — a list in order — and studying where they *head* is the next great question.

**Now you try.** Consider the sequence defined by $a_n = n^2$. Write out the first five terms. Does the gap between consecutive terms grow, shrink, or stay constant? What does that tell you about how fast the sequence is accelerating?

### seasonal_component (0.1s)

## Seasonal Component

Picture a coffee shop's weekly sales. Monday is slow, Friday buzzes, Saturday peaks, Sunday dips again. Come next week, the same rhythm repeats. That repeating, calendar-driven pulse is the seasonal component — the part of any pattern that cycles back on a predictable schedule.

**Why does this feel right?**

Human life runs on cycles: days, weeks, months, years. Ice cream sells more in July than January. Electricity spikes every morning when people shower. Retail explodes every December. These are not random fluctuations — they are *structured* repetitions baked into the calendar. Once you see that, you realise any measurement of the world carries two very different kinds of movement: long-run drift (trend) and this rhythmic heartbeat (season).

**Building the intuition**

Imagine peeling an orange. The trend is the whole orange — the general size and weight. The seasonal component is one repeating segment. If you know the segment shape, you can predict where it sits in next week's slice before you even look.

That image leads naturally to one formal idea. If your time series is $y_t$, you can write:

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

where $T_t$ is the trend you already know, $S_t$ is the seasonal component, and $R_t$ is leftover noise. This section's single new object is $S_t$ — the piece that repeats with period $m$. Everything else in the equation is a placeholder we will unpack in later sections.

**Now you try**

Take your weekly step-count for the past month. Label each day of the week. Do Saturdays look consistently higher than Tuesdays? If yes, you have just spotted a seasonal component with period $m = 7$ — without touching any algebra at all.

### time_series (0.4s)

## Time Series

Imagine you check your city's temperature every morning at 8 a.m. and write it down. After a year, you have 365 numbers. But here's what makes this interesting: the *order* matters. Tuesday's reading depends on Monday's. Winter readings cluster near freezing; summer readings cluster near scorching. If you shuffled those 365 numbers into a random order, you'd lose something real — the shape of the seasons, the memory of yesterday's cold front.

That's the heart of a time series: a sequence of measurements where *position in time* is as important as the value itself.

**Why does order matter?** Because the world has inertia. Prices don't jump randomly from $5 to $500 overnight. Your heart rate right now is close to what it was one second ago. Time-ordered data carries *autocorrelation* — the technical name for "today resembles yesterday."

Formally, a time series is a collection of observations indexed by time:

$$x_1,\ x_2,\ x_3,\ \ldots,\ x_T$$

where $x_t$ is the value at time step $t$. The gap between steps (one second, one day, one year) is called the **sampling interval**. Keep it consistent, or the math gets messy.

Two patterns to look for immediately:

- **Trend** — does the overall level drift upward or downward over time?
- **Seasonality** — does a pattern repeat on a fixed cycle (daily, weekly, yearly)?

A city's electricity demand has both: a long-term rise as the population grows, and a daily spike every evening when people come home.

**Now you try.** Take your phone's step count for the last 30 days. Sketch it roughly. Can you spot a weekly rhythm — fewer steps on weekends, perhaps? That's seasonality revealing itself, hiding in plain sight.

### trend (0.3s)

## Trend

Imagine you track your morning coffee temperature every minute after pouring it. Minute one: 85°C. Minute two: 78°C. Minute three: 71°C. The exact numbers jump around depending on the room, the mug, your measurement. But something deeper is obvious — the coffee is *cooling down*. That persistent direction hiding inside the noise is the trend.

A trend is not a fact about any single data point. It is a fact about the *whole sequence* — the underlying drift that would remain if you smoothed away every accidental wiggle. Think of it as the river current beneath choppy surface waves. Waves vary; the current flows steadily in one direction.

The simplest way to formalise this intuition is to ask: **on average, how much does the measurement change each step?** That average change is the trend. If temperatures drop roughly 7°C every minute, the trend is −7°C per minute. We call this number $b$. Positive $b$ means the sequence is rising; negative means falling; zero means no overall drift. Finding the $b$ that best fits all the points at once is a matter of calculation, but the key insight is simpler than any formula: *$b$ is the trend*. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Trends appear everywhere — stock prices, global temperature, your running pace over weeks of training. Spotting one lets you separate *signal* (where things are headed) from *noise* (the random detours along the way).

**Now you try.** A plant measures 2 cm, 5 cm, 8 cm, 11 cm over four weeks. What is the trend $b$ — how many centimetres per week does it grow on average? Does anything surprise you about how steady it is?

### volatility (15.4s)

## Volatility

Imagine you and a friend both invest $1,000 for a year. At the end, you've made exactly $50 every single month — smooth, predictable, boring. Your friend, however, gained $300 one month, lost $200 the next, gained $400, lost $150 — and ended up with the same $600 total profit as you. Who took the bigger risk?

Your friend did. Even though the *average* return was identical, her portfolio was on a rollercoaster. That rollercoaster is volatility: not how much you gain or lose on average, but how *wildly the path wanders* to get there.

Here's the core intuition. Imagine plotting daily returns as dots on a number line. If all the dots cluster tightly around zero, the investment is calm — low volatility. If the dots scatter far and wide, something chaotic is happening — high volatility. Volatility is simply a measure of that scatter.

The standard way to quantify scatter is the **standard deviation** $\sigma$. Given $n$ returns $r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n$ with mean $\bar{r}$:

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_i - \bar{r})^2}$$

Each $(r_i - \bar{r})^2$ asks "how far did this return stray from average?" Squaring removes negatives, averaging smooths them out, and the square root brings you back to the original units. A large $\sigma$ means the returns are all over the place; a small $\sigma$ means they stay close to the mean.

Volatility doesn't tell you whether something is going up or down — only how *unpredictable* the movement is. Predictably bad is, in a strange sense, less scary than chaotically good.

**Now you try.** Suppose a stock has daily returns (in %) of $\{2, -1, 3, -2, 3\}$. Compute the mean, then compute $\sigma$. Is this stock more or less volatile than one with returns $\{1, 1, 1, 1, 1\}$?

### stationary (20.4s)

## Stationary

Imagine you are hiking up a mountain. For most of the trail, every step either takes you higher or lower. But right at the summit — for one fleeting moment — you are neither climbing nor descending. The slope beneath your feet is perfectly flat. That moment, that point, is what mathematicians call **stationary**.

The word sounds passive, almost boring. But it is secretly one of the most powerful ideas in all of mathematics, because the universe loves stationary points. Nature tends to settle at them: soap bubbles minimize surface area, planets trace paths that minimize travel time, and trained neural networks hunt for stationary valleys in a vast loss landscape.

Here is the core intuition: a function $f(x)$ is racing upward or plunging downward almost everywhere — its slope is nonzero. At a stationary point, that race pauses. The slope is exactly zero. This can happen at a peak (local maximum), a valley (local minimum), or a flat shoulder where the function neither rises nor falls for an instant before continuing (a saddle point).

The algebra that captures this is almost embarrassingly simple. A point $x_0$ is stationary when

$$f'(x_0) = 0.$$

That single equation encodes the whole geometric story: the tangent line at $x_0$ is perfectly horizontal.

For example, $f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 5$ has derivative $f'(x) = 2x - 4$. Setting $2x - 4 = 0$ gives $x_0 = 2$. Sketch the parabola — sure enough, the bottom of the cup sits right at $x = 2$.

**Now you try.** Find the stationary point of $g(x) = -x^2 + 6x$. Is it a peak or a valley, and how can you tell just by looking at the sign of the coefficient of $x^2$?

### augmented_dickey_fuller_test (37.0s)

## Augmented Dickey-Fuller Test

Imagine you track the temperature outside every day for a year. Some days are hotter, some cooler, but the readings hover around a familiar range — they don't drift to infinity. Now imagine tracking a stock price instead. It might wander endlessly upward or downward, never "returning home." These two behaviors feel fundamentally different. The ADF test is the tool statisticians use to tell them apart.

The one idea that unlocks everything here is a single number: **φ** (phi). Ask yourself: when today's value is large, does the series feel a pull back toward the middle? If yes, that pull shows up as a negative φ. If there is no pull at all — yesterday's value just carries forward unchanged — then φ is zero, and the series wanders like a coin-flip running total, accumulating errors forever without any memory of where it started.

The Dickey-Fuller idea is to write the *change* from one period to the next:

$$\Delta y_t = \phi \, y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$$

A negative φ means large values get dragged back — like a rubber band. φ equal to zero means pure drift. So the test is simply asking: is φ significantly negative?

The *augmented* version adds a few extra past-change terms to clean up lingering patterns that would otherwise distort the estimate of φ. They are bookkeeping, not a new idea. The conclusion is the same: a very negative test statistic means the rubber band is real, and the series is safe to model.

**Now you try.** Take a coin-flip sequence where each value is the running total of heads minus tails. Does it feel like temperature or a stock price? Think about φ: is there any force pulling it back toward zero?
