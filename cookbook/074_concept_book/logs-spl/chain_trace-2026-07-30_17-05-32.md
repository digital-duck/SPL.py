## Geospatial Data

**Definition.** Geospatial data is any data set in which each record is associated with a location on or near the Earth's surface. A location is typically encoded as a coordinate pair $(\lambda, \phi)$, where $\lambda \in [-180°, 180°]$ denotes *longitude* and $\phi \in [-90°, 90°]$ denotes *latitude*, expressed in decimal degrees under the WGS 84 datum. More formally, a geospatial data set is a collection of tuples

$$\mathcal{G} = \{(g_i, \mathbf{a}_i) : i = 1, \dots, n\},$$

where $g_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ (or a richer geometry such as a line string or polygon) is the *spatial component*, and $\mathbf{a}_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$ is a vector of *attribute values* (temperature, population, elevation, etc.).

**Coordinate Reference Systems.** Because the Earth is an oblate spheroid, coordinates must be interpreted relative to a *coordinate reference system* (CRS). The geographic CRS EPSG:4326 uses angular degrees on the ellipsoid, while projected CRSs such as UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator) map regions of the ellipsoid onto a flat plane, yielding Cartesian coordinates in metres. Distances computed directly from degree differences are meaningless without projection; the great-circle distance between two points $(\lambda_1, \phi_1)$ and $(\lambda_2, \phi_2)$ is given by the Haversine formula:

$$d = 2R \arcsin\!\left(\sqrt{\sin^2\!\tfrac{\Delta\phi}{2} + \cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2\sin^2\!\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2}}\right),$$

where $R \approx 6{,}371\,\text{km}$ is the mean Earth radius and angles are in radians.

**Worked Example.** Suppose two weather stations are located at $A = (-73.94°,\, 40.67°)$ (Brooklyn, NY) and $B = (-87.63°,\, 41.88°)$ (Chicago, IL). Converting to radians: $\Delta\phi = (41.88 - 40.67)\frac{\pi}{180} \approx 0.02112\,\text{rad}$, $\Delta\lambda = (-87.63 + 73.94)\frac{\pi}{180} \approx -0.23900\,\text{rad}$. Substituting into the Haversine formula yields $d \approx 1{,}149\,\text{km}$, consistent with published geodetic tables.

**Key Theorem (Triangle Inequality on the Sphere).** For any three points $P, Q, R$ on the sphere of radius $R$, the great-circle distances satisfy $d(P, R) \leq d(P, Q) + d(Q, R)$. This ensures that great-circle distance is a proper metric, which underpins spatial indexing structures such as k-d trees and R-trees used in geographic information systems.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, sin, cos, asin, sqrt, pi, N

lam1, phi1 = -73.94 * pi/180, 40.67 * pi/180  # Brooklyn
lam2, phi2 = -87.63 * pi/180, 41.88 * pi/180  # Chicago
R = 6371  # km

dlam = lam2 - lam1
dphi = phi2 - phi1
a = sin(dphi/2)**2 + cos(phi1)*cos(phi2)*sin(dlam/2)**2
d = 2 * R * asin(sqrt(a))
print(f"Great-circle distance: {N(d, 6)} km")
```

## Spatial Heatmap

**Definition.** A *spatial heatmap* is a function $H: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on a bounded spatial domain $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, together with a visualization scheme that encodes the scalar value $H(x,y)$ as a color at each point $(x,y) \in \Omega$. Formally, a color map $\mathcal{C}: \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]^3$ maps each scalar to an RGB triple, producing an image that allows a human observer to perceive the continuous field $H$ at a glance. In discrete applications, $H$ is sampled on a grid $\{(x_i, y_j)\}$ of size $m \times n$, yielding a matrix $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ whose entry $H_{ij} = H(x_i, y_j)$ represents the field intensity at cell $(i,j)$.

**Worked Example.** Consider a thin metal plate occupying $\Omega = [0,1]^2$ whose steady-state temperature is governed by a known analytic function:

$$T(x,y) = \sin(\pi x)\,\sin(\pi y), \quad (x,y)\in[0,1]^2.$$

Sample this function on a $50\times50$ uniform grid: $x_i = i/49$, $y_j = j/49$ for $0 \le i,j \le 49$. The matrix $\mathbf{T}$ with $T_{ij} = \sin(\pi x_i)\sin(\pi y_j)$ attains its maximum value of $1$ at the plate center $(0.5, 0.5)$ and vanishes on all four edges, consistent with Dirichlet boundary conditions $T = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$. Rendering $\mathbf{T}$ with a diverging color map immediately reveals this bowl-shaped profile.

**Key Theorem (Extrema Correspondence).** Let $H \in C(\overline{\Omega})$ be continuous on a compact domain. Then the global maximum and minimum of $H$ correspond to the brightest and darkest cells in any strictly monotone color map $\mathcal{C}$. Consequently, visual inspection of the heatmap locates the extrema of $H$ up to the grid resolution $\delta = \max(\Delta x, \Delta y)$.

*Proof sketch.* Continuity on a compact set guarantees $H$ attains its supremum and infimum (Extreme Value Theorem). A strictly monotone $\mathcal{C}$ is an order-preserving bijection, so the color extremes coincide exactly with the scalar extremes. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x, y = sp.symbols('x y')
T_sym = sp.sin(sp.pi * x) * sp.sin(sp.pi * y)
T_func = sp.lambdify((x, y), T_sym, 'numpy')

xs = np.linspace(0, 1, 50)
ys = np.linspace(0, 1, 50)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, ys)
Z = T_func(X, Y)

plt.figure(figsize=(5, 4))
plt.pcolormesh(X, Y, Z, cmap='RdBu_r', shading='auto')
plt.colorbar(label='T(x,y)')
plt.title('Steady-state temperature heatmap')
plt.xlabel('x'); plt.ylabel('y')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

Running this cell produces a symmetric heatmap confirming that temperature peaks at the plate center and satisfies the zero-boundary condition on all sides.

## Choropleth Graph

**Definition.** A *choropleth graph* (from Greek *chōra*, region, and *plēthos*, multitude) is a thematic map in which geographic regions are shaded or colored in proportion to a statistical variable defined on those regions. Formally, let $\mathcal{R} = \{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n\}$ be a finite collection of non-overlapping polygonal regions partitioning a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, and let $f : \mathcal{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a scalar-valued attribute function. A choropleth graph is the pair $(\mathcal{R}, f)$ together with a color map $\phi : [\min f, \max f] \to \mathcal{C}$, where $\mathcal{C}$ is a perceptually ordered color space. Each region $r_i$ is rendered with color $\phi(f(r_i))$.

The color map $\phi$ is typically constructed by first normalizing values via

$$\hat{f}(r_i) = \frac{f(r_i) - \min_j f(r_j)}{\max_j f(r_j) - \min_j f(r_j)} \in [0, 1],$$

then mapping $\hat{f}(r_i)$ to a hue or luminance gradient. Sequential maps suit data with a natural zero; diverging maps center on a meaningful midpoint $\mu$, mapping deviations $f(r_i) - \mu$ symmetrically to two contrasting hues.

**Worked Example.** Suppose five counties have population densities (persons per km²): $\{12, 45, 78, 34, 91\}$. Normalizing gives $\hat{f}$ values of $\{0.00, 0.42, 0.84, 0.28, 1.00\}$. Under a sequential blue scale, the county with density $91$ receives the darkest shade, while the county with density $12$ receives the lightest, immediately conveying spatial clustering of high-density areas.

**Key Theorem (Visual Preattentive Processing).** Empirical studies in perceptual psychology establish that hue and luminance differences of sufficient magnitude ($\Delta E \geq 3$ in CIELAB space) are detected by the human visual system in under 250 ms, prior to focused attention. Consequently, a well-designed choropleth communicates the spatial distribution of $f$ as a gestalt, enabling pattern recognition at $O(1)$ cognitive cost relative to tabular data requiring $O(n)$ sequential scan.

A critical limitation is the *area-size bias*: large regions dominate visual attention regardless of their $f$ value, potentially misleading interpretation. Cartogram transformations rescale region area proportionally to $f(r_i)$, partially correcting this distortion.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import geopandas as gpd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

world = gpd.read_file(gpd.datasets.get_path("naturalearth_lowres"))
world["log_pop"] = world["pop_est"].apply(lambda x: x**0.5)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 1, figsize=(12, 6))
world.plot(
    column="log_pop",
    cmap="YlOrRd",
    legend=True,
    legend_kwds={"label": r"$\sqrt{\mathrm{Population}}$"},
    ax=ax,
)
ax.set_title("World Population Choropleth ($\\sqrt{\\cdot}$ scale)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The square-root transform compresses the dynamic range of $f$, reducing the visual dominance of extreme outliers and revealing structure in mid-range regions.
