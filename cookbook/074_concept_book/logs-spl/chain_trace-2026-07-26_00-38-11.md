### limits_at_infinity (15.3s)

## Limits At Infinity

**Definition.** Let $f$ be a function defined on some interval $(a, \infty)$. We say that the *limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches infinity* is $L$, written

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L,$$

if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $M > 0$ such that whenever $x > M$, we have $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. Geometrically, this means the graph of $f$ approaches the horizontal line $y = L$ as $x$ grows without bound; such a line is called a **horizontal asymptote**. An analogous definition holds for $x \to -\infty$.

---

**Worked Example.** Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5x + 1}{2x^2 + 7}$.

Divide every term in the numerator and denominator by $x^2$, the highest power appearing:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3 - \tfrac{5}{x} + \tfrac{1}{x^2}}{2 + \tfrac{7}{x^2}}.$$

As $x \to \infty$, each term of the form $\tfrac{c}{x^k}$ (with $k \geq 1$) tends to $0$, leaving

$$\frac{3 - 0 + 0}{2 + 0} = \frac{3}{2}.$$

Hence the function has the horizontal asymptote $y = \tfrac{3}{2}$ in both directions.

---

**Key Theorem (Rational Function Limits at Infinity).** Let $f(x) = p(x)/q(x)$ where $p$ and $q$ are polynomials of degrees $m$ and $n$, respectively, and $q(x) \neq 0$ for all sufficiently large $x$.

- If $m < n$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = 0$.
- If $m = n$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \frac{a_m}{b_n}$, the ratio of leading coefficients.
- If $m > n$, then $|f(x)| \to \infty$ and no finite limit exists.

*Proof sketch.* Factor $x^n$ from $q(x)$ and $x^m$ from $p(x)$; the remaining factors each approach their leading coefficients, and $x^{m-n} \to 0$, $1$, or $\pm\infty$ according to the sign of $m - n$.

---

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit, oo, Rational

x = symbols('x')
f = (3*x**2 - 5*x + 1) / (2*x**2 + 7)

print(limit(f, x,  oo))   # Expected: 3/2
print(limit(f, x, -oo))   # Expected: 3/2

g = (x**3 + 1) / (x**2 - 4)
print(limit(g, x, oo))    # Expected: oo  (degree 3 > degree 2)
```

Experiment by changing the polynomial degrees and observe how the output shifts among $0$, a finite constant, and $\infty$, confirming the theorem in each case.

### derivative (0.0s)

## Derivative

**Definition.** Let $f : (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function and let $x_0 \in (a, b)$. The *derivative* of $f$ at $x_0$ is defined as the limit

$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists and is finite. When the limit exists, $f$ is said to be *differentiable* at $x_0$. The quantity $\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}$ is the *difference quotient*, representing the slope of the secant line through $(x_0, f(x_0))$ and $(x_0+h, f(x_0+h))$; the derivative is the slope of the tangent line obtained as $h \to 0$.

**Worked Example.** Compute $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^3$.

Applying the definition directly:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^3 - x^3}{h}.$$

Expanding $(x+h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3$ gives

$$\frac{(x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3xh^2 + h^3) - x^3}{h} = 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2.$$

Taking $h \to 0$ yields $f'(x) = 3x^2$, confirming the power rule $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$ for $n = 3$.

**Key Theorem (Differentiability Implies Continuity).** If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0) = \frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor converges to $f'(x_0)$ and the second factor converges to $0$, so $\lim_{h \to 0}[f(x_0+h) - f(x_0)] = f'(x_0) \cdot 0 = 0$, establishing continuity. The converse fails: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $x = 0$. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, diff, limit, Function, simplify

x, h = symbols('x h')
f = x**3

# Derivative via limit definition
dq = (f.subs(x, x + h) - f) / h
deriv_limit = limit(dq, h, 0)
print("Limit definition:", deriv_limit)   # 3*x**2

# Derivative via diff
deriv_diff = diff(f, x)
print("diff():", deriv_diff)              # 3*x**2

assert simplify(deriv_limit - deriv_diff) == 0
```

Both methods confirm $\frac{d}{dx} x^3 = 3x^2$, and `simplify` verifies that the two expressions are identical.

### indeterminate_forms (15.3s)

## Indeterminate Forms

**Definition.** An *indeterminate form* arises when evaluating a limit produces an expression whose value cannot be determined from the individual limits of its parts alone. The classical indeterminate forms are

$$\frac{0}{0}, \quad \frac{\infty}{\infty}, \quad 0\cdot\infty, \quad \infty - \infty, \quad 0^{0}, \quad 1^{\infty}, \quad \infty^{0}.$$

More precisely, if $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = 0$, then $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)/g(x)$ is called a $0/0$ indeterminate form because the ratio may converge to any real number, diverge to $\pm\infty$, or fail to exist — depending entirely on the relative rates at which $f$ and $g$ approach zero.

**Worked Example.** Consider

$$L = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}.$$

Direct substitution gives $0/0$, an indeterminate form. Geometrically, the unit-circle squeeze argument establishes $\cos x \leq \frac{\sin x}{x} \leq 1$ for $x \in (-\pi/2, 0)\cup(0,\pi/2)$; since $\cos x \to 1$, the Squeeze Theorem forces $L = 1$. This example illustrates that an indeterminate form is *not* undefined — it simply requires further analysis.

**Key Theorem (L'Hôpital's Rule).** Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable on a punctured neighbourhood of $a$, with $g'(x) \neq 0$ near $a$. If $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)/g(x)$ is of type $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$, and if

$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} = L \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty, -\infty\},$$

then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)/g(x) = L$. The other indeterminate forms reduce to $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$ via algebraic manipulation: for $0\cdot\infty$, write $f\cdot g = f/(1/g)$; for exponential forms such as $1^\infty$, take logarithms to convert $g\ln f \to 0\cdot\infty$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import *
x = symbols('x')

# 0/0 form
print(limit(sin(x)/x, x, 0))          # 1

# infinity/infinity form
print(limit((3*x**2 + 1)/(x**2 - 5), x, oo))   # 3

# 1^infinity form
print(limit((1 + 1/x)**x, x, oo))     # E
```

Each call invokes SymPy's internal limit engine, which applies L'Hôpital's rule and series expansions automatically, confirming the analytic results.

### lhopitals_rule (13.3s)

## L'Hôpital's Rule

**Definition.** Let $f$ and $g$ be functions that are differentiable on an open interval $I$ containing $a$ (except possibly at $a$ itself), with $g'(x) \neq 0$ on $I \setminus \{a\}$. If the limit $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)/g(x)$ produces an indeterminate form of type $0/0$ or $\pm\infty/\pm\infty$, then

$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)},$$

provided the right-hand limit exists (or equals $\pm\infty$). The rule applies equally to one-sided limits and to limits as $x \to \pm\infty$.

---

**Worked Example.** Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}$.

Direct substitution gives $\sin 0 / 0 = 0/0$, an indeterminate form. Differentiating numerator and denominator separately:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{1} = \cos 0 = 1.$$

As a second illustration, consider $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x}{x^2}$, which has the form $\infty/\infty$. Applying the rule twice:

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x}{x^2} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x}{2x} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{e^x}{2} = \infty.$$

This confirms that exponential growth dominates any polynomial.

---

**Key Theorem (Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem).** The rigorous foundation for L'Hôpital's Rule is the Cauchy Mean Value Theorem: if $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that

$$[f(b) - f(a)]\, g'(c) = [g(b) - g(a)]\, f'(c).$$

By applying this theorem to the interval $[a, a+h]$ as $h \to 0$, one can show that the ratio $f(x)/g(x)$ converges to the same limit as $f'(x)/g'(x)$ whenever the hypotheses of L'Hôpital's Rule are satisfied. A complete proof requires careful treatment of the $\infty/\infty$ case using nested applications of the theorem.

---

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, sin, exp, limit, oo, diff

x = symbols('x')

# 0/0 form
expr1 = sin(x) / x
print(limit(expr1, x, 0))          # 1

# inf/inf form
expr2 = exp(x) / x**2
print(limit(expr2, x, oo))         # oo

# Verify by differentiating numerator and denominator manually
f, g = sin(x), x
print(limit(diff(f)/diff(g), x, 0))  # 1
```

## Growth Rates

**Definition.** Let $f$ and $g$ be positive-valued functions defined on $[a, \infty)$. We say $f$ *grows no faster than* $g$, written $f(x) = O(g(x))$ as $x \to \infty$, if there exist constants $C > 0$ and $x_0 \geq a$ such that

$$f(x) \leq C \cdot g(x) \quad \text{for all } x \geq x_0.$$

If additionally $g(x) = O(f(x))$, the two functions are *asymptotically equivalent in order*, written $f(x) = \Theta(g(x))$. The standard growth hierarchy, from slowest to fastest, is

$$1 \;\prec\; \log x \;\prec\; x^\alpha \;\prec\; x^\beta \;\prec\; a^x \;\prec\; x! \;\prec\; x^x$$

for any fixed $0 < \alpha < \beta$ and $a > 1$.

**Worked Example.** Compare $f(x) = 3x^2 + 5x$ and $g(x) = x^2$.

For $x \geq 1$ we have $5x \leq 5x^2$, so

$$f(x) = 3x^2 + 5x \leq 8x^2 = 8\, g(x).$$

Hence $f(x) = O(x^2)$. In the other direction, $f(x) \geq 3x^2 = 3\, g(x)$ for all $x > 0$, so $g(x) = O(f(x))$ as well. Therefore $f(x) = \Theta(x^2)$: the linear term is dominated by the quadratic and does not affect the asymptotic order.

**Key Theorem (L'Hôpital Growth Comparison).** Suppose $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, $g(x) \to \infty$, and

$$L = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$$

exists (finitely or as $+\infty$). Then:

- $L = 0 \implies f(x) = o(g(x))$ (strictly slower growth),
- $0 < L < \infty \implies f(x) = \Theta(g(x))$,
- $L = +\infty \implies g(x) = o(f(x))$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply L'Hôpital's rule or the squeeze theorem to bound the ratio within $[\,L - \varepsilon,\, L + \varepsilon\,]$ for sufficiently large $x$, then read off the $O$-constants directly from the bounding constants. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, limit, oo, log, exp, factorial

x = symbols('x', positive=True)

pairs = [
    (log(x),    x),
    (x**2,      exp(x)),
    (3*x**2 + 5*x, x**2),
]

for f, g in pairs:
    L = limit(f / g, x, oo)
    print(f"lim ({f}) / ({g})  =  {L}")
```

Running this cell confirms the hierarchy: $\log x / x \to 0$, $x^2 / e^x \to 0$, and $(3x^2+5x)/x^2 \to 3$, validating both the strict dominance cases and the $\Theta$ relationship demonstrated above.
