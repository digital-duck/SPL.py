### sequence (13.3s)

## Sequence

You already know the intuition: a sequence is a list of things in a specific order. But there's more structure here than a plain list. Unlike a *set*, where order doesn't matter, a sequence cares deeply about position — the third term is different from the first, even if they happen to have the same value.

**Formal structure.** A sequence is formally written as $(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots)$, where each $a_n$ is called the **$n$-th term** and $n$ is its *index*. The index usually starts at $1$ (sometimes $0$). What makes a sequence powerful is that each term can be defined by a **rule** — a formula that takes an index and produces a value.

Two common types:

- **Explicit formula**: gives you $a_n$ directly. For example, $a_n = 2n + 1$ produces $3, 5, 7, 9, \ldots$
- **Recursive formula**: defines each term using the one before it. For example, $a_1 = 1$ and $a_n = a_{n-1} + 3$ produces $1, 4, 7, 10, \ldots$

**Pattern to remember.** If consecutive terms differ by a constant amount $d$, you have an *arithmetic sequence* and the explicit formula is always:

$$a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d$$

This single formula lets you jump directly to any term without listing everything before it.

### time_series (36.3s)

## Time Series

You already know that a sequence of numbers can tell a story — your height measured every birthday, the temperature at noon each day, or the closing price of a stock each Friday. When measurements are taken **at regular intervals over time**, the resulting list is called a **time series**. The key idea is that *order matters*: shuffling the values destroys the meaning.

**Structure of a time series**

A time series is written as $x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots, x_n$, where the subscript $t$ labels the time step and $x_t$ is the observation at step $t$.

One of the most important features to look for is a **trend** — a long-run direction (rising, falling, or flat). For example, global average temperature has shown an upward trend over recent decades. Spotting a trend in raw data can be tricky, though, because every real measurement comes with a little random noise that makes the picture jagged.

**A simple tool: the moving average**

To smooth out noise and reveal the trend, replace each value with the average of its neighbors:

$$\bar{x}_t = \frac{x_{t-1} + x_t + x_{t+1}}{3}$$

This three-point moving average is one of the simplest tools in data analysis — it keeps the overall direction while ironing out the bumps.

### trend (14.5s)

## Trend

You already know what a trend feels like: temperatures rise as summer approaches, social media followers grow after a viral post, a phone's battery drains throughout the day. A **trend** is the overall direction a quantity moves over time — not every small wiggle, but the big picture shape.

### The Structure Behind the Feeling

To make "trend" precise, mathematicians look at how one variable changes as another increases. The most important case is a **linear trend**, where data roughly follows a straight line. That line has a **slope**, which tells you the rate of change:

$$m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

If $m > 0$, the trend is **positive** (values rise). If $m < 0$, the trend is **negative** (values fall). If $m \approx 0$, there is **no clear trend** — the data is roughly flat.

Real data is messier than a perfect line, so analysts draw a **line of best fit** (also called a trend line) that minimizes the total distance between the line and the data points. This line captures the trend while ignoring random noise.

### The Quick Rule

> The sign of the slope tells you the trend; the magnitude tells you how strong it is.

A slope of $m = 5$ means the quantity grows by 5 units for every 1-unit step forward. A slope of $m = 0.1$ is a much gentler rise.

### differencing (13.4s)

## Differencing

You already have a feel for this: if a friend ran 5 km yesterday and 8 km today, the *change* is 3 km. Differencing is just that idea applied systematically to a whole sequence of numbers — subtract each value from the one that follows it.

**The structure.** Given a sequence $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots, a_n$, the *first difference* sequence is:

$$\Delta a_k = a_{k+1} - a_k$$

So the first difference sequence has $n - 1$ terms. You can then difference *again* to get second differences, $\Delta^2 a_k = \Delta a_{k+1} - \Delta a_k$, and so on.

**Why it matters.** Differencing reveals hidden patterns. Consider the squares:

$$1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ 25, \ldots$$

First differences: $3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 9, \ldots$ — an arithmetic sequence!  
Second differences: $2,\ 2,\ 2, \ldots$ — constant.

Whenever the $d$-th differences are all the same nonzero number, the original sequence is described by a degree-$d$ polynomial. This is a powerful shortcut: you don't need to guess the formula; the differences tell you the degree automatically.

**Simple rule.** If the $d$-th difference row is constant, the sequence is polynomial of degree $d$. If no difference row ever becomes constant, the sequence grows faster than any polynomial (exponential, for instance).

### error (16.2s)

## Error

You already know that measurements are never perfect — a ruler can only be so precise, and rounding $\pi$ to $3.14$ leaves something out. That leftover gap has a name: **error**. But in mathematics, error is not a mistake to be ashamed of; it is a quantity to be measured, managed, and minimized.

**Absolute error** is the simplest form. If the true value is $x$ and your estimate is $\hat{x}$, then:

$$\text{Absolute Error} = |x - \hat{x}|$$

The absolute value bars matter — error is always non-negative, because we care about *how far off* you are, not which direction.

Sometimes the raw gap is misleading. Being off by $1$ cm when measuring a pencil is very different from being off by $1$ cm when measuring a football field. **Relative error** puts the gap in proportion:

$$\text{Relative Error} = \frac{|x - \hat{x}|}{|x|}$$

Multiply by $100$ and you get **percent error**, the version most common in science classes.

A key pattern: errors *accumulate*. If you round two numbers before adding them, the errors in each can add together — or, in lucky cases, partially cancel. This is why mathematicians track error carefully through every step of a calculation, not just at the end.

### lag (16.7s)

## Lag

You have probably noticed that some things respond *after* the cause — your room stays warm for a while after you turn off the heater, or your phone's GPS updates a second behind where you actually are. That delay is the everyday meaning of lag, and mathematics formalizes it with a surprisingly tidy idea.

**The structure of lag**

Suppose you record a value every hour and call it $y_t$, where $t$ is the time index. The **lag-$k$** version of that sequence is simply

$$y_{t-k}$$

meaning: at each moment $t$, look back $k$ steps and use *that* past value. Lag-1 shifts the whole sequence one step into the future; lag-2 shifts it two steps, and so on.

For example, if your sequence is

$$y_1 = 3,\quad y_2 = 7,\quad y_3 = 2,\quad y_4 = 9$$

then the **lag-1 sequence** (starting from $t = 2$) is

$$y_1 = 3,\quad y_2 = 7,\quad y_3 = 2$$

because at each new index you read the *previous* value.

**The simple rule**

Lagging by $k$ creates a new sequence whose value at position $t$ equals the original sequence at position $t - k$. Writing it out: if $z_t = y_{t-k}$, then $z$ is a $k$-step-delayed copy of $y$.

This shows up everywhere from economics (how do sales *this month* compare to sales *last month*?) to music (echo is audio lagged by a tiny delay).

### autoregressive_model (42.1s)

## Autoregressive Model

**Recap:** You've probably already felt the idea — if you know the last few values of a sequence, you can make a decent guess at what comes next. A weather forecaster who knows yesterday's temperature already has a head start.

**The structure:** An autoregressive model makes that intuition precise. The simplest version, called **AR(1)**, says the current value depends on exactly one past value:

$$x_t = c + \phi_1 x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$$

Here $c$ is a constant baseline and $\varepsilon_t$ is small random noise — the part no model can fully predict. The one new thing to focus on is $\phi_1$ (pronounced "phi-one"): a **weight** that controls how strongly the previous value pulls the current one.

If $\phi_1 = 0.8$, each value is pulled toward 80% of the one before it. If $\phi_1 = 0$, the previous value tells you nothing — the sequence is pure noise. If $\phi_1 = 1$, nothing decays; the sequence "remembers" everything indefinitely. The entire personality of the model lives inside this single number.

Once you understand AR(1), extending to more past values — AR(2), AR(3), and so on — is just adding more $\phi$ terms. But the core idea is always $\phi_1$: one weight, one look-back step.

**The pattern to remember:** $\phi_1$ is a *memory dial*. Close to 1 means the past lingers strongly; close to 0 means it fades fast.

### integrative_component (30.1s)

## Integrative Component

You've likely heard that an integral represents "the area under a curve." That image is a good foothold — but now let's look at the actual machinery behind it.

**From slices to a sum.** Suppose you want to find the area under $y = f(x)$ between $x = a$ and $x = b$. Divide that interval into $n$ thin vertical strips, each of width $\Delta x = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$. Each strip is roughly a rectangle with height $f(x_i)$ and area $f(x_i)\,\Delta x$. Stacking all those rectangles gives the **Riemann sum**:

$$S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$$

As $n \to \infty$, the strips become infinitely thin and the approximation becomes exact. That limiting process is exactly what the integral symbol encodes:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$$

The elongated $\int$ is literally a stylized $S$ for *sum* — integration is continuous accumulation.

**The power rule for antiderivatives.** For any power function $f(x) = x^n$ with $n \neq -1$, the antiderivative follows a clean pattern: the exponent rises by one and you divide by it:

$$\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$$

The constant $C$ appears because many functions can share the same derivative. Once you have an antiderivative $F(x)$, a definite integral evaluates as $F(b) - F(a)$.

### moving_average_model (38.2s)

## Moving Average Model

You already know that an average smooths out a noisy list of numbers. A **moving average** takes that idea and makes it dynamic: instead of averaging everything at once, you average only a small *window* of recent values and then slide that window forward one step at a time.

Suppose you record daily temperatures $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$. A **moving average of order $k$**, written $\text{MA}(k)$, produces a smoothed estimate at each time step $t$:

$$\hat{x}_t = \frac{x_{t} + x_{t-1} + \cdots + x_{t-k+1}}{k}$$

Notice that at each new step, you drop the oldest value from the window and bring in the newest one — that sliding motion is what makes it a *moving* average rather than a fixed one. The window size $k$ is the only knob you turn. A large $k$ gives a smoother curve but reacts slowly to sudden changes; a small $k$ tracks the data closely but keeps more noise. This is always the central trade-off, and there is no universally correct answer — choosing $k$ depends on whether you care more about spotting gradual trends or reacting quickly to sharp shifts.

**Key rule**: Moving averages always *lag* — they summarize what already happened, not what comes next. More smoothing means less noise but a slower response to change.

### arima (44.1s)

## Arima

**Recap:** You've probably noticed that some data has a rhythm — temperatures rise in summer, fall in winter; a store sells more on weekends. ARIMA is a mathematical model that captures patterns in time and uses them to predict what comes next.

**Exploring the structure:** ARIMA stands for **AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average**, and its three letters each name one ingredient. For now, focus on the middle one — the **I**, which stands for *Integrated*, but really means **differencing**.

Real-world time series often drift upward or downward over time. That trend makes prediction harder, because the model has to chase a moving target. Differencing removes the drift by replacing each value with the *change* from the step before:

$$y'_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$$

If daily sales are 40, 43, 41 … the differenced series becomes 3, −2, 4 … — fluctuations around zero instead of a drifting line. Once the series is level (statisticians call this *stationary*), the other parts of ARIMA can fit the remaining pattern cleanly.

An ARIMA$(p,\, d,\, q)$ model records how many rounds of differencing were applied as the middle parameter $d$. Usually $d = 0$ or $d = 1$ is enough.

**Simple rule:** Plot your data first. If it drifts steadily up or down, set $d = 1$; if it already hovers around a constant level, $d = 0$ is fine.
