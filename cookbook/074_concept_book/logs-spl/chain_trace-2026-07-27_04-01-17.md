### variable (17.0s)

## Variable

**Definition.** A *variable* is a symbolic placeholder that represents an unspecified element drawn from a given set, called the *domain* of the variable. Formally, if $S$ is a non-empty set, a variable $x$ over $S$ is a symbol together with the assignment $x \in S$. The variable carries no fixed value until it is *bound*—either by substitution, quantification, or the solution of an equation. Until bound, $x$ denotes an arbitrary member of $S$ simultaneously, which is precisely what makes algebraic reasoning general rather than particular.

Variables are distinguished by role. A *free variable* appears in an expression without a governing quantifier or assignment; a *bound variable* is governed by a quantifier ($\forall$, $\exists$) or an operator such as integration ($\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$, where $x$ is bound). The distinction matters: replacing a free variable by a value changes the expression, whereas renaming a bound variable ($\alpha$-renaming) leaves the mathematical meaning unchanged.

**Worked Example.** Consider the polynomial $p(x) = x^2 - 5x + 6$. Here $x$ is a free variable ranging over $\mathbb{R}$. Setting $p(x) = 0$ binds $x$ to the solution set. Factoring gives $(x-2)(x-3) = 0$, so $x \in \{2, 3\}$. For each bound value, $p(2) = 4 - 10 + 6 = 0$ and $p(3) = 9 - 15 + 6 = 0$, confirming correctness. The variable $x$ has been replaced by concrete numbers, and the general statement collapses to two particular arithmetic facts.

**Key Theorem (Substitution Principle).** Let $E(x)$ be an algebraic expression with free variable $x$ over domain $S$, and let $a \in S$. Then the substitution $x \mapsto a$ yields a well-defined value $E(a) \in S'$ (for some codomain $S'$), and every valid identity $E(x) = F(x)$ holding for all $x \in S$ remains valid at $x = a$. This principle underpins equation solving, function evaluation, and formal proof by specialization.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, factor, solve

x = symbols('x')
p = x**2 - 5*x + 6

print("Factored form:", factor(p))
print("Roots:", solve(p, x))          # bind x to solution values
print("Evaluate at x=2:", p.subs(x, 2))
```

Running this cell prints `(x - 2)*(x - 3)`, roots `[2, 3]`, and evaluation `0`, mirroring the worked example and confirming that `subs` implements the Substitution Principle computationally.

### explanatory_response_variable (23.2s)

## Explanatory Response Variable

**Definition.** In a statistical model, variables are classified by the role they play in explaining variation. A *response variable* (also called the *dependent variable* or *outcome*), denoted $Y$, is the quantity whose behavior we seek to understand or predict. An *explanatory variable* (also called the *independent variable*, *predictor*, or *covariate*), denoted $X$, is the quantity hypothesized to account for, or *explain*, variation in $Y$. Formally, a simple linear model posits

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),$$

where $\beta_0$ is the intercept, $\beta_1$ is the slope (the rate at which $Y$ changes per unit increase in $X$), and $\varepsilon$ is irreducible random error. The distinction is not inherent to the variables themselves but is imposed by the scientific question: the same measurement may serve as $X$ in one study and $Y$ in another.

**Worked Example.** A researcher records daily average temperature $X$ (°C) and ice-cream sales $Y$ (units) at a campus kiosk over $n = 5$ days:

| Day | $X$ | $Y$ |
|-----|-----|-----|
| 1   | 18  | 40  |
| 2   | 22  | 55  |
| 3   | 25  | 60  |
| 4   | 29  | 75  |
| 5   | 32  | 80  |

Here temperature is the explanatory variable and sales the response variable. The least-squares estimates are

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum(X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2} \approx 2.80, \quad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{Y} - \hat{\beta}_1\bar{X} \approx 8.6.$$

The fitted model $\hat{Y} = 8.6 + 2.80X$ predicts that each additional degree of temperature is associated with approximately $2.8$ additional units sold.

**Key Theorem (Gauss–Markov).** Under the assumptions that $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I$, and that $X$ is fixed (non-random), the ordinary least-squares estimators $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ are the *Best Linear Unbiased Estimators* (BLUE): among all linear unbiased estimators, they achieve minimum variance. This guarantees that the roles of explanatory and response variable are respected in the optimality claim — reversing them yields a different, generally inferior, estimator.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

X_data = [18, 22, 25, 29, 32]
Y_data = [40, 55, 60, 75, 80]

n = len(X_data)
X_bar = sum(X_data) / n
Y_bar = sum(Y_data) / n

beta1 = sum((x - X_bar)*(y - Y_bar) for x, y in zip(X_data, Y_data)) \
      / sum((x - X_bar)**2 for x in X_data)
beta0 = Y_bar - beta1 * X_bar

x = sp.Symbol('x')
model = sp.Rational(beta0).limit_denominator(100) \
      + sp.Rational(beta1).limit_denominator(100) * x
sp.pprint(model)
```

Running this cell prints the symbolic fitted line and confirms the computed coefficients, allowing the student to substitute any temperature value and read off the predicted response directly.

### population (32.5s)

## Population

**Definition.** In statistics and probability theory, a *population* is the complete collection of all individuals, objects, or outcomes of interest in a given study. A population is characterized by a *parameter*—a fixed numerical quantity describing a feature of the entire collection. The most fundamental parameter is the **population mean** $\mu$, defined as

$$\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i,$$

where $N$ is the total number of elements and $x_i$ denotes the $i$-th observation. When $N$ is finite the population is called *finite*; when outcomes are generated by an ongoing process (e.g., all future coin flips), the population is *infinite* and described by a probability distribution. Spread and shape of the population—captured by variance and higher moments—build on this mean and are developed in subsequent sections.

**Worked Example.** Suppose the population consists of the exam scores of every student in a cohort of $N = 5$: $\{72, 85, 90, 68, 95\}$. The population mean is

$$\mu = \frac{72 + 85 + 90 + 68 + 95}{5} = \frac{410}{5} = 82.$$

This single value summarizes the center of the entire population. Every other student's score can be compared against $\mu = 82$ to judge whether it lies above or below the group center.

**Key Theorem (Law of Large Numbers).** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be independent and identically distributed random variables drawn from a population with mean $\mu < \infty$. Define the sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$. Then for every $\varepsilon > 0$,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P\!\left(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon\right) = 0.$$

This guarantees that as the sample size grows, the sample mean converges in probability to the true population mean—providing the theoretical foundation for statistical inference.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import Rational

scores = [72, 85, 90, 68, 95]
N = len(scores)
mu = Rational(sum(scores), N)
print(f"mu = {mu}")
```

Running this cell produces `mu = 82`, confirming the exact symbolic value before any floating-point rounding is introduced.

### treatments_experimental_unit (18.6s)

## Treatments Experimental Unit

**Definition.** In a designed experiment, an *experimental unit* is the smallest entity to which a treatment is independently applied. A *treatment* is a specific condition — a combination of factor levels — imposed on an experimental unit in order to observe its effect on a response variable. If an experiment involves $k$ factors with levels $\ell_1, \ell_2, \ldots, \ell_k$, then the full factorial set of treatments contains $\prod_{i=1}^{k} \ell_i$ distinct treatment combinations. Formally, let $\mathcal{T} = \{t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_m\}$ denote the treatment set, and let $\Omega$ denote the collection of experimental units. An assignment function $\phi: \Omega \to \mathcal{T}$ maps each unit to exactly one treatment; if $\phi$ is random, the design is a *randomized experiment*.

**Worked Example.** A plant biologist tests two fertilizer types ($F_1$, $F_2$) and three watering frequencies (daily, weekly, biweekly) on seedling growth. The factors yield $2 \times 3 = 6$ treatments. Each treatment is applied to four individual pots (the experimental units), giving $n = 24$ observations in total. The response variable is shoot height (cm) after 30 days. Crucially, each pot receives its fertilizer and watering schedule independently; measurements taken on leaves within a single pot are *sub-samples*, not separate experimental units, because the treatment was not applied to them individually.

**Key Theorem (Unbiasedness under Randomization).** Let $Y_{ij}$ denote the observed response on unit $j$ assigned to treatment $i$, and let $\tau_i$ be the true treatment effect. Under complete random assignment, the treatment estimator $\hat{\tau}_i - \hat{\tau}_{i'}$ is an unbiased estimator of $\tau_i - \tau_{i'}$:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bar{Y}_{i\cdot} - \bar{Y}_{i'\cdot}\right] = \tau_i - \tau_{i'}.$$

The proof follows because randomization ensures $\mathbb{E}[Y_{ij}] = \mu + \tau_i$ for every unit $j$ in treatment group $i$, with no systematic confounding from unit characteristics.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational, simplify

tau1, tau2, mu = symbols('tau1 tau2 mu')
# Expected values for two groups of size n
n = 4
Y_bar1 = mu + tau1          # E[Y-bar for group 1]
Y_bar2 = mu + tau2          # E[Y-bar for group 2]
contrast = simplify(Y_bar1 - Y_bar2)
print("Estimated contrast E[Y1 - Y2] =", contrast)
# Output: tau1 - tau2  (unbiased for true difference)
```

This cell confirms algebraically that the group mean difference recovers the true treatment contrast, provided assignment is independent across experimental units.

### control_group_placebo (18.0s)

## Control Group Placebo

**Definition.** In a controlled experiment, the *control group* is the set of participants who receive no active treatment; their outcomes serve as a baseline against which the treatment group is compared. A *placebo* is an inert intervention — physically indistinguishable from the active treatment — administered to the control group specifically to neutralize the psychological effect of *receiving* a treatment. The combined construct, the *placebo-controlled experiment*, ensures that any measured difference in outcomes is attributable to the biochemical or mechanical action of the treatment, not to participant expectation. Formally, let $Y_i(1)$ denote the potential outcome of subject $i$ under treatment and $Y_i(0)$ under control. The *average treatment effect* (ATE) is

$$\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)].$$

Without a placebo, $Y_i(0)$ is contaminated by expectation bias, so the estimator $\hat{\tau}$ is inconsistent.

**Worked Example.** A clinical trial tests an analgesic tablet. Researchers randomly assign 200 patients to two groups of 100. The treatment group receives the active tablet; the control group receives an identical-looking sugar tablet (the placebo). After two hours, both groups report pain on a 0–10 scale. Suppose the treatment group reports a mean pain score $\bar{Y}^{(1)} = 3.2$ and the control group $\bar{Y}^{(0)} = 5.8$. The estimated ATE is

$$\hat{\tau} = \bar{Y}^{(1)} - \bar{Y}^{(0)} = 3.2 - 5.8 = -2.6,$$

indicating a reduction of 2.6 scale units attributable to the drug's pharmacological action. Without the placebo, the control group might have reported $\bar{Y}^{(0)} = 6.9$ due to pessimism about receiving no care, inflating $|\hat{\tau}|$ by one full unit.

**Key Theorem (Unbiasedness under Randomization).** If treatment assignment is independent of potential outcomes — that is, $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp T_i$ — then

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\tau}] = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i(0)] = \tau.$$

The placebo preserves this independence by eliminating differential expectation; without it, $Y_i(0)$ depends on the knowledge of assignment, violating the independence assumption.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

n_T, n_C = 100, 100
sum_T = sp.Rational(320, 100)   # mean 3.2
sum_C = sp.Rational(580, 100)   # mean 5.8

tau_hat = sum_T - sum_C
print("Estimated ATE:", tau_hat)          # -13/5
print("Decimal:", float(tau_hat))         # -2.6

# Verify unbiasedness symbolically
Y0, Y1 = sp.symbols('Y_0 Y_1')
ATE = Y1 - Y0
print("ATE expression:", ATE)
```

Running this cell confirms $\hat{\tau} = -\tfrac{13}{5} = -2.6$ and displays the symbolic form of the estimand, reinforcing the distinction between the estimand $\tau$ and its sample estimator $\hat{\tau}$.

### blinding (20.7s)

## Blinding

**Definition.** In the design of experiments and clinical trials, *blinding* (also called *masking*) is a procedure that conceals treatment assignment information from one or more parties involved in the study, in order to prevent conscious or unconscious bias from influencing outcomes. Formally, let $\Omega$ be a sample space of experimental units, and let $T: \Omega \to \{0, 1\}$ be a binary treatment indicator ($T=1$ for treatment, $T=0$ for control). A study is said to be *single-blind* if the subjects are unaware of their value of $T$; *double-blind* if both subjects and outcome assessors are unaware; and *triple-blind* if additionally the data analysts are unaware until after primary analyses are locked.

The statistical rationale is grounded in potential outcomes. Let $Y_i(1)$ and $Y_i(0)$ denote the potential outcomes for unit $i$ under treatment and control, respectively. The average treatment effect is

$$\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)].$$

If knowledge of treatment assignment influences how $Y_i$ is measured or reported — an effect captured by a bias term $\delta_i$ — then the observed estimator $\hat{\tau}$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\tau}] = \tau + \bar{\delta}$, where $\bar{\delta} \neq 0$ in an unblinded study. Blinding drives $\bar{\delta} \to 0$ by severing the informational channel through which assignment affects measurement.

**Worked Example.** A pharmaceutical trial tests a new analgesic against a placebo. Without blinding, patients who know they received the drug may report lower pain scores due to expectation alone (the placebo effect), while physicians may probe outcomes more optimistically. By administering identical-looking capsules and withholding allocation codes until trial completion, both $\delta_{\text{subject}}$ and $\delta_{\text{assessor}}$ are eliminated, yielding an unbiased estimate of pharmacological efficacy.

**Key Theorem (Unconfounded Estimation under Blinding).** If treatment assignment is independent of potential outcomes conditional on observed covariates $X_i$ — the *ignorability* condition $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp T_i \mid X_i$ — and blinding ensures measurement is independent of $T_i$ given true outcomes, then the ordinary least-squares estimator of $\tau$ is consistent.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 200
treatment = rng.integers(0, 2, n)          # 0 = control, 1 = treatment
bias = 1.5 * treatment                      # simulated unblinded reporter bias
true_effect = 3.0
Y = true_effect * treatment + rng.normal(0, 2, n) + bias

# Naive (unblinded) estimate
t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(Y[treatment==1], Y[treatment==0])
print(f"Biased estimate of effect: {Y[treatment==1].mean() - Y[treatment==0].mean():.3f}")

# Blinded: bias removed
Y_blind = true_effect * treatment + rng.normal(0, 2, n)
print(f"Unbiased estimate of effect: {Y_blind[treatment==1].mean() - Y_blind[treatment==0].mean():.3f}")
print(f"True effect: {true_effect}")
```

Running this cell demonstrates quantitatively how unblinded measurement inflates the estimated treatment effect beyond the true value $\tau = 3.0$.

### lurking_variable (19.6s)

## Lurking Variable

**Definition.** A *lurking variable* (also called a *confounding variable*) is a variable $Z$ that is not included in a statistical analysis but is causally or correlationally associated with both the explanatory variable $X$ and the response variable $Y$. Its presence can induce a spurious association between $X$ and $Y$, or mask a genuine one, leading the analyst to draw incorrect causal conclusions from observed data.

Formally, suppose we observe a correlation $\hat{\rho}_{XY} \neq 0$. A variable $Z$ is a lurking variable if

$$
Z \not\in \{\text{model predictors}\}, \quad \rho_{XZ} \neq 0, \quad \rho_{YZ} \neq 0.
$$

Even when the true causal effect of $X$ on $Y$ is zero, a shared dependence on $Z$ can produce $\hat{\rho}_{XY} \neq 0$ in finite samples.

**Worked Example.** A city government records, across 30 neighbourhoods, the number of ice-cream vendors $X$ and the monthly drowning count $Y$. A simple linear regression yields a statistically significant positive slope. The lurking variable is ambient temperature $Z$: hot weather drives both ice-cream consumption and swimming frequency. Once $Z$ is held fixed — by including it as a covariate or by stratifying the data by season — the partial correlation $\rho_{XY \mid Z}$ drops to approximately zero.

**Key Theorem (Partial Correlation Decomposition).** Let $X$, $Y$, $Z$ be jointly normally distributed. The partial correlation of $X$ and $Y$ given $Z$ satisfies

$$
\rho_{XY \mid Z} = \frac{\rho_{XY} - \rho_{XZ}\,\rho_{YZ}}{\sqrt{\left(1 - \rho_{XZ}^{2}\right)\left(1 - \rho_{YZ}^{2}\right)}}.
$$

This identity shows that the marginal correlation $\rho_{XY}$ receives a contribution $\rho_{XZ}\,\rho_{YZ}$ from the lurking path through $Z$. If $\rho_{XZ}$ and $\rho_{YZ}$ share the same sign, the lurking variable *inflates* the observed correlation; if they differ in sign, it *suppresses* it.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, sqrt, simplify, Rational

rho_XY, rho_XZ, rho_YZ = symbols('rho_XY rho_XZ rho_YZ')

partial_corr = (rho_XY - rho_XZ * rho_YZ) / (
    sqrt(1 - rho_XZ**2) * sqrt(1 - rho_YZ**2)
)

# Numerical check: ice-cream / drowning scenario
vals = {rho_XY: Rational(7, 10), rho_XZ: Rational(8, 10), rho_YZ: Rational(9, 10)}
result = partial_corr.subs(vals).evalf(4)
print("Partial correlation after controlling for Z:", result)
# Expected: a value near 0, confirming Z explains the observed association
```

Running this cell demonstrates that a marginal correlation of $0.70$ can collapse to near zero once the lurking variable is accounted for, illustrating why correlation alone never establishes causation.

### random_assignment (19.1s)

## Random Assignment

**Definition.** Let $\Omega$ be a finite population of $N$ experimental units and let $\mathcal{T} = \{T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_k\}$ be a set of $k$ treatment conditions with prescribed group sizes $n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k$ satisfying $\sum_{i=1}^k n_i = N$. A *random assignment* (also called a *completely randomized design*) is a bijection $A: \Omega \to \mathcal{T}$ drawn uniformly at random from all $\frac{N!}{n_1!\, n_2! \cdots n_k!}$ possible such partitions. Equivalently, each unit receives treatment $T_i$ with probability $n_i / N$, and assignments are independent across units (conditional on the marginal totals being respected).

The fundamental purpose of random assignment is to break the dependence between unit characteristics—both observed and unobserved—and the treatment received, thereby enabling causal inference.

**Worked Example.** Suppose $N = 6$ students are to be divided equally into a treatment group ($n_1 = 3$) and a control group ($n_2 = 3$). Label the students $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. The number of equally likely assignments is $\binom{6}{3} = 20$. One valid assignment drawn at random might place $\{1, 4, 6\}$ in treatment and $\{2, 3, 5\}$ in control. Because every partition is equally probable, the expected pre-treatment covariate value in each group equals the population mean, and no systematic selection bias can arise.

**Key Theorem (Unconfoundedness under Random Assignment).** Let $Y_i(t)$ denote the potential outcome of unit $i$ under treatment $t$, and let $X_i$ represent any pre-treatment covariate. If treatment $T_i$ is randomly assigned, then

$$T_i \perp\!\!\!\perp \bigl(Y_i(0),\, Y_i(1),\, X_i\bigr),$$

so the observed difference in group means is an unbiased estimator of the average treatment effect $\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]$.

*Proof sketch.* Since every assignment partition is equally likely, the joint distribution of $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1), X_i)$ is identical across all treatment-assignment values by construction. Conditioning on $T_i = t$ therefore leaves the potential-outcome distribution unchanged, establishing independence.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy.stats import Binomial, E, variance

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
units = np.arange(1, 7)          # six students
assignment = rng.permutation(units)
treatment, control = assignment[:3], assignment[3:]
print("Treatment:", treatment, "| Control:", control)

# Verify: expected group mean of a covariate x = unit label
x = np.arange(1, 7)
simulated_diffs = [
    rng.permutation(x)[:3].mean() - rng.permutation(x)[3:].mean()
    for _ in range(10_000)
]
print(f"E[mean_T - mean_C] ≈ {np.mean(simulated_diffs):.4f}  (expect 0)")
```

### randomized_experiment (22.0s)

## Randomized Experiment

**Definition.** A *randomized experiment* (also called a *randomized controlled trial*) is a study in which the investigator assigns each experimental unit to a treatment condition via a random mechanism—typically drawing from a uniform distribution over treatment labels—independently of any characteristics of the unit. Formally, let $\mathcal{U} = \{u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n\}$ be a finite population of units, and let $\mathcal{T} = \{0, 1\}$ denote a binary treatment set. A *completely randomized design* assigns treatment $T_i \in \mathcal{T}$ to unit $u_i$ such that

$$\Pr(T_i = 1) = p \in (0,1), \quad T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_n \text{ mutually independent.}$$

The defining property is that treatment assignment $T_i$ is statistically independent of all pre-treatment covariates, both measured and unmeasured. This independence is the mechanism that justifies causal inference.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a university evaluates a tutoring programme for $n = 100$ students. Each student is assigned by a coin flip ($p = 0.5$) to either tutoring ($T_i = 1$) or control ($T_i = 0$). After the semester, exam scores $Y_i$ are recorded. The *sample average treatment effect* (SATE) is estimated by

$$\widehat{\tau} = \bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_0 = \frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{T_i=1} Y_i \;-\; \frac{1}{n_0}\sum_{T_i=0} Y_i,$$

where $n_1 + n_0 = 100$. Because assignment was random, differences in $\bar{Y}_1$ and $\bar{Y}_0$ cannot be attributed to pre-existing ability gaps; they reflect the causal effect of tutoring.

**Key Theorem** *(Unbiasedness of the Difference-in-Means Estimator).* Under a completely randomized design with $n_1$ units assigned to treatment and $n_0$ to control,

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widehat{\tau}\right] = \tau_{\text{ATE}} := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\!\left(Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\right),$$

where $Y_i(t)$ denotes unit $i$'s *potential outcome* under treatment $t$. The proof follows from the exchangeability induced by random assignment: $\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}_1] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i Y_i(1)$ and similarly for control.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy import symbols, Rational, simplify

# Simulate a small randomized experiment
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 100
treatment = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, size=n)
Y0 = rng.normal(70, 10, size=n)          # potential outcome: control
Y1 = Y0 + 5                               # true effect = 5 points
Y_obs = np.where(treatment, Y1, Y0)

tau_hat = Y_obs[treatment == 1].mean() - Y_obs[treatment == 0].mean()
print(f"Estimated ATE: {tau_hat:.3f}  (true ATE = 5.000)")
```

Running this cell repeatedly demonstrates that $\widehat{\tau}$ fluctuates around the true value of $5$, confirming unbiasedness empirically and motivating the study of variance reduction through blocking and stratification.
