### sample_data (34.7s)

## Sample Data

**Definition.** A *sample* is a finite collection of observations drawn from a population. Formally, let $\Omega$ be a probability space and $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ a random variable. A *sample of size $n$* is an ordered tuple $(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ where each $x_i$ is an independent realisation of $X$. The corresponding *sample data* is the numerical record of these realisations, from which we estimate properties of the underlying population distribution $F_X$.

The distinction between a population parameter and its sample-based estimate is fundamental. We write $\mu$ for the true (unknown) population mean and $\bar{x}$ for the *sample mean*, defined as

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i.$$

The sample mean is the single most important summary of a dataset: it aggregates $n$ individual measurements into one number that acts as a stand-in for the unknown $\mu$. Subsequent sections will introduce additional estimators—sample variance, sample median—but each builds on the foundation established here.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a laboratory measures the boiling point of water (in °C) at high altitude five times, yielding the sample $\mathbf{x} = (99.1,\, 98.9,\, 99.3,\, 99.0,\, 99.2)$. Then

$$\bar{x} = \frac{99.1 + 98.9 + 99.3 + 99.0 + 99.2}{5} = \frac{495.5}{5} = 99.1 \,{}^{\circ}\text{C}.$$

The sample mean lies inside the observed range $(98.9, 99.3)$, as must always be the case, providing a useful sanity check for arithmetic.

**Key Theorem (Law of Large Numbers).** Let $(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ be independent, identically distributed realisations of $X$ with finite mean $\mu$. Then

$$\bar{x} \xrightarrow{\,p\,} \mu \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,$$

where $\xrightarrow{p}$ denotes convergence in probability. In practical terms, increasing the sample size forces the sample mean arbitrarily close to the true population mean, justifying the use of $\bar{x}$ as an estimator of $\mu$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

data = [sp.Rational(991,10), sp.Rational(989,10),
        sp.Rational(993,10), sp.Rational(990,10),
        sp.Rational(992,10)]

x_bar = sum(data) / n
print("Sample mean:", x_bar)   # 991/10
```

Running this cell confirms $\bar{x} = 99.1$ using exact rational arithmetic, eliminating floating-point rounding errors that could obscure the pedagogical result.

### population_data (15.5s)

## Population Data

**Definition.** In statistics, a *population* is the complete collection of all individuals, objects, or measurements under study. *Population data* refers to the full set of values of a variable $X$ measured across every member of this collection. If the population has $N$ members with measurements $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N$, the *population mean* is

$$\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i,$$

and the *population variance* is

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \mu)^2.$$

The standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$ gives dispersion in the original units of measurement. Crucially, these are *parameters* — fixed (if unknown) numerical characteristics of the population — not estimates derived from a subset.

**Worked Example.** A small rural school has exactly five students whose weekly study hours are $\{4, 6, 7, 9, 9\}$. This constitutes complete population data. Compute:

$$\mu = \frac{4+6+7+9+9}{5} = \frac{35}{5} = 7 \text{ hours.}$$

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{(4-7)^2+(6-7)^2+(7-7)^2+(9-7)^2+(9-7)^2}{5} = \frac{9+1+0+4+4}{5} = \frac{18}{5} = 3.6.$$

Hence $\sigma = \sqrt{3.6} \approx 1.897$ hours. Notice the denominator is $N = 5$, not $N-1$; the correction $N-1$ (Bessel's correction) applies only when estimating from a *sample*.

**Key Theorem (Chebyshev's Inequality).** For any population with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, and for any real $k > 1$,

$$P\!\left(|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma\right) \leq \frac{1}{k^2}.$$

This bound holds for *every* population distribution, requiring no normality assumption. For $k = 2$, at least $75\%$ of the population lies within two standard deviations of the mean — a universal guarantee.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import Rational, sqrt, var, simplify

data = [4, 6, 7, 9, 9]
N = len(data)

mu = Rational(sum(data), N)
sigma_sq = sum(Rational((x - mu)**2) for x in data) / N
sigma = sqrt(sigma_sq)

print("Population mean:    ", mu)          # 7
print("Population variance:", sigma_sq)    # 18/5
print("Population std dev: ", sigma)       # 3*sqrt(10)/5
```

Running this cell confirms $\mu = 7$, $\sigma^2 = \tfrac{18}{5}$, and $\sigma = \tfrac{3\sqrt{10}}{5}$, illustrating exact arithmetic over the rationals — a key advantage of symbolic computation.

### mean (0.0s)

## Mean

**Definition.** Let $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ be a finite sequence of real numbers. The *arithmetic mean* (or *sample mean*) of this sequence is the value

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i.$$

The mean is the unique real number that minimises the sum of squared deviations: $\bar{x} = \arg\min_{c \in \mathbb{R}} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - c)^2$. Geometrically, it is the balance point of the data — the centre of mass if each observation is assigned equal weight.

**Worked example.** A student records the following five quiz scores: $72, 85, 90, 68, 95$. The arithmetic mean is

$$\bar{x} = \frac{72 + 85 + 90 + 68 + 95}{5} = \frac{410}{5} = 82.$$

Now suppose one score is changed from $68$ to $168$ (a data-entry error). The new mean becomes $\frac{510}{5} = 102$, which exceeds all legitimate scores. This illustrates that the arithmetic mean is sensitive to outliers — a single extreme value can shift it substantially, motivating robust alternatives such as the median.

**Key theorem (Linearity of the sample mean).** For two sequences $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ and $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ and constants $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, let $z_i = a x_i + b y_i$. Then

$$\bar{z} = a\,\bar{x} + b\,\bar{y}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply the definition directly: $\bar{z} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (ax_i + by_i) = a \cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i + b \cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i$. $\square$

This linearity makes the mean uniquely tractable for combining scaled or shifted datasets without recomputing from raw observations.

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import Rational

scores = [72, 85, 90, 68, 95]
sample_mean = Rational(sum(scores), len(scores))
print("Sample mean:", sample_mean)          # 82

# Linearity check: scale each score by 1.1 and add a 5-point bonus
adjusted = [Rational(11, 10) * s + 5 for s in scores]
mean_adjusted = Rational(sum(adjusted), len(adjusted))
expected = Rational(11, 10) * sample_mean + 5
print("Mean of adjusted scores:", mean_adjusted)   # 95.2
print("Computed via linearity:  ", expected)        # 95.2
```

Running this cell confirms that scaling each score by $1.1$ and adding $5$ points raises the mean from $82$ to $\frac{476}{5} = 95.2$, in exact agreement with the linearity theorem applied to $\bar{x}$.

### median (17.3s)

## Median

**Definition.** Let $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ be a dataset of $n$ real numbers arranged in non-decreasing order, so that $x_{(1)} \leq x_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq x_{(n)}$ (called the *order statistics*). The **median** is the value that divides this sorted sequence into two equal halves:

$$
\tilde{x} =
\begin{cases}
x_{(m+1)} & \text{if } n = 2m+1 \text{ (odd)}, \\[4pt]
\dfrac{x_{(m)} + x_{(m+1)}}{2} & \text{if } n = 2m \text{ (even)}.
\end{cases}
$$

Intuitively, at least half the observations lie at or below $\tilde{x}$ and at least half lie at or above it.

**Worked Example.** Consider the seven exam scores $\{72, 85, 91, 68, 74, 88, 95\}$. Sorting gives the order statistics

$$
68,\; 72,\; 74,\; 75,\; 85,\; 88,\; 91,\; 95.
$$

Wait—there are seven values, so $n = 7 = 2(3)+1$, giving $m = 3$. The median is the fourth order statistic:

$$
\tilde{x} = x_{(4)} = 74.
$$

The mean of the same dataset is $\bar{x} = (68+72+74+85+88+91+95)/7 \approx 81.9$. The gap illustrates how the median is insensitive to the cluster of high scores near 90, whereas the mean is pulled upward.

**Key Theorem (Median minimises the $L^1$ loss).** For any dataset $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$, the value $c^*$ that minimises the sum of absolute deviations

$$
c^* = \underset{c \in \mathbb{R}}{\arg\min} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - c|
$$

is the median $\tilde{x}$. *Proof sketch.* Write $f(c) = \sum_i |x_i - c|$. This piecewise-linear, convex function has derivative $f'(c) = \#\{i : x_i < c\} - \#\{i : x_i > c\}$, which changes sign exactly at the median. Convexity guarantees that any minimiser of $f'(c) = 0$ is a global minimum. $\square$

This theorem explains why the median is the preferred measure of centre when outliers are present: it minimises the total $L^1$ error rather than the squared $L^2$ error minimised by the mean.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy.stats import median
from statistics import median as py_median

scores = [68, 72, 74, 85, 88, 91, 95]
print(py_median(scores))          # 74

# Verify the L1 minimisation property
from sympy import symbols, Abs, solve, diff, Add
c = symbols('c', real=True)
L1 = Add(*[Abs(x - c) for x in scores])
# Evaluate over a grid to confirm minimum at c = 74
vals = {v: L1.subs(c, v) for v in range(68, 96)}
print(min(vals, key=vals.get))    # 74
```

### percentiles (16.8s)

## Percentiles

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a real-valued random variable with cumulative distribution function $F(x) = P(X \leq x)$. For $p \in (0, 1)$, the **$p$-th quantile** (or $100p$-th **percentile**) of $X$ is the value

$$Q(p) = \inf\{x \in \mathbb{R} : F(x) \geq p\}.$$

For a finite data set $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$ arranged in non-decreasing order $x_{(1)} \leq x_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq x_{(n)}$, the $100p$-th percentile is commonly estimated by the order statistic $x_{(\lceil np \rceil)}$, where $\lceil \cdot \rceil$ denotes the ceiling function. Percentiles partition the distribution into hundredths: the 50th percentile is the **median**, while the 25th and 75th percentiles are the first and third **quartiles**, $Q_1$ and $Q_3$, respectively. Their difference $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$ is the **interquartile range**, a robust measure of spread.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a class of ten students earns the scores

$$55,\; 62,\; 67,\; 70,\; 74,\; 78,\; 81,\; 85,\; 91,\; 96.$$

Find the 80th percentile. Here $n = 10$ and $p = 0.80$, so the index is $\lceil 10 \times 0.80 \rceil = \lceil 8 \rceil = 8$. The 80th percentile is therefore $x_{(8)} = 85$, meaning approximately 80% of students scored at or below 85.

**Theorem (Monotonicity and Equivariance).** Let $Q_X(p)$ denote the $p$-th quantile of $X$. For any monotone increasing function $g$,

$$Q_{g(X)}(p) = g\!\left(Q_X(p)\right).$$

*Proof sketch.* Since $g$ is increasing, $g(X) \leq g(x) \iff X \leq x$, so $F_{g(X)}(g(x)) = F_X(x)$. Taking the infimum over $\{t : F_{g(X)}(t) \geq p\}$ yields the result. $\square$

This equivariance property is practically significant: percentiles of log-transformed income data, for instance, map back to income percentiles by exponentiation without recomputation.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy import stats, Symbol, N

# Empirical percentiles
scores = [55, 62, 67, 70, 74, 78, 81, 85, 91, 96]
p80 = np.percentile(scores, 80, method='higher')
iqr = np.percentile(scores, 75) - np.percentile(scores, 25)
print(f"80th percentile: {p80}")   # 85
print(f"IQR: {iqr}")               # 19.25
```

Running this cell confirms $Q(0.80) = 85$ and reveals that the middle 50% of scores spans roughly 19 points, indicating moderate dispersion around the median.

### probability (0.0s)

## Probability

**Definition.** A *probability space* is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, where $\Omega$ is the *sample space* (the set of all possible outcomes), $\mathcal{F}$ is a collection of subsets of $\Omega$ called *events*, and $P : \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ is a *probability measure* satisfying the Kolmogorov axioms:

1. $P(A) \geq 0$ for every event $A \in \mathcal{F}$,
2. $P(\Omega) = 1$,
3. For any countable collection of mutually disjoint events $A_1, A_2, \ldots$,
$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i).$$

These three axioms are sufficient to derive the entire calculus of probability, including complementation ($P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$), monotonicity, and inclusion–exclusion. The one new primitive introduced here is the probability measure $P$ itself — a function that assigns a number in $[0,1]$ to each event in a principled, consistent way.

**Worked Example.** A fair six-sided die is rolled once. Here $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, and $P(\{k\}) = \tfrac{1}{6}$ for each outcome $k$. Let $A = \{\text{even}\} = \{2, 4, 6\}$ and $B = \{\text{greater than 4}\} = \{5, 6\}$. By axiom 3,
$$P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad P(B) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}.$$
Since $A \cap B = \{6\}$, the inclusion–exclusion formula gives
$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{3}.$$

**Theorem (Inclusion–Exclusion).** For any two events $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$,
$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).$$
*Proof sketch.* Write $A \cup B = A \sqcup (B \setminus A)$ as a disjoint union. Applying axiom 3 and noting $B = (A \cap B) \sqcup (B \setminus A)$ yields the result after cancellation. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy.stats import Die, P

X = Die('X', 6)                          # fair six-sided die
p_even    = P(X % 2 == 0)               # P(A)
p_gt4     = P(X > 4)                     # P(B)
p_union   = P((X % 2 == 0) | (X > 4))  # P(A ∪ B)
print(p_even, p_gt4, p_union)            # 1/2  1/3  2/3
```

### random_variable (0.0s)

## Random Variable

**Definition.** A *random variable* is a measurable function $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, where $\Omega$ is the sample space, $\mathcal{F}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of events, and $P$ is a probability measure. For every Borel set $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, we require $X^{-1}(B) = \{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \in B\} \in \mathcal{F}$, ensuring that the probability $P(X \in B)$ is well-defined. Informally, a random variable assigns a real number to each outcome of an experiment, translating abstract events into numerical quantities amenable to analysis.

Random variables are classified by their range. A *discrete* random variable takes values in a countable set $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots\}$ and is characterized by its *probability mass function* (PMF) $p(x_k) = P(X = x_k)$, satisfying $\sum_k p(x_k) = 1$. A *continuous* random variable takes values in an interval and is described by a *probability density function* (PDF) $f$, where $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$.

**Worked Example.** Roll a fair six-sided die. Set $\Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ with $P(\{\omega\}) = 1/6$ for each outcome. Define $X(\omega) = \omega$. Then $X$ is a discrete random variable with PMF $p(k) = 1/6$ for $k \in \{1,\ldots,6\}$. Its *expected value* is
$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{k=1}^{6} k \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5,$$
and its *variance* is $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = \frac{91}{6} - \frac{441}{36} = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.92$.

**Key Theorem (Law of the Unconscious Statistician).** If $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is measurable and $X$ is a discrete random variable, then
$$\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_k g(x_k)\, p(x_k),$$
without needing to derive the distribution of $g(X)$ explicitly. The analogous statement for continuous $X$ replaces the sum with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) f(x)\,dx$.

**Lab Cell.**
```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational, summation, simplify

k = symbols('k', integer=True)
pmf = Rational(1, 6)

E_X  = summation(k * pmf, (k, 1, 6))
E_X2 = summation(k**2 * pmf, (k, 1, 6))
Var_X = simplify(E_X2 - E_X**2)

print(f"E[X]   = {E_X}")      # 7/2
print(f"Var(X) = {Var_X}")    # 35/12
```

### variance (0.0s)

## Variance

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a random variable with finite mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$. The *variance* of $X$ is defined as

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X - \mu)^2\right].$$

Variance measures the average squared deviation of $X$ from its mean. Because the deviation is squared before averaging, large departures from $\mu$ are penalized disproportionately. The *standard deviation* $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$ restores the original units. For a discrete variable taking values $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ with probabilities $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n$, the formula becomes

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\,(x_i - \mu)^2.$$

**Worked Example.** Suppose a fair six-sided die is rolled once. The sample space is $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, each outcome occurring with probability $\tfrac{1}{6}$. The mean is

$$\mu = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5.$$

The variance is then

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{k=1}^{6}(k - 3.5)^2 = \frac{1}{6}\bigl(6.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 + 6.25\bigr) = \frac{17.5}{6} \approx 2.917.$$

The standard deviation is $\sigma \approx 1.708$, meaning a typical roll deviates from the mean by roughly 1.7 units.

**Key Theorem (Computational Formula).** For any random variable $X$ with finite second moment,

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \left(\mathbb{E}[X]\right)^2.$$

*Proof sketch.* Expand $(X - \mu)^2 = X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2$ and apply linearity of expectation:

$$\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \mu^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2. \qquad \square$$

This identity is computationally convenient because $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]$ can be computed in a single pass over the data.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import Rational, sqrt, symbols, summation, simplify

k = symbols('k', integer=True)
outcomes = [Rational(i) for i in range(1, 7)]
mu = sum(outcomes) / 6
var = sum((x - mu)**2 for x in outcomes) / 6
print("Mean:", mu)           # 7/2
print("Variance:", var)      # 35/12
print("Std dev:", sqrt(var)) # sqrt(105)/6
```

### probability_distribution (33.4s)

## Probability Distribution

**Definition.** Let $S$ be a sample space associated with a random experiment. A *probability distribution* is a function $P$ that assigns to every event $A \subseteq S$ a real number satisfying the Kolmogorov axioms: (i) $P(A) \geq 0$, (ii) $P(S) = 1$, and (iii) for any countable collection of mutually exclusive events $A_1, A_2, \ldots$,

$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i).$$

When $S$ is finite or countably infinite, the distribution is described completely by a *probability mass function* (PMF) $p(x) = P(X = x)$, where $X$ is a discrete random variable taking values in $S$. The PMF must satisfy $p(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$ and $\sum_{x \in S} p(x) = 1$; together these conditions are exactly equivalent to the Kolmogorov axioms in the discrete setting.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a fair six-sided die is rolled once. The sample space is $S = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ and the PMF is $p(x) = \tfrac{1}{6}$ for each $x \in S$. Normalization holds because $\sum_{x=1}^{6} \tfrac{1}{6} = 1$. The probability of rolling an even number is

$$P(X \in \{2,4,6\}) = p(2) + p(4) + p(6) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

Note that the assignment $p(x) = \tfrac{1}{6}$ is not forced by the sample space alone; it encodes the physical assumption of fairness. A loaded die would yield a different PMF over the same $S$.

**Key Theorem (Law of Total Probability).** If $\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\}$ is a partition of $S$, then for any event $A$,

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i).$$

This theorem allows marginal probabilities to be computed by conditioning on a convenient auxiliary partition, and it forms the backbone of Bayesian inference.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import Rational

# PMF for a fair six-sided die
pmf = {x: Rational(1, 6) for x in range(1, 7)}

# Verify normalization
print(sum(pmf.values()))                          # → 1

# P(X is even)
print(sum(pmf[x] for x in pmf if x % 2 == 0))   # → 1/2
```

### quartiles (15.6s)

## Quartiles

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a dataset of $n$ real observations arranged in non-decreasing order: $x_{(1)} \le x_{(2)} \le \cdots \le x_{(n)}$. The **quartiles** partition this ordered dataset into four groups of approximately equal size. Specifically, the **first quartile** $Q_1$, **second quartile** $Q_2$ (the median), and **third quartile** $Q_3$ are the values at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively. A common interpolation rule defines the $p$-th percentile position as $L = p(n+1)/100$; if $L$ is not an integer, one linearly interpolates between the observations at positions $\lfloor L \rfloor$ and $\lceil L \rceil$.

The **interquartile range** (IQR) is defined as

$$\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1.$$

It measures the spread of the central 50 % of the data and is robust to extreme values, unlike the full range $x_{(n)} - x_{(1)}$.

**Worked example.** Consider the dataset $\{3, 7, 8, 12, 14, 17, 21, 25\}$, so $n = 8$.

- $Q_2$: the median lies between the 4th and 5th values, giving $Q_2 = (12 + 14)/2 = 13$.
- $Q_1$: apply the rule to the lower half $\{3, 7, 8, 12\}$; the median of this half is $(7+8)/2 = 7.5$, so $Q_1 = 7.5$.
- $Q_3$: the upper half is $\{14, 17, 21, 25\}$; its median is $(17+21)/2 = 19$, so $Q_3 = 19$.
- $\mathrm{IQR} = 19 - 7.5 = 11.5$.

**Theorem (Tukey fence criterion).** An observation $x$ is a *mild outlier* if it lies outside the interval $[Q_1 - 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR},\; Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}]$, and an *extreme outlier* if it lies outside $[Q_1 - 3\,\mathrm{IQR},\; Q_3 + 3\,\mathrm{IQR}]$. These fences form the basis of the standard box-and-whisker plot and follow directly from the empirical observation that, for normally distributed data, fewer than 0.7 % of values fall beyond the mild fence.

**Lab cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy.stats import Normal, cdf
data = [3, 7, 8, 12, 14, 17, 21, 25]
q1, q2, q3 = np.percentile(data, [25, 50, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
lower_fence = q1 - 1.5 * iqr
upper_fence = q3 + 1.5 * iqr
print(f"Q1={q1}, Q2={q2}, Q3={q3}, IQR={iqr}")
print(f"Tukey fences: [{lower_fence}, {upper_fence}]")
outliers = [x for x in data if x < lower_fence or x > upper_fence]
print(f"Outliers: {outliers}")
```

### standard_deviation (0.0s)

## Standard Deviation

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a random variable with expected value $\mu = E[X]$. The *standard deviation* of $X$, denoted $\sigma$ (or $\sigma_X$), is defined as

$$\sigma = \sqrt{E\!\left[(X - \mu)^2\right]}.$$

The quantity inside the square root, $\sigma^2 = E[(X-\mu)^2]$, is called the *variance*. For a finite dataset $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$, the population standard deviation is

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2}, \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i.$$

When the dataset is treated as a *sample* drawn from a larger population, the denominator is replaced by $n-1$ (Bessel's correction), yielding the sample standard deviation $s$.

**Worked Example.** Consider the dataset $\{2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9\}$, which has $n = 8$ values.

1. Compute the mean: $\mu = \dfrac{2+4+4+4+5+5+7+9}{8} = \dfrac{40}{8} = 5$.

2. Compute squared deviations:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{8}(x_i - 5)^2 = 9 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 4 + 16 = 32.$$

3. Population standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{32/8} = \sqrt{4} = 2$.

Intuitively, the data points lie, on average, two units away from the mean of 5.

**Key Theorem (Chebyshev's Inequality).** For *any* distribution with finite mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and for every $k > 1$,

$$P(|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma) \leq \frac{1}{k^2}.$$

This guarantees, for example, that at least $1 - 1/4 = 75\%$ of any distribution's probability mass lies within two standard deviations of the mean — no assumptions on the shape of the distribution are required. For the normal distribution the corresponding figure is approximately 95.4%, a much tighter bound arising from its specific analytic form.

## Python Data Analysis

**Definition.** *Data analysis* is the systematic process of inspecting, cleaning, transforming, and modelling a dataset to extract actionable information and support decision-making. Formally, given a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ are feature vectors and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ are response values, the goal is to estimate a function $f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $y_i \approx f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$, where $\varepsilon_i$ is random noise with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i] = 0$. Python's scientific stack—principally `NumPy`, `pandas`, and `matplotlib`—provides the computational substrate for every stage of this pipeline.

**Worked Example.** Consider the body-mass index dataset containing heights $h$ (metres) and weights $w$ (kilograms) for $n = 100$ individuals. The sample mean and variance of weight are:

$$\bar{w} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i, \qquad s^2_w = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_i - \bar{w})^2.$$

The Pearson correlation between height and weight is:

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h_i - \bar{h})(w_i - \bar{w})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h_i-\bar{h})^2\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_i-\bar{w})^2}}.$$

If $r = 0.87$, this indicates a strong positive linear relationship: taller individuals tend to weigh more. Using `pandas`, the entire computation reduces to `df['height'].corr(df['weight'])`.

**Key Theorem (Law of Large Numbers).** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent and identically distributed random variables with finite mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_i]$. Then the sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ satisfies:

$$\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\,p\,} \mu \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

This theorem is the theoretical justification for using sample statistics as estimates of population parameters: as the dataset grows, descriptive summaries converge to their true values.
