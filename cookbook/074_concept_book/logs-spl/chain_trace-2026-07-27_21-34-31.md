## Intermediate Spanning Forest

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E, w)$ be a weighted undirected graph with $n = |V|$ vertices, $m = |E|$ edges, and edge-weight function $w : E \to \mathbb{R}$. A *forest* on $G$ is an acyclic subgraph $F = (V, E_F)$ with $E_F \subseteq E$. A *spanning forest* is a forest that is simultaneously maximal: no edge of $E \setminus E_F$ can be added without creating a cycle. Given a greedy edge-selection procedure (such as Kruskal's algorithm), the *intermediate spanning forest at step $k$*, denoted $\mathcal{F}_k$, is the forest $(V, E_k)$ where $E_k$ contains exactly the $k$ edges accepted so far — edges added in order of non-decreasing weight that introduce no cycle. Formally,

$$\mathcal{F}_k = \bigl(V,\; \{e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_k\}\bigr), \quad 0 \le k \le n - c(G),$$

where $c(G)$ denotes the number of connected components of $G$ and $n - c(G)$ is the maximum number of edges in any spanning forest of $G$.

**Worked Example.** Consider $G$ with vertices $\{A, B, C, D\}$ and weighted edges $\{(A,B,1),\,(B,C,3),\,(A,C,4),\,(C,D,2)\}$. Sort edges by weight: $(A,B,1),\,(C,D,2),\,(B,C,3),\,(A,C,4)$.

- $\mathcal{F}_0 = (\{A,B,C,D\},\, \emptyset)$: four isolated vertices.
- $\mathcal{F}_1$: add $(A,B,1)$; components $\{A,B\},\{C\},\{D\}$.
- $\mathcal{F}_2$: add $(C,D,2)$; components $\{A,B\},\{C,D\}$.
- $\mathcal{F}_3$: add $(B,C,3)$; merges all into one component — this is the minimum spanning tree, with total weight $6$.

The edge $(A,C,4)$ is rejected at step 4 because $A$ and $C$ are already connected.

**Key Theorem (Matroid Greedy Optimality).** The sequence $\mathcal{F}_0 \subset \mathcal{F}_1 \subset \cdots \subset \mathcal{F}_{n-c(G)}$ produced by processing edges in non-decreasing weight order is *optimal at every step*: for each $k$, no forest with exactly $k$ edges has a smaller total weight. This follows from the graphic matroid structure of $G$, where independent sets are exactly the acyclic edge subsets and the greedy algorithm is optimal over any matroid.

```python
import networkx as nx

G = nx.Graph()
G.add_weighted_edges_from([(0,1,1),(1,2,3),(0,2,4),(2,3,2)])

forest = nx.Graph()
forest.add_nodes_from(G.nodes())
uf = {v: v for v in G.nodes()}

def find(x):
    while uf[x] != x: uf[x] = uf[uf[x]]; x = uf[x]
    return x

for step, (u, v, wt) in enumerate(edges_sorted):
    if find(u) != find(v):
        forest.add_edge(u, v, weight=wt)
        uf[find(u)] = find(v)
        print(f"F_{step+1}: added ({u},{v},{wt}), edges={list(forest.edges(data='weight'))}")
```

### spanning_tree (0.0s)

## Spanning Tree

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E)$ be a connected undirected graph with $n = |V|$ vertices and $m = |E|$ edges. A *spanning tree* of $G$ is a subgraph $T = (V, E_T)$ such that (i) $E_T \subseteq E$, (ii) $T$ is connected, and (iii) $T$ contains no cycles. Because $T$ is both connected and acyclic, it is a tree, and it follows that $|E_T| = n - 1$ exactly. The adjective "spanning" emphasizes that $T$ includes every vertex of $G$, leaving none behind.

A spanning tree can equivalently be characterized as a maximal acyclic subgraph of $G$ (adding any edge from $E \setminus E_T$ creates exactly one cycle) or as a minimal connected spanning subgraph (removing any edge from $E_T$ disconnects it).

**Worked Example.** Consider the graph $G$ with vertex set $V = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and edge set $E = \{\{1,2\}, \{1,3\}, \{2,3\}, \{3,4\}\}$. Since $|V| = 4$, every spanning tree must contain exactly $3$ edges. One spanning tree is $T_1$ with $E_{T_1} = \{\{1,2\}, \{2,3\}, \{3,4\}\}$, forming a path $1 - 2 - 3 - 4$. Another is $T_2 = \{\{1,3\}, \{2,3\}, \{3,4\}\}$, a star centered at vertex $3$. The edge $\{1,2\}$ cannot appear together with $\{1,3\}$ and $\{2,3\}$ in any spanning tree, because those three edges form the cycle $1 - 2 - 3 - 1$.

**Theorem (Cayley's Formula).** The number of distinct labeled spanning trees of the complete graph $K_n$ is $n^{n-2}$. For $n = 4$, this gives $4^2 = 16$ spanning trees, which can be verified by enumeration. The proof proceeds via the *Prüfer sequence*: each labeled tree on $n$ vertices corresponds bijectively to a sequence in $\{1, \ldots, n\}^{n-2}$, establishing a bijection of size $n^{n-2}$.

### weighted_graph (0.0s)

## Weighted Graph

**Definition.** A *weighted graph* is a triple $G = (V, E, w)$ where $V$ is a finite set of *vertices*, $E \subseteq \{\{u, v\} : u, v \in V,\, u \neq v\}$ is a set of *edges*, and $w : E \to \mathbb{R}$ is a *weight function* assigning a real number to each edge. When the graph is directed, $E \subseteq V \times V$ and $w$ is defined on ordered pairs. The weight $w(\{u,v\})$ typically encodes a cost, distance, capacity, or similarity between $u$ and $v$.

The *weighted adjacency matrix* $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ (with $n = |V|$) is defined by

$$
W_{ij} = \begin{cases} w(\{i,j\}) & \text{if } \{i,j\} \in E, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

For undirected graphs, $W$ is symmetric. The *weighted degree* of a vertex $v$ is $\deg_w(v) = \sum_{u : \{u,v\} \in E} w(\{u,v\})$, and the *weighted Laplacian* is $L = D - W$, where $D = \mathrm{diag}(\deg_w(v_1), \ldots, \deg_w(v_n))$.

**Worked Example.** Consider the graph $G$ with $V = \{A, B, C\}$ and edges $\{A,B\}$, $\{B,C\}$, $\{A,C\}$ with weights $w(\{A,B\}) = 2$, $w(\{B,C\}) = 5$, $w(\{A,C\}) = 3$. The adjacency matrix is

$$
W = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 0 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
D = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 8 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
L = D - W = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -2 & -3 \\ -2 & 7 & -5 \\ -3 & -5 & 8 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Theorem (Positive Semi-Definiteness of $L$).** For any weighted graph with non-negative weights, the Laplacian $L$ is positive semi-definite, and for every $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$,

$$
\mathbf{x}^\top L\, \mathbf{x} = \sum_{\{i,j\} \in E} w(\{i,j\})\,(x_i - x_j)^2 \geq 0.
$$

*Proof sketch.* Expand $\mathbf{x}^\top (D - W)\mathbf{x}$ directly; each edge $\{i,j\}$ contributes $w_{ij}(x_i^2 - 2x_ix_j + x_j^2) = w_{ij}(x_i - x_j)^2 \geq 0$ when $w_{ij} \geq 0$. $\square$

The smallest eigenvalue of $L$ is always $0$ (eigenvector $\mathbf{1}$); the second-smallest, the *Fiedler value* $\lambda_2$, measures how well-connected the graph is — a central quantity in spectral clustering and graph partitioning.

```python
# Lab: compute the weighted Laplacian and its spectrum

            [2, 0, 5],
            [3, 5, 0]])

deg = [sum(row) for row in W.tolist()]

             for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)])

L = D - W
print("Laplacian L =", L)
print("Eigenvalues =", L.eigenvals())
```

### minimum_spanning_tree (0.0s)

## Minimum Spanning Tree

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E, w)$ be a connected, undirected graph where $w : E \to \mathbb{R}$ assigns a real weight to each edge. A *spanning tree* of $G$ is a subgraph $T \subseteq G$ that is a tree and contains every vertex in $V$. The *minimum spanning tree* (MST) of $G$ is the spanning tree $T^*$ that minimizes the total weight

$$w(T) = \sum_{e \in T} w(e).$$

When all edge weights are distinct, the MST is unique. If weights may repeat, multiple MSTs can exist, but their total weight is always the same.

**Worked Example.** Consider the graph $G$ with vertices $\{A, B, C, D\}$ and weighted edges:

| Edge | Weight |
|------|--------|
| $AB$ | 4 |
| $AC$ | 2 |
| $BC$ | 5 |
| $BD$ | 3 |
| $CD$ | 1 |

Applying Kruskal's algorithm, sort edges by weight: $CD=1,\; AC=2,\; BD=3,\; AB=4,\; BC=5$. Add $CD$ (no cycle), add $AC$ (no cycle), add $BD$ (no cycle) — three edges connect four vertices, so a spanning tree is complete. The MST is $\{CD, AC, BD\}$ with total weight $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$. Edge $AB$ would create a cycle $A$–$C$–$D$–$B$–$A$ and is therefore rejected.

**Key Theorem (Cut Property).** For any partition of $V$ into two nonempty sets $S$ and $V \setminus S$, the minimum-weight edge crossing the cut $(S, V \setminus S)$ belongs to every MST of $G$. Conversely, the *Cycle Property* states that the maximum-weight edge in any cycle cannot belong to any MST. Together, these two properties form the theoretical foundation for both Kruskal's algorithm ($O(|E| \log |E|)$) and Prim's algorithm ($O(|E| + |V| \log |V|)$ with a Fibonacci heap). Both are provably correct greedy strategies whose exchange arguments follow directly from these cut and cycle properties.

```python
])

print("MST edges:", list(T.edges(data="weight")))
```

### safe_edge (0.0s)

## Safe Edge

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E, w)$ be a connected, weighted, undirected graph with distinct edge weights, and let $A \subseteq E$ be a subset of edges that is contained in some minimum spanning tree (MST) of $G$. An edge $(u, v) \in E \setminus A$ is called a *safe edge* for $A$ if $A \cup \{(u,v)\}$ is still contained in some MST of $G$. The notion formalises the invariant maintained by greedy MST algorithms: at every step, we may extend $A$ by a safe edge without jeopardising optimality.

To state the characterisation precisely, recall two definitions. A *cut* of $G$ is a bipartition $(S,\, V \setminus S)$ of the vertex set. An edge *crosses* the cut if exactly one of its endpoints lies in $S$. A cut *respects* $A$ if no edge in $A$ crosses it. Among all edges crossing a given cut, a *light edge* is one of minimum weight.

**Worked Example.** Consider the graph with vertices $\{a, b, c, d\}$ and edges $ab{=}1,\, ac{=}4,\, bc{=}2,\, bd{=}5,\, cd{=}3$. Suppose $A = \{ab\}$, already part of the eventual MST. Take the cut $S = \{a, b\}$, $V \setminus S = \{c, d\}$. This cut respects $A$ (neither endpoint of $ab$ lies on opposite sides). The edges crossing the cut are $ac{=}4$, $bc{=}2$, $bd{=}5$, $cd{=}3$. The light edge is $bc$ with weight $2$. By the theorem below, $bc$ is safe for $A$, so we may set $A \leftarrow \{ab, bc\}$ and remain on the optimal path.

**Theorem (Safe-Edge / Cut Property).** *Let $A$ be a subset of some MST. If $(S, V \setminus S)$ is any cut that respects $A$, and $(u, v)$ is a light edge crossing that cut, then $(u, v)$ is safe for $A$.*

*Proof sketch.* Let $T$ be an MST containing $A$. If $(u,v) \in T$ we are done. Otherwise, adding $(u,v)$ to $T$ creates a unique cycle $C$. Since $T$ is a spanning tree, $C$ must contain another edge $(x,y)$ that also crosses $(S, V\setminus S)$. Because $(u,v)$ is a light edge, $w(u,v) \leq w(x,y)$. Replacing $(x,y)$ with $(u,v)$ yields a spanning tree $T'$ with $w(T') \leq w(T)$, so $T'$ is also an MST, and $A \cup \{(u,v)\} \subseteq T'$. $\blacksquare$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
import sympy as sp
from sympy.combinatorics.named_groups import *

# Represent a small weighted graph and verify the safe-edge criterion
edges = {('a','b'):1, ('a','c'):4, ('b','c'):2, ('b','d'):5, ('c','d'):3}
A = {('a','b')}
S = {'a','b'}

            if (e[0] in S) != (e[1] in S)}
respects  = all(e not in crossing for e in A)
light     = min(crossing, key=crossing.get)

print("Cut respects A:", respects)
print("Light (safe) edge:", light, "weight:", crossing[light])
```

### useless_edge (0.0s)

## Useless Edge

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E)$ be a connected, undirected graph with a weight function $w : E \to \mathbb{R}$. A *cut* of $G$ is a partition $(S, V \setminus S)$ of the vertex set into two non-empty subsets. An edge $e = (u, v)$ *crosses* the cut if $u \in S$ and $v \in V \setminus S$. We say that $e$ is a **useless edge** with respect to a growing spanning-tree forest $A \subseteq E$ if $e$ creates a cycle when added to $A$—equivalently, if both endpoints of $e$ already lie in the same connected component of $(V, A)$.

The concept is the logical complement of a *safe edge*: whereas a safe edge is the minimum-weight edge crossing some cut that respects $A$, a useless edge contributes no new connectivity and can never appear in a minimum spanning tree (MST) without violating the cycle property.

**Worked example.** Consider the graph with vertices $\{a, b, c, d\}$ and edges

$$e_1 = (a,b,2),\quad e_2 = (b,c,3),\quad e_3 = (a,c,5),\quad e_4 = (c,d,1),\quad e_5 = (b,d,4).$$

Run Kruskal's algorithm, processing edges in increasing weight order:

| Step | Edge | Weight | Action |
|------|------|--------|--------|
| 1 | $e_4$ | 1 | Add — connects $c$ and $d$ |
| 2 | $e_1$ | 2 | Add — connects $a$ and $b$ |
| 3 | $e_2$ | 3 | Add — merges $\{a,b\}$ with $\{c,d\}$ |
| 4 | $e_5$ | 4 | **Useless** — $b$ and $d$ already connected |
| 5 | $e_3$ | 5 | **Useless** — $a$ and $c$ already connected |

Edges $e_5$ and $e_3$ are useless because their endpoints share a component in the current forest $A$.

**Theorem (Cycle Property).** *Let $C$ be a cycle in $G$. If $e^* \in C$ is the unique maximum-weight edge of $C$, then $e^*$ does not belong to any MST of $G$.*

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $T$ is an MST containing $e^*$. Removing $e^*$ splits $T$ into two components. Since $C$ is a cycle, at least one other edge $e' \in C$ crosses this cut. By assumption $w(e') < w(e^*)$, so replacing $e^*$ with $e'$ produces a spanning tree of strictly smaller total weight—contradicting the minimality of $T$. $\square$

The cycle property gives a precise characterisation: an edge is useless for MST construction whenever it is the heaviest edge on some cycle.

```python
             if (u,v) not in mst_set and (v,u) not in mst_set]

print("MST edges :", mst_edges)
print("Useless edges:", useless)
```

### disjoint_set_data_structure (16.9s)

## Disjoint Set Data Structure

**Definition.** A *disjoint-set* (also called a *union–find*) data structure maintains a partition of a finite set $S = \{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ into non-overlapping subsets called *components*. Formally, a partition $\mathcal{P} = \{C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_k\}$ satisfies $C_i \cap C_j = \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$ and $\bigcup_{i=1}^k C_i = S$. Each component is identified by a canonical element called its *representative* or *root*. The structure supports two primitive operations:

- $\texttt{find}(x)$: return the representative of the component containing $x$.
- $\texttt{union}(x, y)$: merge the components containing $x$ and $y$ into a single component.

Internally, the structure is realized as a forest of rooted trees. Each element $x$ stores a *parent pointer* $p[x]$; if $p[x] = x$, then $x$ is a root. Finding the representative of $x$ amounts to following parent pointers until a root is reached.

**Worked Example.** Let $S = \{0,1,2,3,4\}$, initially five singleton components: $p = [0,1,2,3,4]$. Executing $\texttt{union}(0,1)$ sets $p[1] = 0$, giving component $\{0,1\}$ with root $0$. Then $\texttt{union}(2,3)$ yields $p[3] = 2$. Finally, $\texttt{union}(0,3)$ merges the two components: $\texttt{find}(0) = 0$ and $\texttt{find}(3) = 2$, so $p[2] = 0$. Now $\{0,1,2,3\}$ share root $0$, and $\{4\}$ remains isolated. Calling $\texttt{find}(3)$ traces $3 \to 2 \to 0$ and returns $0$.

**Key Theorem (Inverse-Ackermann bound).** When two heuristics are applied simultaneously—*union by rank* (always attach the shorter tree under the taller) and *path compression* (during $\texttt{find}$, redirect every visited node directly to the root)—any sequence of $m$ operations on $n$ elements runs in $O(m\,\alpha(n))$ time, where $\alpha$ is the inverse Ackermann function. Because $\alpha(n) \leq 4$ for all practical values of $n$, the amortized cost per operation is effectively constant.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols

# Minimal union-find with path compression and union by rank
class DisjointSet:
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.parent = list(range(n))
        self.rank   = [0] * n

    def find(self, x):
        if self.parent[x] != x:
            self.parent[x] = self.find(self.parent[x])  # path compression
        return self.parent[x]

    def union(self, x, y):
        rx, ry = self.find(x), self.find(y)
        if rx == ry:
            return
        if self.rank[rx] < self.rank[ry]:
            rx, ry = ry, rx
        self.parent[ry] = rx
        if self.rank[rx] == self.rank[ry]:
            self.rank[rx] += 1

ds = DisjointSet(5)
for a, b in [(0,1),(2,3),(0,3)]:
    ds.union(a, b)
print([ds.find(i) for i in range(5)])  # [0, 0, 0, 0, 4]
```

## Generic Mst Algorithm

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E, w)$ be a connected, undirected graph with a weight function $w : E \to \mathbb{R}$. A *minimum spanning tree* (MST) of $G$ is a spanning tree $T \subseteq E$ such that $w(T) = \sum_{e \in T} w(e)$ is minimized over all spanning trees. The *Generic MST Algorithm* is a greedy schema that builds $T$ one safe edge at a time. Formally, a set $A \subseteq E$ is called a *partial MST* if there exists some MST $T^*$ with $A \subseteq T^*$. An edge $e \notin A$ is *safe* for $A$ if $A \cup \{e\}$ is still a partial MST.

The algorithm maintains $A$ as an acyclic partial MST and, at each step, identifies and adds any safe edge:

$$A \leftarrow \emptyset; \quad \textbf{while } |A| < |V| - 1 \textbf{ do: find safe edge } (u,v); \; A \leftarrow A \cup \{(u,v)\}$$

**Worked Example.** Consider $G$ with $V = \{1,2,3,4\}$ and edges $\{(1,2,1),\,(2,3,3),\,(1,3,4),\,(3,4,2)\}$ (third entry is weight). Begin with $A = \emptyset$. The lightest edge $(1,2)$ with weight $1$ is safe; add it. Next, $(3,4)$ with weight $2$ is safe since it connects two distinct components of $A$; add it. Finally, $(2,3)$ with weight $3$ connects the two remaining components without forming a cycle; add it. We obtain $T = \{(1,2),(3,4),(2,3)\}$ with $w(T) = 6$, which is the MST.

**Key Theorem (Cut Property).** Let $A$ be a partial MST, $(S, V \setminus S)$ a cut that *respects* $A$ (no edge of $A$ crosses it), and $(u,v)$ a minimum-weight edge crossing the cut. Then $(u,v)$ is safe for $A$.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $T^*$ is an MST containing $A$ but not $(u,v)$. Since $T^*$ is spanning, it contains some edge $e'$ crossing $(S, V \setminus S)$. The tree $T' = T^* - e' + (u,v)$ is also spanning; because $w(u,v) \le w(e')$, we have $w(T') \le w(T^*)$, so $T'$ is an MST containing $A \cup \{(u,v)\}$, confirming safety. $\blacksquare$

**Lab Cell.**
```python
import sympy as sp
from sympy.combinatorics.prufer import Prufer

edges_sorted = sorted(edges, key=lambda e: e[2])

    if u not in nodes_seen or v not in nodes_seen or \
       not any(u in c and v in c for c in [nodes_seen]):

        nodes_seen.update([u, v])

print("MST edges:", A)
print("Total weight:", sum(w for _,_,w in A))
```

### kruskals_algorithm (18.1s)

## Kruskal's Algorithm

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E, w)$ be a connected, undirected graph with vertex set $V$, edge set $E$, and weight function $w : E \to \mathbb{R}$. A *minimum spanning tree* (MST) of $G$ is a spanning subgraph $T \subseteq E$ that is acyclic, connects all vertices, and minimizes $\sum_{e \in T} w(e)$. Kruskal's algorithm constructs an MST greedily: sort all edges in non-decreasing order of weight, then add each edge to the growing forest if and only if it does not form a cycle with edges already selected. The process terminates when $|V| - 1$ edges have been accepted.

Formally, maintaining a partition of $V$ into connected components (a *disjoint-set forest*), an edge $(u, v)$ is safe to add precisely when $u$ and $v$ belong to different components; adding it merges those two components.

---

**Worked Example.** Consider $G$ with vertices $\{A, B, C, D\}$ and weighted edges:

$$
(A,B,1),\; (A,C,4),\; (B,C,2),\; (B,D,5),\; (C,D,3).
$$

Sort by weight: $(A,B,1),\,(B,C,2),\,(C,D,3),\,(A,C,4),\,(B,D,5)$.

| Step | Edge    | Components before            | Action  |
|------|---------|------------------------------|---------|
| 1    | $(A,B)$ | $\{A\},\{B\},\{C\},\{D\}$   | Accept  |
| 2    | $(B,C)$ | $\{A,B\},\{C\},\{D\}$       | Accept  |
| 3    | $(C,D)$ | $\{A,B,C\},\{D\}$           | Accept  |
| 4    | $(A,C)$ | $\{A,B,C,D\}$               | Reject (cycle) |

The MST is $\{(A,B),(B,C),(C,D)\}$ with total weight $1+2+3 = 6$.

---

**Key Theorem (Correctness of Kruskal's Algorithm).** *The output of Kruskal's algorithm is a minimum spanning tree of $G$.*

*Proof sketch.* Let $T$ be the tree produced and $T^*$ any MST. Suppose they differ; let $e$ be the lightest edge in $T \setminus T^*$. Adding $e$ to $T^*$ creates a unique cycle $C$. Some edge $f \in C$ lies in $T^* \setminus T$; by the cut property of MSTs, $w(e) \leq w(f)$. Swapping $f$ for $e$ yields another MST no heavier than $T^*$. Repeating this exchange argument shows $w(T) = w(T^*)$. $\square$

Time complexity is $O(|E| \log |E|)$, dominated by sorting; union–find operations contribute only $O(|E|\,\alpha(|V|))$, where $\alpha$ is the inverse Ackermann function.

```python
import sympy as sp
from sympy.combinatorics import Permutation   # not needed here
# Represent graph as edge list (u, v, weight)
edges = [(0,1,1),(0,2,4),(1,2,2),(1,3,5),(2,3,3)]
edges_sorted = sorted(edges, key=lambda e: e[2])

parent = list(range(4))

def find(x):
    while parent[x] != x:
        parent[x] = parent[parent[x]]
        x = parent[x]
    return x

mst, total = [], 0
for u, v, w in edges_sorted:
    pu, pv = find(u), find(v)
    if pu != pv:
        mst.append((u, v, w))
        parent[pu] = pv
        total += w

print("MST edges:", mst)
print("Total weight:", sp.Integer(total))
```
