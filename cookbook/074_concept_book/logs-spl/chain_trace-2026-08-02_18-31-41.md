### limit (17.3s)

## Limit

A **limit** describes the value a function approaches as its input gets arbitrarily close to some point — not necessarily the value *at* that point. Formally, we write

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

and mean: for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. In plain terms: we can make $f(x)$ as close to $L$ as we like by keeping $x$ close enough to $a$. The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition is not just bookkeeping — it is what allows limits to exist at points where the function is undefined or discontinuous, which is exactly the situation that makes limits useful.

**Worked example.** Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$$

At $x = 1$, the denominator is zero, so $f(1)$ is undefined. But we can factor:

$$\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x + 1, \quad x \neq 1.$$

As $x \to 1$, $f(x)$ approaches $1 + 1 = 2$. The limit exists and equals 2 even though the function has a hole at that point. The graph would look like the line $y = x + 1$ with a single missing dot at $(1, 2)$.

**Problem-solving application.** Limits are the engine behind derivatives and integrals. When you compute a derivative,

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$

you are asking what the difference quotient *approaches* as $h$ shrinks, not what it equals at $h = 0$ (where it is $\tfrac{0}{0}$). The practical move is always the same: simplify algebraically to cancel the offending factor, then evaluate. For instance, $f(x) = x^2$ gives

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(2a + h) = 2a.$$

When algebra alone does not cancel the singularity, tools like L'Hôpital's rule or Taylor series take over. The key habit: before reaching for those tools, always try factoring or rationalizing — most limit problems at this level collapse with one algebraic step.

### area_problem (33.5s)

## Area Problem

The **area problem** asks: given a function $f(x) \geq 0$ on a closed interval $[a, b]$, what is the exact area of the region bounded above by the curve $y = f(x)$, below by the $x$-axis, and on the sides by $x = a$ and $x = b$?

For geometric shapes like rectangles and triangles, area is straightforward. For an arbitrary curve, the boundary is irregular — so the strategy is to approximate the region with many thin rectangles, then take a limit as the rectangles become infinitely narrow.

**Riemann Sum Approximation.** Divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$. On each subinterval $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$, erect a rectangle with height $f(x_i^*)$, where $x_i^*$ is any sample point in that subinterval (right endpoint, left endpoint, or midpoint are common choices). The total rectangle area is the **Riemann sum**:

$$R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x$$

As $n \to \infty$ the rectangles thin out and the approximation improves. When $f$ is continuous, the limit exists and gives the exact area:

$$A = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$

This limit is the **definite integral** — the formal resolution of the area problem.

**Worked Example.** Find the area under $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 1]$ using right-endpoint rectangles. With $\Delta x = 1/n$ and $x_i = i/n$:

$$R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\frac{i}{n}\right)^2 \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{n^3} \sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2 = \frac{1}{n^3} \cdot \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} = \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}$$

Taking the limit: $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2} = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$.

**Problem-Solving Application.** The area problem appears whenever a rate accumulates over time. Suppose a car's velocity is $v(t) = 3t^2$ m/s over $0 \leq t \leq 2$ s. Distance equals the area under the velocity curve. Using a right-endpoint Riemann sum with $n = 4$ rectangles ($\Delta t = 0.5$):

$$R_4 = 3(0.5)^2(0.5) + 3(1)^2(0.5) + 3(1.5)^2(0.5) + 3(2)^2(0.5) = 0.375 + 1.5 + 3.375 + 6 = 11.25 \text{ m}$$

The exact answer via the integral is $\displaystyle\int_0^2 3t^2\,dt = \bigl[t^3\bigr]_0^2 = 8$ m. The Riemann sum overestimates because $v(t)$ is increasing and right endpoints pick the largest value on each subinterval — a useful sanity check when you set up the approximation.

### integral_calculus (17.8s)

## Integral Calculus

Integral calculus answers a deceptively simple question: *how do you find the total of something that is continuously changing?* If a car's velocity varies every second, how far did it travel? If a factory's emission rate fluctuates hourly, how much pollutant was released today? The integral is the tool that accumulates infinitely many infinitesimally small pieces into an exact total.

The **definite integral** of a function $f(x)$ from $a$ to $b$ is written

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$$

and represents the signed area between the curve $y = f(x)$ and the $x$-axis over the interval $[a, b]$. Area above the axis contributes positively; area below contributes negatively. The **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** ties this geometric idea to derivatives: if $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ (meaning $F'(x) = f(x)$), then

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = F(b) - F(a).$$

This theorem is the engine of practical computation — it converts accumulation problems into antiderivative lookups.

### Worked Example

A particle moves along a line with velocity $v(t) = 3t^2 - 4t$ (in m/s) for $0 \le t \le 3$ seconds. Find the total displacement.

Displacement is the integral of velocity:

$$\int_0^3 (3t^2 - 4t)\, dt$$

Find the antiderivative term by term: $\int 3t^2\, dt = t^3$ and $\int -4t\, dt = -2t^2$, so $F(t) = t^3 - 2t^2$.

Apply the Fundamental Theorem:

$$F(3) - F(0) = (27 - 18) - (0 - 0) = 9 \text{ m}.$$

The particle ends up 9 meters ahead of its starting point.

### Problem-Solving Application

Integrals appear wherever a rate must be converted into a total. To set one up correctly: (1) identify the rate function $f(x)$ and the interval $[a, b]$; (2) find an antiderivative $F$; (3) evaluate $F(b) - F(a)$.

A common trap is confusing *displacement* with *total distance*. Displacement uses the signed integral; total distance requires integrating $|v(t)|$, which means splitting the interval at any zero of $v$ and adding absolute values of each piece. Always ask whether cancellation of opposite-sign areas is physically meaningful before reporting a final answer.
