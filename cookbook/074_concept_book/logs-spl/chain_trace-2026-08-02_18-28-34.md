### relation (0.2s)

## Relation

A **relation** from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is any subset $R \subseteq A \times B$, where $A \times B$ is the Cartesian product — the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \in A$ and $b \in B$. Writing $a \mathrel{R} b$ means the pair $(a, b)$ belongs to $R$. When $A = B$, we call $R$ a **relation on** $A$.

This definition is more general than it first appears. "Is less than" on $\mathbb{Z}$ is a relation: $3 \mathrel{<} 7$ simply means $(3, 7) \in \{(a,b) \mid a < b\}$. "Is a sibling of" on a set of people is a relation. Even a function $f : A \to B$ is a special relation — specifically, one where every $a \in A$ appears in exactly one pair. Relations are the broader category; functions are the well-behaved special case.

**Worked example.** Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and define $R = \{(1,1),(2,2),(3,3),(1,2),(2,1)\}$. Is $(1,3) \in R$? No — check the list. Is $(2,1) \in R$? Yes. Notice that $R$ satisfies three useful properties: every element is related to itself (reflexive), whenever $a \mathrel{R} b$ then $b \mathrel{R} a$ (symmetric), and whenever $a \mathrel{R} b$ and $b \mathrel{R} c$ then $a \mathrel{R} c$ (transitive — you can verify this holds here). A relation with all three properties is called an **equivalence relation**, and it partitions $A$ into disjoint clusters called **equivalence classes**.

**Problem-solving application.** Suppose you must decide whether "has the same remainder when divided by 3" is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$. Work through each property:

- *Reflexive*: $a - a = 0$, which is divisible by 3, so $a \mathrel{R} a$. ✓
- *Symmetric*: if $3 \mid (a - b)$, then $3 \mid (b - a)$. ✓
- *Transitive*: if $3 \mid (a - b)$ and $3 \mid (b - c)$, then $3 \mid (a - c)$ because $(a - c) = (a - b) + (b - c)$. ✓

All three hold, so $R$ is an equivalence relation, and it partitions $\mathbb{Z}$ into exactly three equivalence classes: $\{..., -3, 0, 3, 6, ...\}$, $\{..., -2, 1, 4, 7, ...\}$, and $\{..., -1, 2, 5, 8, ...\}$. This structure — modular arithmetic — is the foundation of cryptography, hash tables, and error-correcting codes.

### function (0.3s)

## Function

A **function** is a rule that assigns to each input exactly one output. More precisely, a function $f$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$, written $f: A \to B$, pairs every element $x \in A$ with a unique element $f(x) \in B$. The set $A$ is called the **domain**, $B$ is the **codomain**, and the collection of all actual outputs $\{f(x) : x \in A\}$ is the **range** (or image). The uniqueness requirement is the defining constraint: one input cannot produce two different outputs. The reverse is allowed — two inputs can share an output — but ambiguity in the forward direction is forbidden.

This is why $f(x) = x^2$ qualifies as a function ($x = -3$ and $x = 3$ both map to $9$, which is fine), while the relation $x = y^2$ does not define $y$ as a function of $x$ (the input $x = 9$ maps to both $y = 3$ and $y = -3$).

**Worked example.** A cell-phone plan charges a \$20 base fee plus \$0.05 per text message. Define the monthly cost as a function of messages sent:

$$C(n) = 20 + 0.05n, \quad n \in \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$$

Evaluate $C(200)$: $C(200) = 20 + 0.05(200) = 20 + 10 = \$30$. For each number of texts there is exactly one cost — the function condition holds.

**Problem-solving application.** Functions become powerful when you need to find inputs that produce a target output, or compare two functions to locate where they agree.

*Find the break-even point* between Plan A ($C_A(n) = 20 + 0.05n$) and Plan B ($C_B(n) = 35 + 0.02n$):

$$C_A(n) = C_B(n)$$
$$20 + 0.05n = 35 + 0.02n$$
$$0.03n = 15 \implies n = 500$$

Below 500 texts, Plan A is cheaper; above 500, Plan B wins. Setting two function expressions equal and solving for the input is one of the most recurring moves in applied mathematics — it appears in physics (when do two objects meet?), economics (break-even analysis), and computer science (when does one algorithm outperform another?). Recognizing that a quantity *is* a function of another variable is the first step toward asking and answering those questions precisely.

### exponential_function (16.1s)

## Exponential Function

**Definition.** An exponential function has the form $f(x) = a \cdot b^x$, where $a$ is the initial value, $b > 0$ is the base, and $x$ is the exponent. The defining feature is that the *input is in the exponent* — which makes the function grow (or decay) by a constant *multiplicative factor* for every unit increase in $x$. When $b > 1$ the function grows; when $0 < b < 1$ it decays. The natural exponential $f(x) = e^x$, where $e \approx 2.71828$, appears throughout science because it is its own derivative — the rate of change equals the current value.

**Worked example.** A colony of bacteria doubles every 3 hours. You start with 500 cells. How many cells are there after 12 hours?

The doubling period is 3 hours, so after $t$ hours the number of doublings is $t/3$. The population model is:

$$P(t) = 500 \cdot 2^{t/3}$$

At $t = 12$:

$$P(12) = 500 \cdot 2^{12/3} = 500 \cdot 2^4 = 500 \cdot 16 = 8{,}000$$

Notice the mechanics: each 3-hour interval multiplies the population by 2, regardless of how large it already is. That multiplicative consistency is what makes the function exponential.

**Problem-solving application.** Exponential functions appear whenever a quantity changes proportionally to its current size — compound interest, radioactive decay, viral spread, drug clearance. The key problem-solving move is identifying the *base* and the *period*.

For compound interest, $A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$, the base is $(1 + r/n)$ and each compounding period multiplies the balance by that factor. For radioactive decay with half-life $h$, the base is $1/2$ and the period is $h$:

$$N(t) = N_0 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t/h}$$

A practical check: if the ratio $P(t+k)/P(t)$ is constant for all $t$, the relationship is exponential. If that ratio keeps changing, look for a different model (polynomial, logistic, etc.). Plotting data on a log scale turns an exponential into a straight line — a quick visual test that practitioners use before fitting any model.

### number_e (18.5s)

## Number E

The number $e \approx 2.71828\ldots$ is one of the most important constants in mathematics, rivaling $\pi$ in how often it appears across science and engineering. It is defined as the limit

$$e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$$

This definition is not arbitrary. It arises naturally from compound interest: if you invest \$1 at 100% annual interest compounded $n$ times per year, your balance after one year is $\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$. As compounding becomes continuous ($n \to \infty$), the balance converges to exactly $e$ dollars — no more, no matter how finely you subdivide. This is why $e$ is the natural base for continuous growth and decay.

The function $f(x) = e^x$ has a unique property: it is its own derivative. That is, $\frac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$. No other base has this property, which is why $e$ simplifies calculus dramatically — exponential models written in base $e$ differentiate and integrate without extra scale factors.

**Worked example.** A bacterial culture starts with 500 cells and grows continuously at a rate of 3% per hour. The population at time $t$ hours is $P(t) = 500\, e^{0.03t}$. How long until the culture doubles?

Set $P(t) = 1000$:

$$500\, e^{0.03t} = 1000 \implies e^{0.03t} = 2 \implies 0.03t = \ln 2 \implies t = \frac{\ln 2}{0.03} \approx 23.1 \text{ hours}$$

This is the **doubling time formula**: $t_{\text{double}} = \frac{\ln 2}{r}$, where $r$ is the continuous growth rate.

**Problem-solving application.** Radioactive carbon-14 decays with a continuous rate $r = -0.000121$ per year. A fossil sample has 40% of the original carbon-14 remaining. Find its age.

Use $A(t) = A_0\, e^{rt}$ and set $A(t)/A_0 = 0.40$:

$$e^{-0.000121\, t} = 0.40 \implies t = \frac{\ln(0.40)}{-0.000121} \approx \frac{-0.916}{-0.000121} \approx 7{,}570 \text{ years}$$

The key skill is isolating the exponent using the natural logarithm $\ln$, since $\ln$ and $e^x$ are inverse functions: $\ln(e^x) = x$. Whenever a quantity grows or decays continuously — populations, drug concentrations, charge on a capacitor — the model involves $e$, and solving for time or rate always reduces to a single application of $\ln$.

### natural_exponential_function (18.7s)

## Natural Exponential Function

The **natural exponential function** is $f(x) = e^x$, where $e \approx 2.71828$ is Euler's number. What makes $e$ special is that $e^x$ is its own derivative — the rate at which $e^x$ grows at any point equals the value of the function at that point. This self-referential property makes it the natural model for any process where the growth rate is proportional to the current size: populations, radioactive decay, compound interest, and cooling.

The domain is all real numbers, the range is $(0, \infty)$, and the graph is always increasing with a horizontal asymptote at $y = 0$ as $x \to -\infty$.

**Worked example.** A bacterial culture starts with 500 cells and doubles every 20 minutes. Model the population $P(t)$ using the natural exponential function, where $t$ is measured in minutes.

Doubling every 20 minutes means the growth rate constant $k$ satisfies $e^{k \cdot 20} = 2$, so:

$$k = \frac{\ln 2}{20} \approx 0.0347 \text{ min}^{-1}$$

The model is:

$$P(t) = 500 \, e^{0.0347t}$$

At $t = 60$ minutes (three hours): $P(60) = 500 \, e^{0.0347 \times 60} = 500 \, e^{2.08} \approx 500 \times 8.00 = 4000$ cells. This matches the expected result of $500 \times 2^3 = 4000$, confirming the model.

**Problem-solving application.** Newton's Law of Cooling states that an object's temperature approaches the ambient temperature exponentially:

$$T(t) = T_{\text{env}} + (T_0 - T_{\text{env}}) \, e^{-kt}$$

Suppose a cup of coffee at $90°C$ sits in a $20°C$ room. After 10 minutes it measures $70°C$. Find the temperature at 30 minutes.

First, solve for $k$:

$$70 = 20 + 70 \, e^{-10k} \implies e^{-10k} = \frac{50}{70} \implies k = -\frac{\ln(5/7)}{10} \approx 0.0336$$

Then at $t = 30$:

$$T(30) = 20 + 70 \, e^{-0.0336 \times 30} = 20 + 70 \, e^{-1.008} \approx 20 + 25.5 \approx 45.5°C$$

The key problem-solving moves are always the same: identify the initial value, find $k$ from one data point using $\ln$, then substitute the target time. Recognizing the exponential structure of a scenario — growth or decay proportional to current value — is the signal to reach for $e^x$.

### compound_interest_application (17.3s)

## Compound Interest Application

**Compound interest** is the mechanism by which interest is calculated not just on the original principal but also on all previously accumulated interest. This "interest on interest" effect causes balances to grow exponentially rather than linearly, and understanding it is essential for evaluating loans, savings accounts, retirement accounts, and investment returns.

The core formula is:

$$A = P\!\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$$

where $P$ is the principal, $r$ is the annual interest rate (as a decimal), $n$ is the number of compounding periods per year, $t$ is time in years, and $A$ is the final amount. When compounding happens continuously, the formula collapses to $A = Pe^{rt}$.

**Worked example.** Suppose you invest \$5{,}000 at an annual rate of 6%, compounded monthly, for 10 years. Here $P = 5000$, $r = 0.06$, $n = 12$, $t = 10$.

$$A = 5000\!\left(1 + \frac{0.06}{12}\right)^{12 \times 10} = 5000\,(1.005)^{120}$$

Computing $(1.005)^{120} \approx 1.8194$, so $A \approx \$9{,}097$. You nearly doubled your money without adding another dollar — the extra \$4{,}097 came entirely from compound growth.

**Problem-solving application.** Compound interest problems typically come in three flavors: find the future value $A$ (shown above), find the required principal $P$ (present value), or find the time $t$ needed to reach a target.

Suppose you need \$20{,}000 in 8 years for a down payment. Your account earns 5% compounded quarterly. How much should you deposit today?

Rearrange the formula for $P$:

$$P = \frac{A}{\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}} = \frac{20000}{\left(1.0125\right)^{32}} \approx \frac{20000}{1.4881} \approx \$13{,}441$$

You would need to deposit about \$13{,}441 today. Notice that a higher compounding frequency ($n$) or a longer time horizon $t$ both amplify growth — so when comparing financial products, always check both the rate *and* the compounding schedule, not just the advertised annual rate. The **effective annual rate (EAR)**, $\left(1 + r/n\right)^n - 1$, is the single number that lets you compare products on equal footing regardless of how frequently they compound.
