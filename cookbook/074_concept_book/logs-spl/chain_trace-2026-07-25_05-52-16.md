### column_vector (0.0s)

## Column Vector

A **column vector** is a matrix consisting of a single column — that is, an array of $n$ real (or complex) numbers arranged vertically. Formally, a column vector $\mathbf{v}$ of dimension $n$ is an element of $\mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}$, written

$$\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{pmatrix},$$

where each $v_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is called the $i$-th **component** (or **entry**) of $\mathbf{v}$. When the dimension is clear from context, we write $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The **transpose** $\mathbf{v}^\top = (v_1,\, v_2,\, \ldots,\, v_n)$ converts the column vector into a row vector.

**Worked Example.** Let $\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Their sum is

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 3+0 \\ -1+4 \\ 2+(-3) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Scalar multiplication by $c = 2$ yields $2\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ -2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$. These operations are performed component-wise, confirming that $\mathbb{R}^n$ is closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

**Theorem (Standard Inner Product).** For column vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the **dot product** is defined as

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i.$$

This is a real number, not a vector. In particular, the **Euclidean norm** of $\mathbf{v}$ is $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v}}$, and two vectors are **orthogonal** if and only if $\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = 0$. For the example above, $\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{w} = (3)(0)+(-1)(4)+(2)(-3) = -10 \neq 0$, so $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ are not orthogonal.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, sqrt

u = Matrix([3, -1, 2])
w = Matrix([0,  4, -3])

print("Sum:      ", u + w)
print("2u:       ", 2 * u)
print("Dot prod: ", u.dot(w))
print("||u||:    ", sqrt(u.dot(u)))
```

Running this cell confirms $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} = (3, 3, -1)^\top$, $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w} = -10$, and $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{14}$.

### complex_number (0.0s)

## Complex Number

**Definition.** A *complex number* is an ordered pair $(a, b)$ of real numbers, written in standard form as

$$z = a + bi,$$

where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $i$ is the *imaginary unit*, defined by the property $i^2 = -1$. The value $a = \operatorname{Re}(z)$ is the *real part* and $b = \operatorname{Im}(z)$ is the *imaginary part*. The set of all complex numbers is denoted $\mathbb{C}$. Geometrically, $z$ is identified with the point $(a, b)$ in the *complex plane* (Argand diagram), where the horizontal axis carries real parts and the vertical axis carries imaginary parts.

The *modulus* (absolute value) of $z$ is $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, and the *argument* is the angle $\theta = \arg(z) = \arctan(b/a)$ measured counterclockwise from the positive real axis. These give the *polar form* $z = r e^{i\theta}$, justified by Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$.

**Worked example.** Let $z_1 = 3 + 4i$ and $z_2 = 1 - 2i$. Compute the product $z_1 z_2$ and find $|z_1|$.

$$z_1 z_2 = (3 + 4i)(1 - 2i) = 3 - 6i + 4i - 8i^2 = 3 - 2i + 8 = 11 - 2i.$$

$$|z_1| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5.$$

Note how the factor $-8i^2$ becomes $+8$ because $i^2 = -1$; this substitution is the central computational rule for all arithmetic in $\mathbb{C}$.

**Key theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra).** Every non-constant polynomial with coefficients in $\mathbb{C}$ has at least one root in $\mathbb{C}$. Consequently, every degree-$n$ polynomial factors completely into exactly $n$ linear factors over $\mathbb{C}$. This theorem, whose proof relies on topological completeness of $\mathbb{C}$, explains why complex numbers are the natural domain for polynomial algebra: the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ are insufficient (e.g., $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real solution), whereas $\mathbb{C}$ is *algebraically closed*.

**Lab cell.**

```python
z2 = 1 - 2*I

euler_check = exp(I * pi) + 1      # simplifies to 0

print(product, modulus, euler_check.simplify())
```

### complex_conjugate_vector (0.0s)

## Complex Conjugate Vector

**Definition.** Let $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n) \in \mathbb{C}^n$ be a vector with complex entries, where each $v_k = a_k + b_k i$ for real numbers $a_k, b_k$. The *complex conjugate vector* of $\mathbf{v}$, denoted $\overline{\mathbf{v}}$, is the vector obtained by replacing each entry with its complex conjugate:

$$\overline{\mathbf{v}} = (\overline{v_1}, \overline{v_2}, \ldots, \overline{v_n}) = (a_1 - b_1 i,\; a_2 - b_2 i,\; \ldots,\; a_n - b_n i).$$

The operation is applied entrywise; no reordering of components occurs. When $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, every imaginary part is zero and $\overline{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{v}$.

**Worked Example.** Let $\mathbf{v} = (3 + 2i,\; -1 + 4i,\; 5i) \in \mathbb{C}^3$. Applying the definition entry by entry:

$$\overline{\mathbf{v}} = (3 - 2i,\; -1 - 4i,\; -5i).$$

To verify, compute the entrywise product $\mathbf{v} \odot \overline{\mathbf{v}}$:

$$(3+2i)(3-2i) = 9 + 4 = 13, \quad (-1+4i)(-1-4i) = 1 + 16 = 17, \quad (5i)(-5i) = 25.$$

Each result is a non-negative real number, illustrating the general identity $v_k \overline{v_k} = |v_k|^2$.

**Key Theorem (Conjugate Linearity).** For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{C}^n$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$:

$$\overline{\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}} = \overline{\mathbf{u}} + \overline{\mathbf{v}}, \qquad \overline{\alpha\, \mathbf{v}} = \overline{\alpha}\;\overline{\mathbf{v}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Both identities follow immediately from the corresponding scalar identities $\overline{z + w} = \overline{z} + \overline{w}$ and $\overline{\alpha z} = \overline{\alpha}\,\overline{z}$, applied coordinate by coordinate. $\square$

A direct consequence is that the standard Hermitian inner product on $\mathbb{C}^n$ is expressible as $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\top} \mathbf{v}$, making the complex conjugate vector indispensable in quantum mechanics and signal processing.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python

print("v̄ =", v_conj)
print("|v_k|² =", (v.applyfunc(conjugate).multiply_elementwise(v)))
```

### scalar_multiplication (0.0s)

## Scalar Multiplication

**Definition.** Let $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a vector and let $c \in \mathbb{R}$ be a scalar. The *scalar multiple* of $\mathbf{v}$ by $c$ is the vector

$$c\mathbf{v} = (cv_1, cv_2, \ldots, cv_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n.$$

Geometrically, scalar multiplication scales the magnitude of $\mathbf{v}$ by $|c|$ and reverses its direction when $c < 0$. When $c = 0$ the result is the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$, and when $c = 1$ the vector is unchanged.

**Worked Example.** Let $\mathbf{v} = (3, -2, 5)$ and $c = -4$. Then

$$c\mathbf{v} = -4 \cdot (3, -2, 5) = (-12,\ 8,\ -20).$$

The Euclidean norm satisfies $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = |c|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|$. Here $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{9 + 4 + 25} = \sqrt{38}$, so $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = 4\sqrt{38}$, which agrees with direct computation: $\sqrt{144 + 64 + 400} = \sqrt{608} = 4\sqrt{38}$.

**Key Theorem (Properties of Scalar Multiplication).** For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$:

1. $(cd)\mathbf{v} = c(d\mathbf{v})$ *(associativity)*
2. $c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$ *(distributivity over vector addition)*
3. $(c + d)\mathbf{v} = c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{v}$ *(distributivity over scalar addition)*
4. $1 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$ *(identity)*

*Proof sketch.* Each identity follows directly from the componentwise definition and the corresponding properties of real-number arithmetic. For property 2, the $i$-th component of $c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v})$ is $c(u_i + v_i) = cu_i + cv_i$, which is the $i$-th component of $c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$. The remaining identities are analogous. $\square$

These four properties, together with the axioms of vector addition, form the complete axiomatic definition of a *vector space* over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import Matrix
v = Matrix([3, -2, 5])
c = -4
cv = c * v
print(cv)           # Matrix([-12, 8, -20])
print(cv.norm())    # 4*sqrt(38)
```

### vector_addition (0.0s)

## Vector Addition

**Definition.** Let $\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n)$ be vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Their *vector sum* is defined componentwise as

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (u_1 + v_1,\; u_2 + v_2,\; \ldots,\; u_n + v_n).$$

Geometrically, this corresponds to the *parallelogram law*: place $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ tail-to-tail at the origin; the diagonal of the parallelogram they span is $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$. Equivalently, under the *tip-to-tail* interpretation, translate $\mathbf{v}$ so that its tail meets the tip of $\mathbf{u}$; the resultant arrow from the origin to the new tip of $\mathbf{v}$ is the sum.

**Worked Example.** Consider $\mathbf{u} = (3, -1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (-1, 4, 5)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Applying the definition component by component:

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (3 + (-1),\; -1 + 4,\; 2 + 5) = (2, 3, 7).$$

We can verify the geometric interpretation: the magnitude of $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 49} = \sqrt{62} \approx 7.87$, which lies between $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{14}$ and $\|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{14} + \sqrt{42}$, consistent with the triangle inequality.

**Key Theorem (Properties of Vector Addition).** For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

1. *Commutativity:* $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$.
2. *Associativity:* $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$.
3. *Additive identity:* $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u}$, where $\mathbf{0} = (0, 0, \ldots, 0)$.
4. *Additive inverse:* $\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}$.

Each property follows directly from the corresponding property of real-number addition applied componentwise, so no separate proof is required beyond citing the field axioms of $\mathbb{R}$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy.matrices import Matrix

u = Matrix([3, -1, 2])
v = Matrix([-1, 4, 5])

w = u + v          # componentwise addition
print("u + v =", w.T)
print("||u + v|| =", w.norm())
print("Commutative check:", u + v == v + u)
```

Running this cell confirms $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (2, 3, 7)^{\top}$, $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{62}$, and commutativity, providing immediate computational verification of the definitions and theorem above.

### inner_product (0.0s)

## Inner Product

**Definition.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ (either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$). An *inner product* on $V$ is a function $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to \mathbb{F}$ satisfying, for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in V$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}$:

1. **Conjugate symmetry:** $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \overline{\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u} \rangle}$
2. **Linearity in the first argument:** $\langle \alpha\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \alpha\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle + \langle \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$
3. **Positive definiteness:** $\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle \geq 0$, with equality if and only if $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$

A vector space equipped with an inner product is called an *inner product space*. Over $\mathbb{R}$, conjugate symmetry reduces to ordinary symmetry $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u} \rangle$.

**Worked Example.** Consider $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the *standard (Euclidean) inner product* $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} u_i v_i$. Take $\mathbf{u} = (1, 2, -1)^\top$ and $\mathbf{v} = (3, 0, 2)^\top$. Then

$$\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = (1)(3) + (2)(0) + (-1)(2) = 3 + 0 - 2 = 1.$$

The induced *norm* is $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle} = \sqrt{9 + 0 + 4} = \sqrt{13}$. The angle $\theta$ between the two vectors satisfies $\cos\theta = \langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle / (\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|)$, providing a coordinate-free measure of geometric orientation.

**Key Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality).** For any $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ in an inner product space,

$$|\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle|^2 \leq \langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u} \rangle \cdot \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle.$$

*Proof sketch.* For $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$, define $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} - \frac{\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle}{\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle}\mathbf{v}$. Positive definiteness gives $\langle \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{w} \rangle \geq 0$; expanding this expression and rearranging yields the inequality.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Matrix, sqrt, acos, pi

u = Matrix([1, 2, -1])
v = Matrix([3, 0,  2])

ip   = u.dot(v)                       # inner product
norm_u = sqrt(u.dot(u))
norm_v = sqrt(v.dot(v))
theta = acos(ip / (norm_u * norm_v))

print(f"⟨u, v⟩ = {ip}")
print(f"‖v‖    = {norm_v}")
print(f"θ      = {theta} rad  ≈ {float(theta.evalf()):.4f} rad")
```

### linear_combination (0.0s)

## Linear Combination

**Definition.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$, and let $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\} \subset V$ be a finite collection of vectors. A *linear combination* of these vectors is any expression of the form

$$\mathbf{w} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i\mathbf{v}_i,$$

where the *coefficients* $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{F}$ are scalars chosen freely. The resulting vector $\mathbf{w}$ is said to be *expressible as* a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$. When $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{R}$ this construction captures the geometric idea of scaling and adding directed arrows in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

**Worked Example.** Let $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0, 2)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1, -1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Set $c_1 = 3$ and $c_2 = -2$. Then

$$\mathbf{w} = 3(1,0,2) + (-2)(0,1,-1) = (3,0,6) + (0,-2,2) = (3,-2,8).$$

Now ask whether $\mathbf{u} = (3, -2, 5)$ is also a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$. Solving $c_1(1,0,2) + c_2(0,1,-1) = (3,-2,5)$ yields the system $c_1 = 3$, $c_2 = -2$, and $2c_1 - c_2 = 5$, i.e.\ $8 \neq 5$. The system is inconsistent, so $\mathbf{u}$ is *not* a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$.

**Key Theorem** *(Characterisation via span)*. The set of *all* linear combinations of $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ forms a subspace of $V$, called the *span*:

$$\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \left\{\, \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i\mathbf{v}_i \;\middle|\; c_i \in \mathbb{F} \,\right\}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Closure under addition: $(c_i\mathbf{v}_i) + (d_i\mathbf{v}_i) = (c_i+d_i)\mathbf{v}_i$, which remains a linear combination. Closure under scalar multiplication is immediate. The zero vector arises by setting all $c_i = 0$. Hence all three subspace axioms hold. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import Matrix, symbols

c1, c2 = symbols('c1 c2')
v1 = Matrix([1, 0, 2])
v2 = Matrix([0, 1, -1])
u  = Matrix([3, -2, 5])

A   = Matrix([[1, 0], [0, 1], [2, -1]])   # columns are v1, v2
sol = A.solve_least_squares(u)            # least-squares attempt
residual = A * sol - u
print("Residual:", residual)              # non-zero ⟹ u not in span{v1,v2}
```

### orthogonal_vectors (0.0s)

## Orthogonal Vectors

**Definition.** Two vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are *orthogonal* if their dot product is zero:

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i = 0.$$

Geometrically, orthogonality generalises the notion of perpendicularity from the plane to arbitrary dimensions. When $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$, the angle $\theta$ between the vectors satisfies $\cos\theta = 0$, giving $\theta = 90^\circ$. A set of vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ is called an *orthogonal set* if every pair is mutually orthogonal; if each vector additionally has unit length, the set is *orthonormal*.

**Worked Example.** Let $\mathbf{u} = (3, -1, 2)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (1, 5, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Compute:

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (3)(1) + (-1)(5) + (2)(1) = 3 - 5 + 2 = 0.$$

Since the dot product is zero, $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal. To normalise $\mathbf{u}$:

$$\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{9 + 1 + 4} = \sqrt{14}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{u}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{14}}(3, -1, 2).$$

**Key Theorem (Pythagorean Identity).** If $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal, then:

$$\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2.$$

*Proof sketch.* Expand $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2 = (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$. Because $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$, the cross-term vanishes and the identity follows. $\square$

This result extends the classical Pythagorean theorem to $n$ dimensions and underpins the theory of orthogonal projections used throughout numerical analysis and signal processing.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import Matrix, sqrt, Rational

u = Matrix([3, -1, 2])
v = Matrix([1,  5, 1])

dot_product = u.dot(v)                     # should be 0
norm_u      = sqrt(u.dot(u))
u_hat       = u / norm_u

print("u · v =", dot_product)             # 0
print("‖u‖  =", norm_u)                   # sqrt(14)
print("û    =", u_hat.T)                  # [3/√14, -1/√14, 2/√14]

# Verify Pythagorean identity
lhs = (u + v).dot(u + v)
rhs = u.dot(u) + v.dot(v)
print("Pythagorean identity holds:", lhs == rhs)   # True
```

Running this cell confirms the orthogonality check and the Pythagorean identity numerically, providing a template for testing orthogonality in larger vector spaces.

### relation_of_linear_dependence (0.0s)

## Relation Of Linear Dependence

**Definition.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$, and let $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ be a finite subset of $V$. The set $S$ is said to satisfy a *relation of linear dependence* if there exist scalars $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{F}$, not all zero, such that

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}.$$

The equation itself — together with the specific coefficients $(c_1, \ldots, c_k)$ — is called a **relation of linear dependence** among the vectors. When no such non-trivial combination exists (i.e., the only solution is $c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0$), the set is *linearly independent*.

**Worked Example.** Consider $\mathbb{R}^3$ and the three vectors

$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\5\end{pmatrix}.$$

We seek scalars satisfying $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + c_3\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}$. Row-reducing the augmented matrix yields $c_1 = 2c_3$, $c_2 = -c_3$ for any free parameter $c_3$. Setting $c_3 = 1$ gives the relation $2\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2 - \mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}$, confirming that $\mathbf{v}_3 = 2\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2$ and the set is linearly dependent.

**Key Theorem.** A set $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is linearly dependent if and only if at least one vector in the set lies in the span of the remaining vectors. Equivalently, the matrix $A = [\mathbf{v}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \mathbf{v}_k]$ has a non-trivial null space, i.e., $\operatorname{rank}(A) < k$.

*Proof sketch.* If a relation $\sum c_i\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ holds with some $c_j \neq 0$, isolate $\mathbf{v}_j = -c_j^{-1}\sum_{i \neq j} c_i\mathbf{v}_i$, placing it in $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_i : i \neq j\}$. The converse is immediate by negating that expression. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import Matrix
A = Matrix([[1, 0, 2],
            [0, 1,-1],
            [2,-1, 5]])
print(A.nullspace())   # non-empty ⟹ linearly dependent
```
Running this cell returns one null vector, confirming the relation found above and illustrating how `nullspace()` mechanically detects linear dependence.

### linear_independence (0.0s)

## Linear Independence

**Definition.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$, and let $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\} \subset V$. The set $S$ is called *linearly independent* if the only solution to the equation

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$$

is the *trivial solution* $c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0$, where each $c_i \in \mathbb{F}$. If a non-trivial solution exists — that is, at least one $c_i \neq 0$ — then $S$ is called *linearly dependent*. Informally, a set is linearly independent when no vector in it can be expressed as a linear combination of the others.

**Worked Example.** Consider the vectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0, 2)$, $\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1, -1)$, and $\mathbf{v}_3 = (2, -1, 5)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. To test independence, form the system $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + c_3\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}$, which gives:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{0}.$$

Row-reducing the coefficient matrix yields a row of zeros, revealing that $c_3$ is free. Setting $c_3 = 1$ gives $c_1 = -2$, $c_2 = 1$, confirming $-2\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2 + \mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}$. The set is therefore *linearly dependent*.

**Key Theorem.** A set $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is linearly independent if and only if the matrix $A = [\mathbf{v}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \mathbf{v}_k]$ has rank $k$ — equivalently, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution. As a corollary, any set of $k > n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ must be linearly dependent, since $A$ is tall and cannot have $k$ pivot columns.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**


### orthogonal_set (0.0s)

## Orthogonal Set

**Definition.** Let $V$ be an inner product space over $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$) with inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$. A set of vectors $S = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n\} \subset V$ is called an *orthogonal set* if every pair of distinct vectors in $S$ is orthogonal, that is,

$$\langle v_i, v_j \rangle = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \neq j.$$

If, additionally, each vector has unit norm ($\|v_i\| = 1$ for all $i$), the set is called *orthonormal*. Every orthogonal set of nonzero vectors is linearly independent, making orthogonal sets particularly powerful as bases.

---

**Worked Example.** Consider $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the standard dot product. Verify that $S = \{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$, where

$$v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \quad v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \quad v_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix},$$

forms an orthogonal set. Computing each inner product:

$$\langle v_1, v_2 \rangle = (1)(1)+(1)(-1)+(0)(0) = 0,$$
$$\langle v_1, v_3 \rangle = (1)(0)+(1)(0)+(0)(1) = 0,$$
$$\langle v_2, v_3 \rangle = (1)(0)+(-1)(0)+(0)(1) = 0.$$

All pairwise inner products vanish, so $S$ is orthogonal. To normalize, divide each vector by its norm: $\|v_1\| = \|v_2\| = \sqrt{2}$ and $\|v_3\| = 1$, yielding the orthonormal set $\left\{\tfrac{v_1}{\sqrt{2}},\, \tfrac{v_2}{\sqrt{2}},\, v_3\right\}$.

---

**Key Theorem (Orthogonal Expansion).** Let $\{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ be an orthogonal basis for a subspace $W \subseteq V$. For any $w \in W$,

$$w = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\langle w, v_i \rangle}{\langle v_i, v_i \rangle}\, v_i.$$

*Proof sketch.* Write $w = \sum c_i v_i$. Taking the inner product of both sides with $v_k$ and using orthogonality eliminates all terms $i \neq k$, giving $c_k = \langle w, v_k \rangle / \langle v_k, v_k \rangle$. $\square$

This formula bypasses Gaussian elimination entirely, reducing coordinate extraction to scalar products.

### gram_schmidt_procedure (0.0s)

## Gram Schmidt Procedure

**Definition.** Let $V$ be an inner product space and let $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ be a linearly independent set of vectors in $V$. The *Gram–Schmidt procedure* is an algorithm that produces an orthogonal set $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_n\}$ spanning the same subspace. Explicitly, define

$$\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1,$$

and for $k = 2, 3, \ldots, n$,

$$\mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{v}_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \frac{\langle \mathbf{v}_k, \mathbf{u}_j \rangle}{\langle \mathbf{u}_j, \mathbf{u}_j \rangle}\,\mathbf{u}_j.$$

Each subtracted term is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{v}_k$ onto $\mathbf{u}_j$; removing these projections strips away every component of $\mathbf{v}_k$ that lies along a previously constructed basis vector. Normalising each $\mathbf{u}_k$ yields an *orthonormal* basis, commonly denoted $\{\mathbf{e}_k\}$.

**Worked example.** Let $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 1, 0)^\top$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1, 0, 1)^\top$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the standard inner product.

Set $\mathbf{u}_1 = (1, 1, 0)^\top$. Then

$$\frac{\langle \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{u}_1 \rangle}{\langle \mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_1 \rangle} = \frac{1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0}{1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2} = \frac{1}{2},$$

so

$$\mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1/2\\-1/2\\1\end{pmatrix}.$$

One verifies directly that $\langle \mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2 \rangle = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} + 0 = 0$. $\checkmark$

**Key theorem.** If $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ is linearly independent, the Gram–Schmidt procedure terminates in exactly $n$ steps and produces a basis $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_n\}$ satisfying $\langle \mathbf{u}_i, \mathbf{u}_j \rangle = 0$ for all $i \neq j$, with $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_k\} = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ for every $k$. This is the existence half of the QR decomposition: writing $Q = [\mathbf{e}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \mathbf{e}_n]$ and $R_{jk} = \langle \mathbf{v}_k, \mathbf{e}_j \rangle$ gives $A = QR$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $R$ upper triangular.

```python
from sympy import Matrix, GramSchmidt, pprint

for i, e in enumerate(ortho, 1):
    pprint(f"e_{i} ="); pprint(e)
```
