### sequence (0.0s)

## Sequence

**Definition.** A *sequence* is a function $a : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ (or more generally, into any set $S$). We write its values as an ordered list $(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots)$, or in compact notation $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$. The integer $n$ is called the *index* and $a_n$ the $n$-th *term*. A sequence is *finite* if the domain is $\{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ for some $N \in \mathbb{N}$, and *infinite* otherwise. Two sequences are equal if and only if $a_n = b_n$ for every index $n$ in their shared domain.

**Worked Example.** Consider the sequence defined by $a_n = \dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$. Listing the first five terms:

$$a_1 = 1,\quad a_2 = -\tfrac{1}{2},\quad a_3 = \tfrac{1}{3},\quad a_4 = -\tfrac{1}{4},\quad a_5 = \tfrac{1}{5}.$$

The terms alternate in sign and decrease in absolute value toward zero. This is the sequence of partial harmonic terms with alternating sign, and it plays a central role in the theory of conditionally convergent series.

**Key Theorem (Squeeze Theorem for Sequences).** Let $\{a_n\}$, $\{b_n\}$, $\{c_n\}$ be sequences satisfying $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. If

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L = \lim_{n \to \infty} c_n,$$

then $\lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = L$ as well.

*Proof sketch.* Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N$ large enough that $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ and $|c_n - L| < \varepsilon$ for all $n > N$. The sandwich inequality $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ then forces $L - \varepsilon < b_n < L + \varepsilon$, i.e., $|b_n - L| < \varepsilon$. $\square$

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import *

n = symbols('n', positive=True, integer=True)

a = ((-1)**(n + 1)) / n

# Compute and display first ten terms
terms = [a.subs(n, k) for k in range(1, 11)]
print("Terms:", terms)

# Verify the limit
print("Limit:", limit(a, n, oo))
```

Running this cell confirms that each term matches the pattern derived above and that $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$, consistent with the Squeeze Theorem applied with $c_n = 1/n$ and $a_n = -1/n$.

### time_series (0.0s)

## Time Series

**Definition.** A *time series* is a sequence of observations $\{x_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ indexed by discrete time $t \in \mathbb{Z}$, where each $x_t$ is a realization of a random variable $X_t$. The collection $\{X_t\}$ is called a *stochastic process*. A time series is said to be *weakly stationary* (or *covariance-stationary*) if its mean and autocovariance structure are invariant under time shifts:

$$\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu \quad \text{for all } t,$$
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \gamma(k) \quad \text{for all } t, k,$$

where $\gamma(k)$ is called the *autocovariance function* at lag $k$. The normalized version, $\rho(k) = \gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$, is the *autocorrelation function* (ACF). These two quantities — $\gamma$ and $\rho$ — are the primary tools for characterizing the internal dependence structure of a stationary series.

**Worked Example.** Consider daily closing prices of a stock over $T = 5$ days: $x_1 = 100,\ x_2 = 102,\ x_3 = 101,\ x_4 = 105,\ x_5 = 103$. Compute the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{t=1}^{5} x_t = 102.2$. The lag-1 sample autocovariance is

$$\hat{\gamma}(1) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=2}^{T}(x_t - \bar{x})(x_{t-1} - \bar{x}) = \frac{1}{5}\bigl[(-1.2)(-2.2) + (-1.2)(-0.2) + (2.8)(-1.2) + (0.8)(2.8)\bigr] = 0.56.$$

A positive $\hat{\gamma}(1)$ indicates that consecutive observations tend to move together — a feature common in financial data.

**Key Theorem (Properties of the ACF).** For any weakly stationary process $\{X_t\}$, the autocorrelation function satisfies:

$$\rho(0) = 1, \qquad \rho(-k) = \rho(k) \quad \text{for all } k \in \mathbb{Z}, \qquad |\rho(k)| \leq 1.$$

Symmetry follows directly from the definition of covariance; the bound $|\rho(k)| \leq 1$ is a consequence of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $X_t$ and $X_{t+k}$. Together, these constraints rule out spurious correlation estimates outside $[-1, 1]$ and allow the ACF to be plotted on a standardized scale — the foundation of all graphical stationarity diagnostics.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
import sympy as sp

k = sp.Symbol('k', integer=True)
phi, sigma2 = sp.symbols('phi sigma2', positive=True)

# AR(1) autocovariance: gamma(k) = sigma^2 * phi^|k| / (1 - phi^2)
gamma = sigma2 * phi**sp.Abs(k) / (1 - phi**2)
rho   = sp.simplify(gamma / gamma.subs(k, 0))
sp.pprint(rho)  # yields phi^|k|; verify rho(0)=1 and symmetry
```

### trend (0.0s)

## Trend

**Definition.** Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{n}$ be a time series observed at equally spaced time points $t = 1, 2, \ldots, n$. The *trend* of the series is the smooth, long-run component $T_t$ such that

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $S_t$ denotes any periodic (seasonal) component and $\varepsilon_t$ is zero-mean random noise. Informally, the trend captures the direction and rate of sustained change in the mean level of the series, abstracting away short-term fluctuations. A trend is called *linear* if $T_t = \alpha + \beta t$ for constants $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, and *nonlinear* otherwise.

**Worked Example.** Suppose annual global mean temperature anomalies (in °C relative to a 1951–1980 baseline) are modelled as $y_t = 0.02\,t - 0.10 + \varepsilon_t$ for $t = 1, \ldots, 50$. The trend component is $T_t = 0.02\,t - 0.10$, indicating a sustained warming of $0.02$ °C per year. At $t = 25$ the predicted mean anomaly is $T_{25} = 0.02(25) - 0.10 = 0.40$ °C. The slope $\beta = 0.02$ is the *trend rate*; its sign determines whether the series is *upward* ($\beta > 0$) or *downward* ($\beta < 0$) trending.

**Key Theorem (Ordinary Least-Squares Trend Estimation).** Given observations $\{(t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{n}$, the OLS estimators of the linear trend parameters are

$$\hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(t - \bar{t})(y_t - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(t - \bar{t})^2}, \qquad \hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}\,\bar{t},$$

where $\bar{t} = (n+1)/2$ and $\bar{y} = n^{-1}\sum y_t$. Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions ($\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t]=0$, constant variance, no autocorrelation), $\hat{\alpha}$ and $\hat{\beta}$ are the Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUE) of the true trend parameters.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import numpy as np
from sympy import symbols, summation, simplify, Rational

t, n = symbols('t n', positive=True, integer=True)

# Symbolic numerator of beta-hat for uniform time index
y_t = 2*t - 1          # example: y_t = 2t - 1 (exact linear series)
t_bar = (n + 1) / 2
numerator   = summation((t - t_bar) * (y_t - summation(y_t, (t, 1, n)) / n), (t, 1, n))
denominator = summation((t - t_bar)**2, (t, 1, n))
beta_hat = simplify(numerator / denominator)
print("Estimated slope:", beta_hat)   # Expected: 2
```

Running this cell confirms that for a perfectly linear series the OLS formula recovers the true slope exactly, providing an algebraic sanity check before applying the estimator to noisy empirical data.

### differencing (0.0s)

## Differencing

**Definition.** Let $\{x_t\}_{t=0}^{N}$ be a sequence of real numbers indexed by a discrete variable $t$. The *first-order difference operator* $\Delta$ is defined by

$$\Delta x_t = x_{t+1} - x_t, \qquad t = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1.$$

Applying $\Delta$ repeatedly yields higher-order differences. The *$k$-th order difference* is defined recursively as $\Delta^k x_t = \Delta\!\left(\Delta^{k-1} x_t\right)$, with $\Delta^0 x_t = x_t$. Differencing is the discrete analogue of differentiation: whereas $\frac{d}{dt}f(t)$ measures an instantaneous rate of change, $\Delta x_t$ measures the change over one unit step. In time-series analysis, differencing is the primary tool for removing trends and achieving *stationarity* — a property requiring that the statistical moments of a sequence do not depend on $t$.

**Worked example.** Consider the quadratic sequence $x_t = t^2$ for $t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$.

| $t$ | $x_t$ | $\Delta x_t$ | $\Delta^2 x_t$ |
|-----|--------|--------------|----------------|
| 0   | 0      | 1            | 2              |
| 1   | 1      | 3            | 2              |
| 2   | 4      | 5            | 2              |
| 3   | 9      | 7            | —              |
| 4   | 16     | —            | —              |

The first-order differences $\Delta x_t = 2t + 1$ are linear in $t$, while the second-order differences $\Delta^2 x_t = 2$ are constant. This mirrors the calculus result $\frac{d^2}{dt^2}(t^2) = 2$.

**Theorem (Annihilation of polynomials).** If $x_t$ is a polynomial of degree $k$ in $t$, then $\Delta^k x_t$ is a nonzero constant and $\Delta^{k+1} x_t = 0$ for all valid $t$. Consequently, differencing a sequence $d$ times removes any polynomial trend of degree at most $d$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $x_t = \sum_{j=0}^{k} a_j t^j$. One verifies by induction that $\Delta(t^j)$ is a polynomial of degree $j-1$, so each application of $\Delta$ reduces the leading degree by exactly one. After $k$ applications the result is the constant $k!\,a_k$; one further application yields zero. $\square$

**Lab cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, factor, simplify

t = symbols('t', integer=True)
x = t**2

delta1 = x.subs(t, t + 1) - x          # first difference
delta2 = delta1.subs(t, t + 1) - delta1  # second difference

print("Δx_t  =", simplify(delta1))   # 2t + 1
print("Δ²x_t =", simplify(delta2))   # 2
```

### error (0.0s)

## Error

**Definition.** Let $x^*$ denote the exact value of a quantity and $\tilde{x}$ its computed or measured approximation. The *absolute error* is

$$
e_{\text{abs}} = |x^* - \tilde{x}|,
$$

and the *relative error*, defined when $x^* \neq 0$, is

$$
e_{\text{rel}} = \frac{|x^* - \tilde{x}|}{|x^*|}.
$$

Relative error is dimensionless and therefore the preferred measure when comparing approximations across different scales. A related concept is *significant digits*: $\tilde{x}$ agrees with $x^*$ to $d$ significant digits when $e_{\text{rel}} < 5 \times 10^{-d}$.

Error arises from two principal sources. *Truncation error* results from replacing an infinite mathematical process (a series, an integral, a limit) with a finite one. *Round-off error* results from representing real numbers in finite-precision floating-point arithmetic. In practice these two sources interact: reducing truncation error by taking more steps can amplify round-off error through accumulated floating-point operations.

**Worked Example.** Approximate $e^{0.1}$ using the two-term Taylor series $1 + x$, evaluated at $x = 0.1$.

- Approximation: $\tilde{x} = 1.1$
- Exact value: $x^* = e^{0.1} \approx 1.10517091\ldots$
- Absolute error: $e_{\text{abs}} = |1.10517091 - 1.1| \approx 5.17 \times 10^{-3}$
- Relative error: $e_{\text{rel}} = \dfrac{5.17 \times 10^{-3}}{1.10517} \approx 4.68 \times 10^{-3}$

Adding the next term $\tfrac{x^2}{2} = 0.005$ gives $\tilde{x} = 1.105$, reducing the relative error to approximately $1.54 \times 10^{-4}$, roughly a 30-fold improvement. Each additional term decreases the truncation error by a factor of approximately $x/n$.

**Theorem (Taylor Remainder Bound).** If $f$ is $(n+1)$ times continuously differentiable on an interval containing $0$ and $x$, then the truncation error of the $n$-term Taylor approximation satisfies

$$
\left|f(x) - \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!} x^k\right| \leq \frac{M}{(n+1)!} |x|^{n+1},
$$

where $M = \max_{\xi \in [0,x]} |f^{(n+1)}(\xi)|$.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import exp, series, Abs, symbols, N

x = symbols('x')
f = exp(x)
x0 = 0.1

for n in range(1, 5):
    approx = series(f, x, 0, n).removeO().subs(x, x0)
    exact  = N(f.subs(x, x0), 15)
    rel_err = Abs(exact - approx) / Abs(exact)
    print(f"n={n}  approx={float(approx):.8f}  rel_err={float(rel_err):.2e}")
```

### lag (0.0s)

## Lag

**Definition.** Let $\{x_t\}$ be a discrete-time sequence indexed by integers $t \in \mathbb{Z}$. The *lag-$k$ operator* $L^k$ is defined by

$$L^k x_t = x_{t-k}, \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

When $k > 0$ the operator shifts the sequence backward in time (a *delay*); when $k < 0$ it shifts forward (a *lead*). The special case $L^1$ is called the *backshift operator* and is frequently denoted $B$ in the time-series literature. Lag is a purely algebraic construction: it does not depend on stationarity or any probabilistic assumptions, making it well-defined for arbitrary real- or complex-valued sequences.

**Worked Example.** Consider the weekly sales figures $x = (3, 7, 2, 9, 5)$ for $t = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$. Applying the lag-2 operator yields

$$L^2 x_t = x_{t-2},$$

so the lagged series is $(\text{undefined}, \text{undefined}, 3, 7, 2)$ for $t = 1, \ldots, 5$. In practice one pads the first $k$ positions with `NaN` or discards them, depending on the application. The *first difference* of the original series is $\Delta x_t = x_t - x_{t-1} = (1 - L)x_t$, demonstrating that lag operators compose with ordinary polynomial algebra: $(1 - L)$ is a degree-one *lag polynomial*.

**Key Theorem (Lag Polynomial Factorisation).** Let $\Phi(L) = 1 - \phi_1 L - \cdots - \phi_p L^p$ be a lag polynomial with roots $z_1, \ldots, z_p$ (solutions to $\Phi(z) = 0$). The autoregressive process $\Phi(L)x_t = \varepsilon_t$ is *stationary* if and only if every root satisfies $|z_i| > 1$, i.e., all roots lie strictly outside the unit circle in the complex plane. This geometric condition on the roots underpins the stability analysis of AR, ARMA, and state-space models alike.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, factor, Rational

L, z = symbols('L z')

# Lag polynomial for AR(2): 1 - 0.5L - 0.25L^2
Phi = 1 - Rational(1,2)*L - Rational(1,4)*L**2
print("Phi(L) =", Phi)

# Check roots (substitute z for L, solve Phi(z)=0)
from sympy import solve
roots = solve(Phi.subs(L, z), z)
print("Roots:", roots)
print("All outside unit circle?", all(abs(complex(r)) > 1 for r in roots))
```

### autoregressive_model (0.0s)

## Autoregressive Model

**Definition.** An *autoregressive model of order $p$*, denoted $\mathrm{AR}(p)$, expresses a time series $\{X_t\}$ as a linear combination of its own $p$ most recent values plus a white-noise innovation:

$$X_t = c + \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \phi_2 X_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p X_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $c \in \mathbb{R}$ is a constant intercept, $\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_p \in \mathbb{R}$ are the *autoregressive coefficients*, and $\varepsilon_t \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ is the innovation sequence. The model is called *autoregressive* because the response variable is regressed on its own past realizations.

**Worked Example.** Consider the $\mathrm{AR}(1)$ process $X_t = 0.8\, X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ with $\sigma = 1$ and $X_0 = 0$. Drawing $\varepsilon_1 = 1.2$, $\varepsilon_2 = -0.5$, $\varepsilon_3 = 0.9$, the first three values are:

$$X_1 = 0.8(0) + 1.2 = 1.2,$$
$$X_2 = 0.8(1.2) + (-0.5) = 0.46,$$
$$X_3 = 0.8(0.46) + 0.9 = 1.268.$$

Each observation is anchored to the previous one, creating temporal dependence that decays geometrically as the lag grows.

**Key Theorem (Stationarity Condition).** The $\mathrm{AR}(p)$ process is *weakly stationary* if and only if all roots of the characteristic polynomial

$$\Phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \phi_2 z^2 - \cdots - \phi_p z^p$$

lie strictly outside the complex unit disk, i.e., $\Phi(z) \neq 0$ for all $|z| \leq 1$.

*Proof sketch.* Solving the recurrence forward shows that each $X_t$ can be written as a weighted sum of past innovations $\varepsilon_{t-j}$. Those weights are proportional to powers of $1/z_k$, where $z_k$ are the roots of $\Phi(z)$. When every $|z_k| > 1$, the weights decay geometrically and the sum converges, yielding a time-invariant mean and autocovariance function—the two conditions that define weak stationarity.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, solve, Rational

z = symbols('z')
phi1 = Rational(4, 5)          # AR(1) coefficient 0.8
char_poly = 1 - phi1 * z
roots = solve(char_poly, z)
print("Characteristic root:", roots)
print("Stationary?", all(abs(complex(r)) > 1 for r in roots))
```

Running this cell confirms the single root at $z = 1.25$, which lies outside the unit disk, verifying that the process $X_t = 0.8\,X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ is stationary.

### integrative_component (0.0s)

## Integrative Component

### Definition

In the theory of linear dynamical systems and control, the **integrative component** (also called the *integral action* or *I-term*) is the feedback element whose output at time $t$ is proportional to the accumulated history of the error signal. Given an error function $e : [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$, the integrative component produces the signal

$$
u_I(t) = K_I \int_0^t e(\tau)\, d\tau,
$$

where $K_I \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is the *integral gain*. Unlike proportional action, which responds only to the instantaneous error, the integrative component retains memory of all past deviations and corrects for persistent bias that proportional control alone cannot eliminate. In the Laplace domain this relationship becomes

$$
U_I(s) = \frac{K_I}{s}\, E(s),
$$

so the integrative component introduces a pole at the origin, endowing the closed-loop system with a free integrator and guaranteeing zero steady-state error for step inputs.

### Worked Example

Suppose a heating system maintains a target temperature of $T^* = 70\,^\circ\text{C}$. The measured temperature follows $T(t) = 70 - 5e^{-t}$, so the error is $e(t) = T^* - T(t) = 5e^{-t}$. With $K_I = 2$, the integrative correction signal is

$$
u_I(t) = 2\int_0^t 5e^{-\tau}\,d\tau = 10\bigl(1 - e^{-t}\bigr).
$$

As $t \to \infty$, $u_I \to 10$, providing a steady correction that exactly cancels the persistent offset — something a pure proportional controller cannot achieve when its gain is finite.

### Key Theorem: Internal Model Principle (Steady-State Form)

**Theorem.** A feedback controller achieves zero steady-state error to a step disturbance if and only if the open-loop transfer function contains at least one free integrator (a pole at $s = 0$).

*Proof sketch.* By the Final Value Theorem, $\lim_{t\to\infty} e(t) = \lim_{s\to 0} s\, E(s)$. For a unit step, $E(s) = 1/[s(1 + G(s)H(s))]$. The limit equals zero precisely when $G(s)H(s) \to \infty$ as $s \to 0$, which holds if and only if $G$ or $H$ contains the factor $1/s$. $\square$

### Lab Cell

```python
import sympy as sp

t, s, K_I = sp.symbols('t s K_I', positive=True)

# Error signal and integrative output (time domain)
e   = 5 * sp.exp(-t)
u_I = K_I * sp.integrate(e, (t, 0, t))
print("u_I(t) =", sp.simplify(u_I))

# Transfer function in Laplace domain
E_s  = sp.laplace_transform(e, t, s, noconds=True)
U_Is = (K_I / s) * E_s
print("U_I(s) =", sp.simplify(U_Is))

# Steady-state value via Final Value Theorem
fvt = sp.limit(s * U_Is, s, 0)
print("Steady-state u_I =", fvt)
```

Running this cell with $K_I = 2$ reproduces $u_I(t) = 10(1 - e^{-t})$ and confirms the steady-state value of $10$, illustrating how symbolic computation makes the Internal Model Principle concrete and verifiable.

### moving_average_model (0.0s)

## Moving Average Model

**Definition.** A *moving average model of order $q$*, denoted $\mathrm{MA}(q)$, is a discrete-time stochastic process $\{X_t\}$ defined by

$$X_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2\varepsilon_{t-2} + \cdots + \theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q},$$

where $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ is the process mean, $\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_q \in \mathbb{R}$ are the *moving average coefficients*, and $\{\varepsilon_t\}$ is a white-noise sequence with variance $\sigma^2$ (see the preceding section for the formal definition). Unlike an autoregressive model, $X_t$ depends directly on past *shocks* rather than past *observations*.

**Worked Example.** Consider the $\mathrm{MA}(1)$ process $X_t = \varepsilon_t + 0.8\,\varepsilon_{t-1}$ with $\sigma^2 = 1$. The mean is $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = 0$. The variance is

$$\mathrm{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2(1 + \theta_1^2) = 1 + 0.64 = 1.64.$$

The autocovariance at lag $k$ satisfies

$$\gamma(k) = \begin{cases} \sigma^2(1 + \theta_1^2) & k = 0, \\ \sigma^2\theta_1 & k = \pm 1, \\ 0 & |k| > 1. \end{cases}$$

Hence the autocorrelation at lag 1 is $\rho(1) = \gamma(1)/\gamma(0) = 0.8/1.64 \approx 0.488$, and all higher-lag autocorrelations are exactly zero — a distinctive *cutoff* property that distinguishes MA processes from AR processes.

**Key Theorem (Finite Memory).** An $\mathrm{MA}(q)$ process is always weakly stationary, and its autocorrelation function satisfies $\rho(k) = 0$ for all $|k| > q$. This cutoff at lag $q$ provides the primary diagnostic for identifying the order of a moving average model from empirical autocorrelation plots.

*Proof sketch.* Since $X_t$ is a finite linear combination of white-noise terms, both $\mathbb{E}[X_t]$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_t)$ are constants independent of $t$. For $|k| > q$, the sets $\{\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-1}, \ldots, \varepsilon_{t-q}\}$ and $\{\varepsilon_{t-k}, \ldots, \varepsilon_{t-k-q}\}$ are disjoint, so uncorrelated white-noise terms yield $\gamma(k) = 0$. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational, simplify

theta, sigma2 = symbols('theta sigma2', positive=True)

var_X   = sigma2 * (1 + theta**2)
gamma_1 = sigma2 * theta
rho_1   = simplify(gamma_1 / var_X)

print("Var(X_t)  =", var_X)
print("rho(1)    =", rho_1)

# Evaluate at theta=0.8, sigma2=1
print("rho(1)|_{θ=0.8} =", float(rho_1.subs([(theta, Rational(4,5)), (sigma2, 1)])))
```

Running this cell confirms $\rho(1) = \theta/(1+\theta^2)$ and recovers the numerical value $\approx 0.488$ obtained above.

### arima (0.0s)

## Arima

**Definition.** Assume the reader is already familiar with the backshift operator $B$, the autoregressive polynomial $\phi(B) = 1 - \phi_1 B - \cdots - \phi_p B^p$, the moving-average polynomial $\theta(B) = 1 + \theta_1 B + \cdots + \theta_q B^q$, and white noise $\varepsilon_t \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. The single new primitive introduced here is the **differencing operator**: for a non-negative integer $d$, define $\nabla^d = (1-B)^d$, so $\nabla^1 X_t = X_t - X_{t-1}$ and $\nabla^2 X_t = X_t - 2X_{t-1} + X_{t-2}$. Setting $Y_t = \nabla^d X_t$ and requiring $Y_t$ to be weakly stationary yields the *AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average* model $\text{ARIMA}(p,d,q)$:

$$
\phi(B)\,\nabla^d X_t = \theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t.
$$

The integer $d \geq 0$ is the *degree of integration*: applying $\nabla^d$ removes deterministic or stochastic trends and renders the transformed series $Y_t$ stationary. When $d=0$ the model reduces to the familiar $\text{ARMA}(p,q)$.

**Worked Example.** Monthly airline passenger counts (Box–Jenkins data) exhibit exponential growth, so a log-transform is applied first to stabilise variance. The autocorrelation function of the log series decays slowly and linearly — a hallmark of a unit root — so one difference ($d=1$) is taken. For the differenced series the partial ACF cuts off after lag 1 and the ACF cuts off after lag 1, suggesting $p=1$, $q=1$. The fitted $\text{ARIMA}(1,1,1)$ model is

$$
(1 - \hat\phi_1 B)(1-B)\,X_t = (1 + \hat\theta_1 B)\,\varepsilon_t,
$$

with estimated coefficients $\hat\phi_1 \approx 0.60$ and $\hat\theta_1 \approx -0.38$. Residuals pass the Ljung–Box portmanteau test at the 5 % level, confirming adequate fit.

**Key Theorem (Wold Decomposition).** Every zero-mean covariance-stationary process $\{Y_t\}$ has a representation $Y_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j}$ with $\psi_0 = 1$ and $\sum_j \psi_j^2 < \infty$. An invertible $\text{ARMA}(p,q)$ process is a finite-parameter approximation to this infinite moving average, which justifies using ARIMA — after differencing removes the trend — as a parsimonious forecasting model.

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA
from sympy import symbols, expand

# Illustrate the differencing operator symbolically
B = symbols('B')
nabla = 1 - B
```
