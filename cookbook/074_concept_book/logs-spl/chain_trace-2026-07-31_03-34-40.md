### sequence (0.2s)

## Sequence

Imagine you're saving money for a bicycle. Week one you have $5. Week two, $10. Week three, $15. You don't need a formula to see what's happening — your brain already feels the pattern. That feeling *is* the concept of a sequence.

A **sequence** is simply a list of numbers written in a definite order, where the position of each number matters. The first number isn't interchangeable with the second; order is everything. Think of your savings as a story: each chapter reveals the next number, and skipping a chapter breaks the narrative.

We write a sequence using subscripts to name each term by its position:

$$a_1, \; a_2, \; a_3, \; \ldots, \; a_n, \; \ldots$$

Here $a_n$ is the $n$-th term — the value at step $n$. In the savings story, $a_n = 5n$, so $a_1 = 5$, $a_2 = 10$, and so on. The rule $a_n = 5n$ is called the **general term** or **closed form** — a single expression that generates any term directly without listing all the ones before it.

Not every sequence grows so neatly. The sequence $1, -1, 1, -1, \ldots$ alternates forever and never settles. The sequence $1, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \ldots$ shrinks toward zero but never quite arrives. Each is a valid sequence — a list in order — and studying where they *head* is the next great question.

**Now you try.** Consider the sequence defined by $a_n = n^2$. Write out the first five terms. Does the gap between consecutive terms grow, shrink, or stay constant? What does that tell you about how fast the sequence is accelerating?

### time_series (0.2s)

## Time Series

Imagine you check your city's temperature every morning at 8 a.m. and write it down. After a year, you have 365 numbers. But here's what makes this interesting: the *order* matters. Tuesday's reading depends on Monday's. Winter readings cluster near freezing; summer readings cluster near scorching. If you shuffled those 365 numbers into a random order, you'd lose something real — the shape of the seasons, the memory of yesterday's cold front.

That's the heart of a time series: a sequence of measurements where *position in time* is as important as the value itself.

**Why does order matter?** Because the world has inertia. Prices don't jump randomly from $5 to $500 overnight. Your heart rate right now is close to what it was one second ago. Time-ordered data carries *autocorrelation* — the technical name for "today resembles yesterday."

Formally, a time series is a collection of observations indexed by time:

$$x_1,\ x_2,\ x_3,\ \ldots,\ x_T$$

where $x_t$ is the value at time step $t$. The gap between steps (one second, one day, one year) is called the **sampling interval**. Keep it consistent, or the math gets messy.

Two patterns to look for immediately:

- **Trend** — does the overall level drift upward or downward over time?
- **Seasonality** — does a pattern repeat on a fixed cycle (daily, weekly, yearly)?

A city's electricity demand has both: a long-term rise as the population grows, and a daily spike every evening when people come home.

**Now you try.** Take your phone's step count for the last 30 days. Sketch it roughly. Can you spot a weekly rhythm — fewer steps on weekends, perhaps? That's seasonality revealing itself, hiding in plain sight.

### trend (0.2s)

## Trend

Imagine you track your morning coffee temperature every minute after pouring it. Minute one: 85°C. Minute two: 78°C. Minute three: 71°C. The exact numbers jump around depending on the room, the mug, your measurement. But something deeper is obvious — the coffee is *cooling down*. That persistent direction hiding inside the noise is the trend.

A trend is not a fact about any single data point. It is a fact about the *whole sequence* — the underlying drift that would remain if you smoothed away every accidental wiggle. Think of it as the river current beneath choppy surface waves. Waves vary; the current flows steadily in one direction.

The simplest way to formalise this intuition is to ask: **on average, how much does the measurement change each step?** That average change is the trend. If temperatures drop roughly 7°C every minute, the trend is −7°C per minute. We call this number $b$. Positive $b$ means the sequence is rising; negative means falling; zero means no overall drift. Finding the $b$ that best fits all the points at once is a matter of calculation, but the key insight is simpler than any formula: *$b$ is the trend*. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Trends appear everywhere — stock prices, global temperature, your running pace over weeks of training. Spotting one lets you separate *signal* (where things are headed) from *noise* (the random detours along the way).

**Now you try.** A plant measures 2 cm, 5 cm, 8 cm, 11 cm over four weeks. What is the trend $b$ — how many centimetres per week does it grow on average? Does anything surprise you about how steady it is?

### differencing (0.2s)

## Differencing

Imagine you are tracking the daily temperature: 12°, 15°, 21°, 30°, 42°. The numbers themselves tell you it is getting warmer, but they do not scream *how fast*. Now subtract each reading from the one that follows it: $3, 6, 9, 12$. Suddenly a pattern leaps out — the gap itself is growing by 3 each day. You did not just measure the temperature; you measured the *change in the change*. That act of subtracting neighbours is called **differencing**.

Why does this feel right? Because our brains are naturally change-detectors. You do not notice a room that has always been slightly cold; you notice the moment it starts getting colder. Differencing formalises that instinct — it shifts your focus from "where are we?" to "how fast are we moving?"

Given a sequence $a_0, a_1, a_2, \ldots$, the **first difference** is

$$\Delta a_n = a_{n+1} - a_n.$$

Applying the same operation to $\Delta a_n$ gives the **second difference**:

$$\Delta^2 a_n = \Delta a_{n+1} - \Delta a_n = a_{n+2} - 2a_{n+1} + a_n.$$

First differences detect linear trends (constant gap $\Rightarrow$ arithmetic sequence); second differences detect quadratic ones. A sequence whose second differences are all zero is exactly a straight line in disguise.

This is the discrete twin of the derivative: where calculus shrinks the step to zero, differencing keeps it at one unit and counts exactly. Both answer the same question — *how much does the output change when the input nudges forward?*

**Now you try.** Take the squares $1, 4, 9, 16, 25$. Compute the first differences, then the second differences. What do you find, and why does it make geometric sense?

### error (0.2s)

## Error

Imagine you're trying to hit a bullseye with a dart. You throw, and the dart lands two centimetres to the left. That gap — the distance between where you *aimed* and where you *landed* — is the error. It's not a failure; it's information. It tells you exactly how wrong you were, and in which direction.

This is the spirit of error in mathematics: **the difference between what you expected and what actually happened**.

When a model predicts the temperature tomorrow will be $20°C$ and it turns out to be $23°C$, the error is simply:

$$e = \hat{y} - y = 20 - 23 = -3$$

The negative sign matters — it tells you the prediction was *too low*. If we just want the size of the mistake without caring about direction, we take the absolute value: $|e| = 3$.

Often, though, we care about many predictions at once. A single error might be a fluke. So we collect them all and ask: on average, how far off am I? One popular answer is the **Mean Squared Error (MSE)**:

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} ({\hat{y}_i - y_i})^2$$

Squaring each error does two things: it makes every term positive (no cancellations), and it *punishes* big mistakes more heavily than small ones.

Error is the compass of learning — machines adjust their parameters specifically to shrink it, and scientists redesign experiments when it's too large.

**Now you try:** You guess a friend's age as $30$; they're actually $34$. What is the signed error? What is the absolute error? If you made three guesses with errors $-3, 1, 2$, what is the MSE?

### lag (0.2s)

## Lag

**Motivating story.** Imagine you're watching ocean waves roll in. Each wave looks almost identical to the one before it — just a few seconds behind. If you filmed the shoreline and then played two copies of the video simultaneously, one shifted forward by, say, 3 seconds, the two waves would line up almost perfectly. That time shift is the *lag*.

**Intuition.** The same idea lives in any sequence of measurements over time: stock prices, temperatures, your heartbeat. A *lag-$k$ version* of a sequence is simply the original sequence shifted back by $k$ steps, so each value is paired with the value that came $k$ moments *earlier*. When $k = 1$, today is paired with yesterday. When $k = 7$, today is paired with the same day last week.

This matters because many real signals echo themselves. Yesterday's temperature predicts today's. Last week's sales hint at this week's. Lag lets you compare a signal to its own past — without that time shift, you'd be comparing apples to oranges.

**Minimal formalisation.** Given a sequence $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, the **lag-$k$ shift** produces the paired sequence

$$\bigl(x_{k+1},\, x_1\bigr),\;\bigl(x_{k+2},\, x_2\bigr),\;\ldots,\;\bigl(x_n,\, x_{n-k}\bigr).$$

You lose $k$ observations from the front because there is no "yesterday" for the very first entries. The correlation between these paired values, computed across all valid pairs, is the *lag-$k$ autocorrelation* — a single number measuring how strongly the present echoes the past at distance $k$.

**Now you try.** Take the sequence $[2, 4, 6, 8, 10]$. Write out all the pairs at lag $k = 2$. Do large values on the left tend to come with large values on the right? What does that tell you?

### autoregressive_model (0.2s)

## Autoregressive Model

### The Next-Word Game

Close your eyes and finish this sentence: *"The cat sat on the…"* You said *mat*, didn't you? You didn't need the entire English language in front of you. You used what came *before* to predict what comes *next*. That everyday mental habit is the core idea behind an autoregressive model.

### Why "Auto"?

*Auto* means self. The model regresses — that is, it looks backward — on its **own previous outputs**. Each new piece is generated using everything that was already generated. It's a snake eating its own tail, productively.

Think of writing a mystery novel one sentence at a time, where each sentence you write is handed back to you as a clue for writing the next one. You never look at a blank page; you always look at your own growing story.

### The Minimal Maths

Say we want to model a sequence of values $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T$. The joint probability of the whole sequence factors as:

$$
P(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})
$$

This is just the chain rule of probability — no assumption added. An autoregressive model **parametrises** each conditional $P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$ with a neural network (often a transformer), then trains it to make that prediction as accurate as possible.

At generation time, you sample $x_1$, feed it back in, sample $x_2$, and so on — one step at a time.

### Now You Try

Pick any ordered sequence you enjoy — weather readings, chess moves, musical notes. Write down three steps of that sequence. Cover the fourth value and ask yourself: *what information from steps 1–3 actually mattered?* That intuition is exactly the conditional dependence an autoregressive model learns to capture.

### integrative_component (0.2s)

## Integrative Component

Imagine you're on a road trip. You glance at your speedometer: 60 mph *right now*. But what you really care about is how far you've actually traveled. That total on your odometer is something different — it has been built up, mile by mile, from every speed reading along the way. The speedometer tells you the *rate*; the odometer captures the *accumulation*.

This is the essence of the integrative component. Whenever a quantity changes over time, we can always ask two distinct questions: "How fast is it changing at this instant?" and "What has it all added up to so far?" The first question belongs to the derivative. The second belongs to the integrative component — the accumulated total of a continuously changing rate.

Picture filling a bathtub with a faucet whose flow rate varies. At 9:00 am it pours 2 gallons per minute; by 9:05 it slows to 1. You can't just multiply a single rate by the total time. Instead, you chop time into tiny slivers, ask how many gallons arrived in each sliver, and add everything up. Make the slivers infinitely thin and that process becomes an integral.

Formally, if a quantity flows at rate $f(t)$, its integrative component over the interval $[a, b]$ is

$$\int_a^b f(t)\, dt.$$

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus reveals why this feels inevitable: if $F'(t) = f(t)$, then

$$\int_a^b f(t)\, dt = F(b) - F(a).$$

Differentiation and integration are exact inverses — every accumulated total can be unwound back into an instantaneous rate, and vice versa.

**Now you try:** A car's speed at time $t$ seconds is $v(t) = 4t$ m/s. How far does it travel from $t = 0$ to $t = 3$? Compute $\int_0^3 4t\, dt$ and check your answer against common sense.

### moving_average_model (0.2s)

## Moving Average Model

Imagine you work at a bakery and each morning you guess how many loaves you'll sell. You're never perfectly right, but here's what you notice: if you *over-stocked* yesterday, you unconsciously order a little *less* today. Your decision today is shaped by yesterday's mistake — and maybe the mistake from the day before too.

That's the heart of the **Moving Average model**. Not an average of the data itself, but an average of *errors* — the surprises that knocked you off course in recent periods.

Think of each day's outcome as the "expected normal" plus a ripple from past shocks. A big storm yesterday (an unexpected surge in bread-buying) still echoes faintly in today's supply decision. The model says: current reality is baseline plus a weighted echo of recent surprises.

Formally, an MA$(q)$ model writes today's value $y_t$ as:

$$y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\,\varepsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2\,\varepsilon_{t-2} + \cdots + \theta_q\,\varepsilon_{t-q}$$

where $\mu$ is the long-run mean, each $\varepsilon_{t-k}$ is a past error term (a random shock with mean zero), and the $\theta$ weights control how strongly each past shock echoes forward. Crucially, the shocks $\varepsilon_t$ are *independent* — only their weighted sum carries memory.

Notice what makes MA models elegant: the memory is *finite*. After $q$ steps, past shocks vanish completely. Compare that to autoregressive models, where a distant past value can linger indefinitely.

**Now you try:** If $q = 1$ and $\theta_1 = 0.8$, a large positive shock $\varepsilon_{t-1} = 3$ hits. How much does it contribute to $y_t$? What about $y_{t+1}$?

### arima (0.2s)

## Arima

### The Weather Diary Problem

Imagine you kept a daily temperature diary for three years. Someone bets you $100 that you can't predict tomorrow's temperature better than "just guess the average." You flip through your notebook and notice something: cold days tend to follow cold days, and sudden warm spells are usually followed by a correction back toward normal. There's *memory* in the data — and that single observation is the engine of everything that follows.

### One Idea, Two Refinements

The core primitive is **autoregression**: a value predicts itself. If $y_t$ is today's temperature, write $y_t \approx \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \text{noise}$ — the series literally regresses onto its own past. That's the "AR" in ARIMA, and it's the only genuinely new concept you need to internalize here.

The other two letters are refinements that fix specific problems. **Differencing** (the "I") removes a drifting trend — subtract yesterday from today and the series stabilizes. **Moving-average terms** (the "MA") absorb shocks that echo for a day or two after they arrive. You don't need full intuition for either right now; think of them as small corrections you dial in once the autoregressive backbone is working.

Together they give ARIMA$(p, d, q)$:

$$\Delta^d y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i \Delta^d y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j \varepsilon_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t$$

where $p$ = AR lags, $d$ = times you differenced, $q$ = MA lags, and $\varepsilon_t$ is white noise.

### Now You Try

Grab monthly electricity bills for two years. Plot them — do they drift upward, or bounce around a stable mean? If they drift, subtract consecutive months. Does the result look like noisy clumps with no long-term climb? If yes, fit an AR(1) to that differenced series first. Once that works, layer in the MA term and see whether January's bill becomes predictable.
