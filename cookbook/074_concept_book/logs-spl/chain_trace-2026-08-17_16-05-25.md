## Social Network

A **social network** is a structure made up of actors (people, organizations, or other entities) and the relationships connecting them. In graph theory terms, actors are **nodes** and relationships are **edges**. Edges can be undirected (friendship is mutual) or directed (A follows B, but B may not follow A). This simple representation turns messy human relationships into something we can analyze computationally.

The power of the model comes from the metrics it enables. **Degree** counts how many connections a node has — a person with 500 friends has a degree of 500 in an undirected network. **Betweenness centrality** measures how often a node sits on the shortest path between two other nodes; nodes with high betweenness act as brokers or bridges. **Clustering coefficient** captures how tightly knit a node's neighbors are — if all your friends also know each other, your local cluster is dense.

**Worked example.** Suppose five students — Alice, Bob, Cara, Dan, and Eve — are connected as follows: Alice–Bob, Alice–Cara, Bob–Dan, Dan–Eve. Draw this as a path with Alice and Eve at opposite ends. Alice's degree is 2 (connected to Bob and Cara). Dan's degree is 2 (Bob and Eve). Now ask: who is most central? Bob sits on every shortest path between Alice's side and Dan's side, giving Bob the highest betweenness centrality — even though no one has the most connections.

**Problem-solving application.** Imagine a public-health team wants to slow the spread of misinformation in an online community. They can export follower data and build a directed graph, then compute betweenness centrality for every account. Rather than targeting the most-followed accounts (highest in-degree), they target high-betweenness accounts — the ones whose removal disconnects the largest number of information pathways. In Python with NetworkX:

```python
import networkx as nx

G = nx.DiGraph()
G.add_edges_from([("A","B"), ("B","C"), ("A","D"), ("D","C"), ("C","E")])
centrality = nx.betweenness_centrality(G)
key_node = max(centrality, key=centrality.get)
print(key_node, centrality[key_node])
```

Running this identifies node C as the critical broker. Suspending or throttling that account disrupts more spread than removing any single high-follower account, illustrating how network structure — not raw popularity — drives influence.

## Affiliation Network

An **affiliation network** (also called a bipartite network or two-mode network) is a graph that connects two distinct types of nodes — typically *actors* and *groups* — where edges only run between types, never within the same type. A student belongs to clubs; an author co-writes papers; a company sits on industry boards. The network encodes these memberships, and from that structure we can infer relationships among actors that are never stated explicitly.

The key analytical move is **projection**: collapsing a bipartite graph down to a one-mode network. If two actors share at least one group, they get a link in the projected actor network. The weight of that link can reflect how many groups they share, giving a richer picture of indirect connection.

**Worked example.** Suppose four students — Alice, Bob, Carol, Dana — belong to the following clubs:

- Chess Club: Alice, Bob, Carol  
- Debate Team: Bob, Dana  
- Math League: Alice, Carol, Dana  

Draw a bipartite graph with students on one side, clubs on the other, and edges for membership. Now project onto students. Alice and Bob share Chess; Alice and Carol share Chess and Math League (weight 2); Bob and Carol share Chess; Alice and Dana share Math League; Carol and Dana share Math League; Bob and Dana share Debate. The resulting weighted network reveals that Alice–Carol is the strongest pair, a fact invisible in the raw membership list.

**Problem-solving application.** A streaming platform stores which users have watched which films. You want to recommend movies by finding users with similar taste — but you don't have explicit ratings, only watch histories. Model this as an affiliation network (users ↔ films), then project onto users. Two users connected by a high-weight edge (many shared films) are strong candidates for cross-recommendation. In code:

```python
import networkx as nx

B = nx.Graph()
B.add_nodes_from(["Alice","Bob","Carol"], bipartite=0)
B.add_nodes_from(["Chess","Debate","Math"], bipartite=1)
B.add_edges_from([("Alice","Chess"),("Bob","Chess"),("Carol","Chess"),
                  ("Bob","Debate"),("Dana","Debate"),
                  ("Alice","Math"),("Carol","Math"),("Dana","Math")])

from networkx.algorithms import bipartite
students = {"Alice","Bob","Carol","Dana"}
G = bipartite.weighted_projected_graph(B, students)
print(G.edges(data=True))
```

When analyzing an affiliation network, always ask: *what does a shared group actually mean?* Co-authorship implies collaboration; co-membership in a club may only imply proximity. The network is only as meaningful as the relationship the affiliation represents.
