### sequence (0.1s)

## Sequence

Imagine you're saving money for a bicycle. Week one you have $5. Week two, $10. Week three, $15. You don't need a formula to see what's happening — your brain already feels the pattern. That feeling *is* the concept of a sequence.

A **sequence** is simply a list of numbers written in a definite order, where the position of each number matters. The first number isn't interchangeable with the second; order is everything. Think of your savings as a story: each chapter reveals the next number, and skipping a chapter breaks the narrative.

We write a sequence using subscripts to name each term by its position:

$$a_1, \; a_2, \; a_3, \; \ldots, \; a_n, \; \ldots$$

Here $a_n$ is the $n$-th term — the value at step $n$. In the savings story, $a_n = 5n$, so $a_1 = 5$, $a_2 = 10$, and so on. The rule $a_n = 5n$ is called the **general term** or **closed form** — a single expression that generates any term directly without listing all the ones before it.

Not every sequence grows so neatly. The sequence $1, -1, 1, -1, \ldots$ alternates forever and never settles. The sequence $1, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \ldots$ shrinks toward zero but never quite arrives. Each is a valid sequence — a list in order — and studying where they *head* is the next great question.

**Now you try.** Consider the sequence defined by $a_n = n^2$. Write out the first five terms. Does the gap between consecutive terms grow, shrink, or stay constant? What does that tell you about how fast the sequence is accelerating?

### time_series (0.2s)

## Time Series

Imagine you check your city's temperature every morning at 8 a.m. and write it down. After a year, you have 365 numbers. But here's what makes this interesting: the *order* matters. Tuesday's reading depends on Monday's. Winter readings cluster near freezing; summer readings cluster near scorching. If you shuffled those 365 numbers into a random order, you'd lose something real — the shape of the seasons, the memory of yesterday's cold front.

That's the heart of a time series: a sequence of measurements where *position in time* is as important as the value itself.

**Why does order matter?** Because the world has inertia. Prices don't jump randomly from $5 to $500 overnight. Your heart rate right now is close to what it was one second ago. Time-ordered data carries *autocorrelation* — the technical name for "today resembles yesterday."

Formally, a time series is a collection of observations indexed by time:

$$x_1,\ x_2,\ x_3,\ \ldots,\ x_T$$

where $x_t$ is the value at time step $t$. The gap between steps (one second, one day, one year) is called the **sampling interval**. Keep it consistent, or the math gets messy.

Two patterns to look for immediately:

- **Trend** — does the overall level drift upward or downward over time?
- **Seasonality** — does a pattern repeat on a fixed cycle (daily, weekly, yearly)?

A city's electricity demand has both: a long-term rise as the population grows, and a daily spike every evening when people come home.

**Now you try.** Take your phone's step count for the last 30 days. Sketch it roughly. Can you spot a weekly rhythm — fewer steps on weekends, perhaps? That's seasonality revealing itself, hiding in plain sight.

### error (12.9s)

## Error

Imagine you're trying to hit a bullseye with a dart. You throw, and the dart lands two centimetres to the left. That gap — the distance between where you *aimed* and where you *landed* — is the error. It's not a failure; it's information. It tells you exactly how wrong you were, and in which direction.

This is the spirit of error in mathematics: **the difference between what you expected and what actually happened**.

When a model predicts the temperature tomorrow will be $20°C$ and it turns out to be $23°C$, the error is simply:

$$e = \hat{y} - y = 20 - 23 = -3$$

The negative sign matters — it tells you the prediction was *too low*. If we just want the size of the mistake without caring about direction, we take the absolute value: $|e| = 3$.

Often, though, we care about many predictions at once. A single error might be a fluke. So we collect them all and ask: on average, how far off am I? One popular answer is the **Mean Squared Error (MSE)**:

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} ({\hat{y}_i - y_i})^2$$

Squaring each error does two things: it makes every term positive (no cancellations), and it *punishes* big mistakes more heavily than small ones.

Error is the compass of learning — machines adjust their parameters specifically to shrink it, and scientists redesign experiments when it's too large.

**Now you try:** You guess a friend's age as $30$; they're actually $34$. What is the signed error? What is the absolute error? If you made three guesses with errors $-3, 1, 2$, what is the MSE?

### time_series_model (29.7s)

## Time Series Model

**Your coffee temperature tells a story.** Pour a cup, walk away, and check it every five minutes. You don't need a physics degree to predict the next reading — you just notice the pattern: it cools fast at first, then slower, then nearly stops. That sequence of measurements, ordered by time, is a *time series*. A time series model is simply a mathematical way of saying "the past tells us something about the future."

**Why does this feel right?** Because most things in the world have *memory*. Today's stock price leans on yesterday's. This hour's air temperature echoes the last hour's. If measurements were completely independent — if tomorrow's weather had nothing to do with today's — forecasting would be hopeless. Time series models work precisely because that independence rarely holds.

The simplest model makes this explicit. Suppose your measurement at time $t$ is $y_t$. An *autoregressive* model of order 1 says:

$$y_t = \phi \, y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$$

Here $\phi$ is a number you estimate from data (how strongly the past pulls the future), and $\varepsilon_t$ is random noise — the unpredictable part. If $|\phi| < 1$, the series stays bounded and forecastable. If $|\phi| \geq 1$, it drifts away without limit. That single inequality tells you whether the pattern is something you can trust or something that will eventually escape you.

**Now you try.** Record the outside temperature at noon for seven days. Plot the values. Does tomorrow's temperature seem closer to today's, or to the week's average? That gut feeling is already an informal time series model.

### forecasting (16.4s)

## Forecasting

**The weather reporter's secret**

Your phone tells you there's a 70% chance of rain tomorrow. But tomorrow hasn't happened yet — so where does that number come from? Nobody is guessing randomly. The forecaster is doing something deeply human: using patterns from the past to shrink uncertainty about the future.

**Why this feels right**

Think about a friend who is almost always late. After the fifth time, you stop being surprised — you *expect* it. You've built an internal model. Forecasting is just making that instinct precise.

The core idea: the future tends to rhyme with the past. If something happened under certain conditions before, it's likely to happen again under similar conditions. We're not predicting with certainty — we're shifting a probability in the right direction.

**The simplest possible model**

Suppose you record the temperature every day for a week:

$$x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_7$$

The dumbest useful forecast for tomorrow is the average:

$$\hat{x}_8 = \frac{1}{7}\sum_{i=1}^{7} x_i$$

But you notice recent days matter more — yesterday is more informative than last Monday. So you weight recent observations higher. This leads to the **exponential moving average**:

$$\hat{x}_{t+1} = \alpha \, x_t + (1-\alpha)\, \hat{x}_t, \quad 0 < \alpha < 1$$

A large $\alpha$ makes the forecast chase today's value. A small $\alpha$ trusts history more. The right $\alpha$ is whichever minimises your past errors.

**Now you try**

Pick any daily quantity you can measure for a week — steps walked, minutes of sleep, cups of coffee. Record seven values, then use the formula above with $\alpha = 0.3$ to predict day eight. Check how close you were.

### naive_forecasting (12.5s)

## Naive Forecasting

Your friend asks what the weather will be like tomorrow. You glance outside, shrug, and say "probably the same as today." Congratulations — you just invented naive forecasting.

It sounds almost too simple to be useful. But pause and ask: *why does this feel right?* Weather, sales figures, stock prices, and commute times all carry momentum. Today's value is the freshest signal you have. Everything else — seasonal patterns, economic theory, gut instinct — is a guess layered on top of that single data point. Naive forecasting strips away all those layers and bets on inertia alone.

**The intuition.** Imagine a river. The water level this morning is the best predictor of the water level this afternoon — not because rivers are boring, but because they change slowly relative to the measurement window. Naive forecasting works best when that "slow change" assumption holds.

**The minimal formalisation.** If your time series is $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_t$, the naive forecast for the next period is simply:

$$\hat{y}_{t+1} = y_t$$

That's it. No parameters to fit, no training data to split, no hyperparameters to tune. The forecast is always the last observed value.

For seasonal data — say, ice-cream sales — a small twist: use the value from the *same period last year*:

$$\hat{y}_{t+1} = y_{t - s}$$

where $s$ is the season length (e.g., $s = 52$ for weekly data spanning a year).

**Now you try.** Monthly electricity bills for a household: Jan $\$120$, Feb $\$115$, Mar $\$118$. What is the naive forecast for April? Now suppose the same month last year was $\$122$ — which forecast would you trust more, and why?
