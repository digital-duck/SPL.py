### natural_logarithm_integral_def (0.1s)

## Natural Logarithm Integral Def

The natural logarithm is usually introduced as the inverse of the exponential function $e^x$, but there is a cleaner, self-contained way to *define* it that reveals exactly where it comes from and why it behaves the way it does.

**Definition.** For any $x > 0$,

$$\ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t} \, dt$$

Geometrically, $\ln x$ is the signed area under the curve $y = 1/t$ from $t = 1$ to $t = x$. When $x > 1$ the area is positive; when $0 < x < 1$ the area is negative (you are integrating leftward from 1); and $\ln 1 = 0$ because the interval has zero width.

This definition immediately explains the derivative: by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, if $f(x) = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt$, then $f'(x) = \frac{1}{x}$. So $\frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}$—no circular reasoning, no hand-waving.

**Worked example.** Show that $\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$ for $a, b > 0$.

Split the integral at $t = a$:

$$\ln(ab) = \int_1^{ab} \frac{dt}{t} = \int_1^{a} \frac{dt}{t} + \int_a^{ab} \frac{dt}{t}$$

In the second integral, substitute $t = au$ so $dt = a\,du$. When $t = a$, $u = 1$; when $t = ab$, $u = b$:

$$\int_a^{ab} \frac{dt}{t} = \int_1^{b} \frac{a\,du}{au} = \int_1^{b} \frac{du}{u} = \ln b$$

Therefore $\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$. The product rule for logarithms is a direct consequence of how the integral scales under substitution—it is built into the definition, not a separate fact to memorize.

**Problem-solving application.** Evaluate $\int_1^{e^3} \frac{2}{x}\,dx$.

Recognize $\frac{1}{x}$ as the integrand of $\ln x$:

$$\int_1^{e^3} \frac{2}{x}\,dx = 2\ln x \Big|_1^{e^3} = 2\ln(e^3) - 2\ln 1 = 2(3) - 0 = 6$$

More broadly, whenever you see $\int \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx$, the substitution $u = f(x)$ converts it to $\int \frac{du}{u} = \ln|u| + C$. Recognizing this pattern—a derivative in the numerator over the original function—is one of the most frequently tested integration techniques in calculus.

### exponential_function_def (0.2s)

## Exponential Function Definition

An **exponential function** is a function of the form

$$f(x) = a \cdot b^x$$

where $a \neq 0$ is the initial value (the output when $x = 0$), $b > 0$ and $b \neq 1$ is the **base**, and $x$ is any real number. What makes this function special — and fundamentally different from polynomial functions — is that the variable $x$ sits in the exponent rather than the base. When $b > 1$, the function grows without bound as $x$ increases; when $0 < b < 1$, it decays toward zero. Both behaviors appear constantly in science, finance, and computing.

The natural exponential function $f(x) = e^x$, where $e \approx 2.71828$, is the most important special case. It arises naturally whenever a quantity grows at a rate proportional to its current size — bank interest compounding continuously, radioactive decay, population growth in unlimited environments.

### Worked Example

Suppose a bacterial culture starts with 500 cells and doubles every 3 hours. Write an exponential function for the population $P$ after $t$ hours, then find the population after 9 hours.

The doubling time is 3 hours, so the base is $2$ and the exponent must equal $1$ after every 3 hours. This gives:

$$P(t) = 500 \cdot 2^{t/3}$$

At $t = 9$:

$$P(9) = 500 \cdot 2^{9/3} = 500 \cdot 2^3 = 500 \cdot 8 = 4{,}000 \text{ cells}$$

### Problem-Solving Application

Exponential functions appear any time a quantity changes by a **constant multiplicative factor** over equal time intervals — this is the key diagnostic. If you see a table of values where the ratio $f(x+1)/f(x)$ is constant, the relationship is exponential (not linear, where the *difference* is constant).

To identify the model from data: (1) check whether consecutive ratios are approximately equal; (2) read $a$ as the $y$-intercept; (3) read $b$ as that common ratio. To find when a quantity reaches a target value, set up the equation $a \cdot b^x = \text{target}$ and solve using logarithms:

$$x = \frac{\ln(\text{target}/a)}{\ln b}$$

This logarithm step is the standard tool for "unlocking" an unknown exponent, and it connects exponential functions directly to their inverses.

### exponential_decay_model (0.2s)

## Exponential Decay Model

**Definition.** Many real-world quantities decrease at a rate proportional to their current size — the more you have, the faster you lose it. This produces exponential decay, described by:

$$Q(t) = Q_0 \, e^{-kt}$$

where $Q_0$ is the initial quantity, $k > 0$ is the decay constant (units: 1/time), and $t$ is elapsed time. The underlying differential equation is $\frac{dQ}{dt} = -kQ$, which simply says "the rate of loss is proportional to what remains." The solution to that equation is the formula above — a fact worth knowing, even if you derive it later in calculus.

A convenient rearrangement defines the **half-life** $t_{1/2}$: the time at which $Q$ falls to half its starting value.

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} \approx \frac{0.693}{k}$$

Half-life and decay constant are two descriptions of the same phenomenon; knowing one immediately gives the other.

**Worked example.** Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. A charcoal sample from an archaeological site retains 38% of its original $^{14}$C. How old is the sample?

First, find $k$:

$$k = \frac{\ln 2}{5730} \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4} \text{ yr}^{-1}$$

Then solve $0.38 = e^{-kt}$ for $t$:

$$t = \frac{-\ln(0.38)}{k} = \frac{0.968}{1.21 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 8{,}000 \text{ years}$$

The sample is roughly 8,000 years old.

**Problem-solving application.** The same logic applies outside physics. A drug with a 4-hour half-life is administered at 200 mg. A second dose is given 6 hours later. To find the total drug concentration in the body immediately after the second dose:

$$C = 200\,e^{-k \cdot 6} + 200, \quad k = \frac{\ln 2}{4}$$

$$C = 200 \cdot e^{-1.04} + 200 \approx 200(0.354) + 200 \approx 271 \text{ mg}$$

This kind of stacking calculation — summing decayed residuals from prior doses — appears in pharmacokinetics, environmental contamination modeling, and capacitor discharge analysis. The key habit: identify $Q_0$ and $t_{1/2}$ (or $k$) from context, write the formula, then solve algebraically. Exponential decay is one of the most transferable quantitative tools across science and engineering.

### newtons_law_of_cooling (16.5s)

## Newtons Law Of Cooling

When a hot cup of coffee sits on your desk, it doesn't cool at a constant rate — it cools quickly at first, then more and more slowly as its temperature approaches room temperature. Newton's Law of Cooling captures this behavior precisely: **the rate at which an object's temperature changes is proportional to the difference between the object's current temperature and the surrounding ambient temperature.**

Formally, if $T(t)$ is the object's temperature at time $t$ and $T_a$ is the ambient (surrounding) temperature, then:

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_a)$$

where $k > 0$ is a cooling constant that depends on the object's material and surface properties. The negative sign ensures the temperature decreases when $T > T_a$. Solving this differential equation with initial condition $T(0) = T_0$ gives:

$$T(t) = T_a + (T_0 - T_a)\,e^{-kt}$$

This is an exponential decay toward the ambient temperature — exactly the behavior you'd observe with that coffee.

**Worked Example.** A cup of coffee starts at $90\,°C$ in a room at $20\,°C$. After 10 minutes it has cooled to $70\,°C$. What will its temperature be after 30 minutes?

Step 1: Use the 10-minute data point to find $k$.

$$70 = 20 + (90 - 20)\,e^{-10k} \implies 50 = 70\,e^{-10k} \implies e^{-10k} = \frac{5}{7}$$

$$k = -\frac{1}{10}\ln\!\left(\frac{5}{7}\right) \approx 0.03365\,\text{min}^{-1}$$

Step 2: Plug in $t = 30$.

$$T(30) = 20 + 70\,e^{-3 \times 0.03365 \times 10} = 20 + 70\left(\frac{5}{7}\right)^3 \approx 20 + 70(0.3644) \approx 45.5\,°C$$

**Applying it to new problems.** Newton's Law of Cooling appears wherever exponential thermal decay matters: forensic scientists estimate time of death by measuring a body's temperature against room temperature; food safety engineers verify that refrigerated goods cross below $4\,°C$ within a required time window; engineers size heat sinks for electronics. In each case, the strategy is identical — collect two temperature readings at known times to pin down $k$, then use the formula to predict temperatures at any other time or to solve for the time when a target temperature is reached.
