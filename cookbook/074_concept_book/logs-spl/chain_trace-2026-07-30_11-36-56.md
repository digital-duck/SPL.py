## Data Privacy

**Definition.** *Data privacy* refers to the set of mathematical and computational guarantees that control what information an adversary can infer about an individual from a published dataset or query result. The most rigorous modern formulation is *differential privacy* (Dwork, 2006). A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{R}$ is said to be $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differentially private if, for every pair of *neighboring* datasets $D, D' \in \mathcal{D}$ differing in exactly one record, and for every measurable output set $S \subseteq \mathcal{R}$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \;\leq\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta.$$

When $\delta = 0$ the guarantee is called *pure* differential privacy. The parameter $\varepsilon > 0$ is the *privacy budget*: smaller values impose stricter privacy at the cost of greater output noise.

**Worked Example.** Suppose a hospital wishes to publish the mean age $\mu$ of its $n = 1000$ patients, where each age lies in $[0, 120]$. The *sensitivity* of the mean query is

$$\Delta f = \frac{120 - 0}{n} = \frac{120}{1000} = 0.12,$$

because replacing one record can shift the mean by at most $0.12$ years. The *Laplace mechanism* releases $\tilde{\mu} = \mu + \mathrm{Lap}(0,\, \Delta f / \varepsilon)$. Setting $\varepsilon = 0.5$ yields noise drawn from $\mathrm{Lap}(0, 0.24)$, whose standard deviation is $0.24\sqrt{2} \approx 0.34$ years — a clinically negligible perturbation that nonetheless satisfies $(0.5, 0)$-differential privacy.

**Key Theorem (Composition).** If $\mathcal{M}_1$ is $(\varepsilon_1, \delta_1)$-DP and $\mathcal{M}_2$ is $(\varepsilon_2, \delta_2)$-DP, then their sequential composition $(\mathcal{M}_1, \mathcal{M}_2)$ is $(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2,\, \delta_1 + \delta_2)$-DP. This *sequential composition theorem* implies that privacy budgets accumulate across queries, so practitioners must track total expenditure $\sum_i \varepsilon_i$ across the lifetime of a dataset.

## Ethics In Data Science

**Definition.** Ethics in data science is the systematic study of moral obligations that arise when collecting, processing, analyzing, and deploying data-driven models. It addresses three interlocking domains: *privacy* (the right of individuals to control information about themselves), *fairness* (the requirement that algorithmic decisions do not unjustly discriminate), and *accountability* (the obligation to explain, audit, and correct automated systems). Formally, a data pipeline $\mathcal{P}: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is said to satisfy **demographic parity** with respect to a protected attribute $A \in \{0,1\}$ if

$$P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1),$$

where $\hat{Y}$ denotes the model's binary prediction. Violations of this condition constitute a measurable, quantifiable form of algorithmic bias.

**Worked Example.** A credit-scoring model trained on historical loan data produces acceptance rates of $78\%$ for group $A=0$ and $52\%$ for group $A=1$. The disparity ratio is $52/78 \approx 0.667$, below the widely-cited **four-fifths rule** threshold of $0.8$. One corrective strategy is **reweighting**: assign each training sample $(x_i, y_i)$ a weight

$$w_i = \frac{P(A)}{P(A = a_i)},$$

effectively rebalancing the empirical distribution so the model cannot exploit group membership as a proxy feature. After reweighting and retraining, practitioners re-evaluate both the disparity ratio and overall accuracy, accepting a possible accuracy–fairness trade-off as an explicit design decision rather than an overlooked artifact.

**Key Theorem (Impossibility of Simultaneous Fairness Criteria).** Chouldechova (2017) proved that when base rates differ across groups ($P(Y=1 \mid A=0) \neq P(Y=1 \mid A=1)$), no classifier can simultaneously satisfy demographic parity, equalized odds, and calibration. This result is not a failure of engineering; it is a mathematical fact that forces practitioners to *choose* which fairness criterion the application's social context demands, and to document that choice transparently.

**Lab Cell (SymPy).**

```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational, simplify

p0, p1 = Rational(78, 100), Rational(52, 100)

```

## Regulatory Compliance

**Definition.** Regulatory compliance is the state in which a system, process, or entity satisfies all constraints imposed by an external governing authority within a specified operational domain. Formally, let $\mathcal{S}$ denote a system described by a state vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, and let $\mathcal{R} = \{g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_m\}$ be a finite set of regulatory constraints, where each $g_i : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a measurable function. The system is said to be *compliant* if and only if

$$g_i(\mathbf{x}) \leq 0 \quad \forall\, i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, m\}.$$

The *feasible compliance region* is the set $\mathcal{F} = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X} \mid g_i(\mathbf{x}) \leq 0,\ \forall i\}$. Compliance analysis then reduces to verifying that the operational trajectory $\mathbf{x}(t)$ remains within $\mathcal{F}$ for all $t$ in the relevant time horizon.

**Worked Example.** A pharmaceutical manufacturer must ensure that active-ingredient concentration $c$ (mg/L) satisfies $80 \leq c \leq 120$. Expressing this as two inequality constraints: $g_1(c) = 80 - c \leq 0$ and $g_2(c) = c - 120 \leq 0$. A batch yields $c = 95\,\text{mg/L}$. Since $g_1(95) = -15 < 0$ and $g_2(95) = -25 < 0$, the batch lies strictly inside $\mathcal{F}$ and is compliant. A batch with $c = 125\,\text{mg/L}$ violates $g_2$ and must be rejected.

**Theorem (Feasibility of Linear Compliance Regions).** If every constraint $g_i$ is affine—that is, $g_i(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a}_i^\top \mathbf{x} - b_i$ for some $\mathbf{a}_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $b_i \in \mathbb{R}$—then $\mathcal{F}$ is a convex polytope. Consequently, membership testing is decidable in polynomial time via linear programming, and the distance from any point $\mathbf{x}_0$ to $\mathcal{F}$ is computable as $d(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathcal{F}) = \min_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{F}} \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\|_2$, which is a convex quadratic program.

**Lab Cell.**
```python
from sympy import symbols, solve, Rational

c = symbols('c', real=True)
lower, upper = 80, 120

g1 = lower - c   # <= 0 means c >= 80
g2 = c - upper   # <= 0 means c <= 120

for batch in [95, 125]:
    status = "COMPLIANT" if g1.subs(c, batch) <= 0 and g2.subs(c, batch) <= 0 \
             else "NON-COMPLIANT"
    print(f"c = {batch}: g1 = {g1.subs(c,batch)}, g2 = {g2.subs(c,batch)} → {status}")
```

## Transparency

**Definition.** A computational model $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is said to be *transparent* if there exists a human-interpretable representation $\phi$ such that, for every input $x \in \mathcal{X}$, one can reconstruct the full chain of reasoning $f(x) = \phi(x)$ without appealing to opaque intermediate states. Formally, let $S = \{s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_k\}$ denote the sequence of intermediate symbolic steps produced during computation. Transparency requires that each $s_i$ is expressible in a domain language $\mathcal{L}$ legible to a domain expert, and that $f(x) = s_k \circ s_{k-1} \circ \cdots \circ s_1(x)$.

This contrasts with *black-box* models, where the mapping from input to output passes through latent representations (e.g., neural network weight matrices) that carry no intrinsic semantic interpretation.

**Worked Example.** Suppose $f$ computes the roots of a quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$. A transparent implementation records the following steps:

$$s_1: \Delta = b^2 - 4ac, \qquad s_2: x_{1,2} = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}.$$

For $a=1, b=-5, c=6$, we obtain $s_1: \Delta = 25 - 24 = 1$ and $s_2: x = \{2, 3\}$. Every intermediate quantity is a named, inspectable symbol. A reviewer can audit each step independently, verify correctness, and localise any error to a specific $s_i$.

**Key Theorem (Symbolic Auditability).** Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a class of functions computable by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of primitive symbolic operations drawn from $\mathcal{L}$. Then every $f \in \mathcal{F}$ is transparent: there exists a topological ordering of the DAG nodes that constitutes an auditable proof trace of $f(x)$ for any $x$.

*Proof sketch.* By definition of a DAG, no cycles exist; hence a topological sort terminates in $O(|V|+|E|)$ time. Each node in the ordering corresponds to exactly one primitive operation in $\mathcal{L}$, yielding a finite, ordered chain $s_1, \ldots, s_k$ with $s_k = f(x)$. $\square$

**Lab Cell.**

```python
import sympy as sp

a, b, c, x = sp.symbols('a b c x')
discriminant = b**2 - 4*a*c
roots = sp.solve(a*x**2 + b*x + c, x)

# Inspect each symbolic step
print("Discriminant:", discriminant)
print("Roots (symbolic):", roots)

# Evaluate at a=1, b=-5, c=6
print("Roots (numeric):", [r.subs({a:1, b:-5, c:6}) for r in roots])
```

Running this cell exposes $\Delta$ and each root as inspectable SymPy objects, demonstrating that every computational step remains accessible and verifiable — the operational definition of transparency in symbolic systems.

## Gdpr Ccpa

### Definition

The **General Data Protection Regulation** (GDPR, EU 2016/679, effective 25 May 2018) and the **California Consumer Privacy Act** (CCPA, Cal. Civ. Code §1798.100 et seq., effective 1 January 2020) are the two dominant statutory frameworks governing the collection, processing, and transfer of personal data. Both instruments share a common logical skeleton: they define a *data subject* $s \in S$, a *data controller* $c \in C$, a finite set of *processing purposes* $P = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}$, and require that every processing event $e = (s, c, p_i, t)$ be *lawful*, *purposeful*, and *minimised*.

Formally, let $\mathcal{D}(s)$ denote the personal-data universe of subject $s$. A controller $c$ may process subset $A \subseteq \mathcal{D}(s)$ under purpose $p_i$ if and only if a **legal basis** $\ell \in L$ exists, where $L$ is the enumerated basis set. Under GDPR Article 6, $|L| = 6$ (consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interest, public task, legitimate interest). CCPA narrows the instrument to a binary opt-out right rather than an opt-in consent model for most categories.

### Worked Example

Suppose an e-commerce platform collects $\mathcal{D}(s) = \{\text{name}, \text{email}, \text{purchase history}, \text{IP address}\}$. It wishes to send marketing emails ($p_{\text{mkt}}$) and share purchase history with a third-party analytics firm ($p_{\text{analytics}}$).

Under GDPR, both operations require an independent legal basis. The platform selects $\ell = \text{consent}$ for $p_{\text{mkt}}$ and $\ell = \text{legitimate interest}$ for $p_{\text{analytics}}$. The controller must document a *Legitimate Interest Assessment* (LIA) demonstrating that $\text{benefit}(c) \succ \text{risk}(s)$ under a proportionality test. A data-subject request to erase $\mathcal{D}(s)$ (Article 17) must be honoured within 30 days unless a retention obligation overrides it.

Under CCPA, the same subject, if a California resident, may submit an opt-out of sale; the platform must cease transferring purchase history within 15 business days ($t \leq t_0 + 15$).

### Key Principle

**Data Minimisation Principle.** For any lawful processing event $(s, c, p_i, t)$ with legal basis $\ell$, the processed data set $A$ must satisfy

$$A = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{A' \subseteq \mathcal{D}(s),\; \text{sufficient}(A', p_i)} |A'|,$$

where $\text{sufficient}(A', p_i)$ denotes that $A'$ is adequate to fulfil purpose $p_i$. Collecting any $a \in \mathcal{D}(s) \setminus A$ constitutes an unlawful over-collection.

### Lab Cell (SymPy)

```python
from sympy import FiniteSet, Eq, Symbol

# Personal data universe for subject s
D_s = FiniteSet('name', 'email', 'purchase_history', 'ip_address')

# Minimum data required for marketing purpose
A_mkt = FiniteSet('name', 'email')

# Verify minimisation: A_mkt is a proper subset of D_s
print("Minimised?", A_mkt.is_subset(D_s) and A_mkt != D_s)  # True

# GDPR retention window (days): erasure deadline
t0, delta = Symbol('t0'), 30
deadline = t0 + delta
print("Erasure deadline:", deadline)
```

Running this cell confirms that the marketing campaign processes only the minimal sufficient set $A_{\text{mkt}} \subsetneq \mathcal{D}(s)$, and that the erasure deadline is precisely $t_0 + 30$ days, satisfying the Article 17 requirement.
