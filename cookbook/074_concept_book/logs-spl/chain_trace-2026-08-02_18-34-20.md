### rate_of_change (16.2s)

## Rate Of Change

**Rate of change** measures how much one quantity shifts relative to another — most commonly, how a output variable changes as an input variable increases. When both quantities change by fixed amounts, the rate is constant and the relationship is linear. When the rate itself varies, you need calculus to pin it down at a single instant.

The **average rate of change** of a function $f$ over an interval $[a, b]$ is:

$$\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

This is the slope of the secant line connecting two points on the graph. The **instantaneous rate of change** at a point $x = a$ is the limit of that ratio as the interval shrinks to zero — the derivative $f'(a)$.

### Worked Example

A car's position (in miles) at time $t$ hours is given by $s(t) = 3t^2 + 2t$. Find the average rate of change from $t = 1$ to $t = 3$, then find the instantaneous rate at $t = 1$.

**Average rate:**
$$\frac{s(3) - s(1)}{3 - 1} = \frac{(27 + 6) - (3 + 2)}{2} = \frac{33 - 5}{2} = 14 \text{ mph}$$

**Instantaneous rate:** Differentiate $s(t) = 3t^2 + 2t$ to get $s'(t) = 6t + 2$. At $t = 1$:
$$s'(1) = 6(1) + 2 = 8 \text{ mph}$$

The car is moving at 8 mph exactly at $t = 1$, even though its average over the whole interval is 14 mph — the two answers differ because the car accelerates throughout.

### Problem-Solving Application

Rate of change appears wherever you track how systems evolve. In epidemiology, $\frac{dI}{dt}$ tells you how fast infections are spreading today, which is far more actionable than the total case count. In finance, a stock's instantaneous rate of change signals momentum; the average rate over a quarter tells a different, slower story. The key skill is choosing the right rate for the question: use the **average** when comparing across an interval (unit costs, quarterly growth), and the **instantaneous** when you need the current trend or need to optimize (minimize waste, maximize efficiency). Recognizing which one a problem calls for is usually half the work.

### limit (0.2s)

## Limit

A **limit** describes the value a function approaches as its input gets arbitrarily close to some point — not necessarily the value *at* that point. Formally, we write

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

and mean: for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. In plain terms: we can make $f(x)$ as close to $L$ as we like by keeping $x$ close enough to $a$. The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition is not just bookkeeping — it is what allows limits to exist at points where the function is undefined or discontinuous, which is exactly the situation that makes limits useful.

**Worked example.** Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$$

At $x = 1$, the denominator is zero, so $f(1)$ is undefined. But we can factor:

$$\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x + 1, \quad x \neq 1.$$

As $x \to 1$, $f(x)$ approaches $1 + 1 = 2$. The limit exists and equals 2 even though the function has a hole at that point. The graph would look like the line $y = x + 1$ with a single missing dot at $(1, 2)$.

**Problem-solving application.** Limits are the engine behind derivatives and integrals. When you compute a derivative,

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$

you are asking what the difference quotient *approaches* as $h$ shrinks, not what it equals at $h = 0$ (where it is $\tfrac{0}{0}$). The practical move is always the same: simplify algebraically to cancel the offending factor, then evaluate. For instance, $f(x) = x^2$ gives

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(2a + h) = 2a.$$

When algebra alone does not cancel the singularity, tools like L'Hôpital's rule or Taylor series take over. The key habit: before reaching for those tools, always try factoring or rationalizing — most limit problems at this level collapse with one algebraic step.

### secant_line (17.8s)

## Secant Line

A **secant line** is a straight line that passes through two distinct points on a curve. Given a function $f(x)$ and two input values $x = a$ and $x = b$ (with $a \neq b$), the secant line connects the points $(a,\, f(a))$ and $(b,\, f(b))$. Its slope is the familiar rise-over-run formula:

$$m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

This slope has a concrete physical meaning: it measures the **average rate of change** of $f$ over the interval $[a, b]$. If $f$ models position over time, the secant slope is average velocity. If $f$ models revenue, it is average marginal revenue per unit. The secant line is how calculus begins connecting algebra to real-world rates.

**Worked example.** Let $f(x) = x^2$ with $a = 1$ and $b = 4$.

$$m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{f(4) - f(1)}{4 - 1} = \frac{16 - 1}{3} = \frac{15}{3} = 5$$

The equation of the secant line uses point-slope form with the point $(1, 1)$:

$$y - 1 = 5(x - 1) \implies y = 5x - 4$$

You can verify: at $x = 4$, the line gives $y = 5(4) - 4 = 16 = f(4)$. Both points lie on the line, as required.

**Problem-solving application.** The secant line becomes a lens for approximation and limit reasoning. Suppose a car's position (in miles) is $s(t) = t^3 - 2t$ and you want its average velocity between $t = 2$ and $t = 2 + h$ seconds for a small $h$:

$$m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{s(2+h) - s(2)}{h} = \frac{(2+h)^3 - 2(2+h) - 4}{h}$$

Expanding and simplifying:

$$= \frac{12h + 6h^2 + h^3 - 2h}{h} = 10 + 6h + h^2$$

As $h \to 0$, this approaches $10$ — the instantaneous velocity at $t = 2$. This is exactly the limit definition of the derivative. Every derivative you compute is the limit of a secant slope; understanding the secant line geometrically and algebraically is the essential first step before that limit machinery makes sense.

### tangent_line (15.3s)

## Tangent Line

A **tangent line** to a curve at a point $P$ is the unique line that touches the curve at $P$ and has the same instantaneous slope as the curve at that point. Unlike a secant line, which crosses the curve at two points, the tangent line captures the curve's exact direction at a single instant — think of it as the best straight-line approximation to the curve right at $P$.

The slope of the tangent line at $x = a$ is defined as the limit of the slope of secant lines as the second point approaches $P$:

$$m = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

This limit is exactly $f'(a)$, the derivative of $f$ at $a$. Once you have the slope, the equation of the tangent line follows from point-slope form:

$$y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$$

**Worked example.** Find the tangent line to $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ at $x = 2$.

First, compute the point: $f(2) = 4 + 1 = 5$, so the point is $(2, 5)$.

Next, find the slope: $f'(x) = 2x$, so $f'(2) = 4$.

Plug into point-slope form:

$$y - 5 = 4(x - 2) \implies y = 4x - 3$$

You can verify this geometrically: the line $y = 4x - 3$ grazes the parabola at $(2,5)$ without crossing through it near that point.

**Problem-solving application.** Tangent lines are the engine behind linear approximation. If you know $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$, you can estimate $f$ near $a$ without computing it exactly:

$$f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$$

Suppose you need $\sqrt{9.1}$ without a calculator. Let $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $a = 9$. Then $f(9) = 3$ and $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$, so $f'(9) = \frac{1}{6}$. The approximation gives:

$$\sqrt{9.1} \approx 3 + \frac{1}{6}(0.1) \approx 3.0167$$

The true value is $\approx 3.01662$ — accurate to four decimal places. This technique, called **linearization**, is foundational in physics, engineering optimization, and numerical methods wherever a small perturbation needs a quick, reliable estimate.

### derivative (15.3s)

## Derivative

The derivative of a function measures how fast its output changes relative to its input at any given point. More precisely, the derivative of $f$ at $x = a$ is the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to zero:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

Geometrically, this limit is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a, f(a))$. When the limit exists, $f$ is said to be **differentiable** at $a$. The derivative function $f'(x)$ gives you that slope at every point simultaneously.

**Worked Example.** Find the derivative of $f(x) = x^2$ at $x = 3$.

Apply the definition directly:

$$f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(6 + h) = 6$$

So the slope of $y = x^2$ at $x = 3$ is $6$, and the tangent line there is $y - 9 = 6(x - 3)$.

In practice, the power rule $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$ lets you skip the limit algebra: $f'(x) = 2x$, so $f'(3) = 6$ immediately.

**Problem-Solving Application.** A company's daily revenue (in thousands of dollars) is modeled by $R(x) = -2x^2 + 80x$, where $x$ is the number of units sold. To maximize revenue, you need the value of $x$ where the rate of change flips from positive to negative — that is, where $R'(x) = 0$.

Differentiate: $R'(x) = -4x + 80$. Setting this equal to zero gives $x = 20$. Since $R''(x) = -4 < 0$, the function is concave down, confirming this is a maximum. Selling 20 units yields peak daily revenue of $R(20) = -2(400) + 80(20) = \$800{,}000$.

This pattern — differentiate, set equal to zero, check concavity — is the core algorithm for optimization problems across physics, economics, and engineering. The derivative transforms a vague question ("where is the peak?") into a precise algebraic equation you can solve.

### differential_calculus (19.2s)

## Differential Calculus

Differential calculus studies how functions change. Its central object is the **derivative**, which measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function at a point — the slope of the curve right there, not averaged over an interval.

The derivative of $f(x)$ at a point $x$ is defined as the limit

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

This expression takes the average rate of change over a tiny interval of width $h$ and asks what value that ratio approaches as $h$ shrinks to zero. When the limit exists, the function is **differentiable** at that point.

### Worked Example

Find the derivative of $f(x) = x^2$ and determine the slope of the curve at $x = 3$.

Applying the limit definition:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(2x + h) = 2x$$

So $f'(x) = 2x$, and at $x = 3$ the slope is $f'(3) = 6$. In practice, the **power rule** shortcut — $\frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = nx^{n-1}$ — gives the same result immediately without re-deriving the limit each time.

### Problem-Solving Application

Derivatives answer three broad classes of problems:

**1. Instantaneous rates.** A car's position is $s(t) = 4t^3 - 12t$ meters at time $t$ seconds. Its velocity is $s'(t) = 12t^2 - 12$ m/s. At $t = 2\,\text{s}$, velocity is $12(4) - 12 = 36\,\text{m/s}$.

**2. Optimization.** To maximize or minimize $f$, set $f'(x) = 0$ (a **critical point**) and check the sign of $f'$ on either side. If a company's profit is $P(x) = -2x^2 + 80x - 300$ (dollars, $x$ = units sold), then $P'(x) = -4x + 80 = 0$ gives $x = 20$. Since $P'$ changes from positive to negative there, $x = 20$ is a maximum.

**3. Linear approximation.** Near a known point $a$, $f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$. This tangent-line approximation underlies numerical solvers, error estimates, and the entire field of linearization in engineering.

Mastering the derivative rules — power, product, quotient, and chain — is less about memorization than recognizing which rule applies to the function's structure, then executing it cleanly.
