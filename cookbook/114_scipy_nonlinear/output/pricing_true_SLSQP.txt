## Pricing Optimization — Business Interpretation

### 1. The Optimal Decision

**Set price to $24.00 — this maximizes profit at $1,160.83 per period.**

At this price, the demand model predicts approximately 85 units sold, generating:
- Revenue: $24.00 × 85 = $2,040
- Variable cost: $8.00 × 85 = $680
- Fixed cost: $200
- **Net profit: $1,161**

### 2. Why $24 Is the Sweet Spot

The demand elasticity is 1.5, meaning every 1% price increase causes a 1.5% drop in demand — customers are moderately sensitive. The math resolves to a clean rule: **optimal price = unit cost × elasticity/(elasticity−1) = $8 × 1.5/0.5 = $24**, exactly 3× your variable cost.

- **Below $24**: Margin per unit is thin; you'd need much higher volume to compensate, but demand only grows modestly as you cut price.
- **Above $24**: Margin per unit grows, but demand collapses faster than the margin expands — you end up with fewer dollars total.

### 3. Payoff Comparison Across the Range

| Price | Units Sold | Profit |
|-------|-----------|--------|
| $6 (floor) | ~680 | **−$1,561** (selling below cost) |
| **$24 (optimal)** | **~85** | **+$1,161** |
| $150 (ceiling) | ~5 | **+$573** |

The floor is catastrophic — at $6, you're $2 below cost on every unit. The ceiling leaves $588 of profit on the table versus optimal. The optimum isn't near either boundary, which is a healthy sign.

### 5. One Sensitivity to Explore

**What if elasticity rises to 2.0?** (e.g., a new competitor enters the market, making customers more price-sensitive)

The optimal price would drop to $16 (= $8 × 2/1), demand would roughly double to ~156 units, but per-unit margin halves to $8 — net profit falls to about $1,050. That's a $111 hit from the same cost base. **Knowing your elasticity with confidence is worth more than fine-tuning the price.**
