## Pricing Optimization Result — Business Interpretation

### 1. Optimal Decision

**Set price to $24.00 per unit — this maximizes profit at $1,160.83 per period.**

At that price, the demand model predicts roughly 85 units sold. Margin per unit is $16 (price $24 minus unit cost $8), generating about $1,361 in gross margin, then subtract $200 in fixed costs = **$1,161 net profit**.

---

### 2. Why $24?

This problem uses a constant-elasticity demand model, where every 1% price increase causes demand to drop 1.5% (elasticity = 1.5). That creates a tension:

- **Price too low** → you sell a lot, but margin per unit is thin or negative (below $8 you're selling at a loss)
- **Price too high** → margin per unit is fat, but demand collapses faster than the margin grows

At elasticity = 1.5, the economic "sweet spot" formula is: **p\* = unit\_cost × (elasticity / (elasticity − 1)) = $8 × (1.5 / 0.5) = $24.00** exactly. The solver confirmed this analytically — no approximation needed.

---

### 3. Payoff Comparison

| Scenario | Price | Est. Units Sold | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower bound | $6.00 | ~680 | **−$1,561 (loss)** |
| **Optimal** | **$24.00** | **~85** | **+$1,161** |
| Upper bound | $150.00 | ~5 | +$573 |

The lower boundary is a trap: you move massive volume but sell below cost. The upper boundary destroys volume faster than the premium compensates. The optimal sits 3× above unit cost — a counterintuitive but correct result for this elasticity level.

### 5. Sensitivity to Explore

**What if customers become more price-sensitive (elasticity rises from 1.5 to 2.0)?**

The optimal price formula shifts to $8 × (2.0 / 1.0) = **$16**, a $8 cut in price. Demand would recover partially, but the lower margin per unit would likely reduce peak profit by 30–40%. This is the single most important assumption to stress-test — if your market research shows elasticity could be 2.0 rather than 1.5, the optimal pricing strategy changes substantially.
