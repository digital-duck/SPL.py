**1. Bayesian vs. Random (25 trials)**
Both hit 35% here, but Bayesian found it by trial 14 — likely 5–10 trials earlier than random would have. General advantage: GP builds a surrogate model of the objective surface after each trial and directs the next sample toward high-value regions. Random wastes budget probing low-conversion corners Bayesian already ruled out.

**2. Expected Improvement (EI)**
EI = probability of beating the current best × expected magnitude of that gain. It balances *exploitation* (sample near known peaks) and *exploration* (sample uncertain regions that might be better). The GP gives both a predicted mean and uncertainty; EI is high where the mean is promising *or* uncertainty is large enough to justify the gamble.

**3. Why CRO optima aren't at extremes**
User psychology has diminishing returns. Maximum personalization (headline=1.0) can feel intrusive; maximum above-fold CTA pressure can trigger resistance. The true peak at (0.75, 0.85, 0.60) reflects an interaction effect: layout and headline must be "good enough" without being aggressive. CRO is modeling human comfort, not a pure math function — plateaus and soft cliffs are the norm.

**4. Bayesian GP vs. Optuna TPE**
| Scenario | Use |
|---|---|
| Budget ≤ 50 trials, continuous params | GP — exact uncertainty quantification, better EI |
| Budget > 100 trials, mixed/categorical params | TPE — scales better, handles categoricals natively |
| Need parallelism (batch experiments) | TPE — GP acquisition is sequential by default |
| Interpretability of the surrogate | GP — the model itself is explainable |

GP shines when trials are expensive (real A/B tests). TPE wins when you can run many cheap trials or have hyperparameters that include categorical choices.
