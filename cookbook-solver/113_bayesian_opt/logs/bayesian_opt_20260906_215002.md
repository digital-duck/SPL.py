## CRO Analysis: Bayesian Optimization vs. Random Search

---

**1. Did Bayesian actually outperform random here?**

Technically no — both hit 35.00%, and the headline "+0.0% improvement" confirms it. The real win is **sample efficiency**: Bayesian found the optimum at trial 14/25, meaning 11 remaining trials were confirmatory, not exploratory. In a real CRO context with expensive A/B test slots (weeks of traffic, statistical power costs), finding the answer in 14 experiments vs. burning all 25 matters. Random search got lucky on a relatively easy landscape with a broad plateau at 35%.

---

**2. Expected Improvement (EI)**

EI asks: *"Given my GP model's current belief, how much better than the current best can I expect this point to be?"*

```
EI(x) = E[max(f(x) - f_best, 0)]
       = (μ(x) - f_best) · Φ(Z) + σ(x) · φ(Z)
       where Z = (μ(x) - f_best) / σ(x)
```

- `μ(x)` — GP's predicted mean at candidate point
- `σ(x)` — GP's uncertainty (standard deviation)
- `Φ`, `φ` — CDF and PDF of the standard normal

The two terms represent **exploitation** (first: sample where mean is high) and **exploration** (second: sample where uncertainty is high). EI naturally balances both — a point with mediocre predicted mean but high uncertainty still earns sampling budget, because the GP might be wrong in a good direction.

---

**3. Why CRO optima rarely live at the extremes**

The stated true optimum is `(0.75, 0.85, 0.60)`, not `(1.0, 1.0, 1.0)`. Three real-world mechanisms drive this:

| Mechanism | Example in this problem |
|---|---|
| **Saturation / diminishing returns** | headline_score 0.75→1.0 adds marginal personalization users may not notice |
| **Interaction effects** | CTA above-fold (1.0) + max personalization can read as aggressive; the combination lowers trust |
| **Cognitive load tradeoffs** | A perfectly "clean" layout (1.0) with above-fold CTA (1.0) may feel sparse and untrustworthy vs. slightly structured |

In practice, "more is more" thinking is the most common CRO mistake. Users respond to coherence, not maximized individual signals.

Note: both `(1.0, 1.0, 0.727)` and `(0.75, 0.85, 0.60)` hit 35.00% — the objective has a **flat plateau**, which is why the Bayesian found a valid optimum but not the analytically-defined "true" one. This is also realistic: in live experiments, you often can't distinguish the plateau interior from its peak within noise.

---

**4. Bayesian GP (scikit-optimize) vs. Optuna TPE**

| Dimension | Bayesian GP (this recipe) | Optuna TPE (r112) |
|---|---|---|
| **Parameter space** | Continuous, low-dimensional (≤10 params) | Mixed: continuous + categorical + conditional |
| **Budget** | Small (10–100 trials) | Medium-large (100–10,000 trials) |
| **Parallelism** | Poor — GP refit is sequential | Good — TPE handles async workers |
| **Uncertainty** | Explicit σ(x) from GP posterior | No native uncertainty estimate |
| **Overhead** | GP refit scales O(n³) — slows after ~200 trials | Tree model refit scales cheaply |
| **Interpretability** | Acquisition function is analytically inspectable | Less transparent |

**Rule of thumb**: use GP-Bayesian when each trial is expensive (real A/B test, lab experiment, GPU training run) and you have few dimensions. Use TPE when you need pruning, parallelism, or have categorical/conditional hyperparameters — Optuna's integration with trial pruning is unmatched for ML hyperparameter search.
