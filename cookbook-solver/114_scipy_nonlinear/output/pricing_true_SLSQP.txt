## Pricing Optimization Result

### The Decision

**Set your price to $24.00 per unit.** At this price, the model projects a profit of **$1,160.83 per period**.

---

### Why $24 Is the Sweet Spot

Your customers are price-sensitive (elasticity = 1.5, meaning a 1% price increase cuts demand by 1.5%). Two opposing forces determine the optimal price:

- **Too low (e.g., $6):** You sell a lot of units, but each unit costs $8 to produce. At $6 you're selling *below cost* — volume is high but you lose money on every sale.
- **Too high (e.g., $150):** Your margin per unit is excellent, but almost no one buys at that price — demand collapses faster than margin grows.

At **$24**, the margin per unit ($16) and the sales volume balance out to produce the highest total profit. The math confirms this exactly: with elasticity 1.5 and a $8 unit cost, the analytic optimum is `8 × 1.5 / (1.5 − 1) = $24`.

---

### Profit at Key Price Points

The boundaries are instructive: the floor price is catastrophic, and even the far-end ceiling underperforms the optimum by half.

### One Sensitivity to Explore

**What if customers become more price-sensitive — elasticity rising from 1.5 to 2.0?**

The optimal price would drop to **$16**, and profit would shrink to roughly **$113 per period** — a ~90% reduction. This is the single biggest lever in the model. If you're entering a more competitive market or launching a commodity version of your product, re-running the optimization with elasticity = 2.0 will tell you quickly whether the business case still holds.
