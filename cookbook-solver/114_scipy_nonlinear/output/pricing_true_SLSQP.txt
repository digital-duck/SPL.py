## Pricing Optimization: Plain-English Interpretation

---

### 1. The Optimal Decision

**Set price to $24.00 — this maximizes profit at $1,160.83 per period.**

At $24, you sell approximately 85 units per period. Each unit costs $8 to produce, so you earn $16 margin per unit. The $200 fixed cost is covered, leaving $1,161 in profit.

---

### 2. Why $24 Is the Sweet Spot

This result follows directly from how demand responds to price. With an elasticity of 1.5, every 1% price increase cuts demand by 1.5%. That makes customers moderately sensitive — not extremely so, but sensitive enough that you cannot keep pushing price up indefinitely.

The math produces a clean rule: **optimal price = unit cost × elasticity / (elasticity − 1) = $8 × 1.5 / 0.5 = $24.00**.

- **Below $24:** You sell more units, but you're leaving margin on the table. Worse, at $6 you're actually selling *below* your breakeven threshold, losing money on every unit.
- **Above $24:** Demand shrinks faster than your margin grows. You end up with fewer customers and less total profit, even though each sale earns more per unit.

---

### 3. Payoff at Optimum vs. Boundaries

| Price | Est. Units Sold | Profit |
|-------|-----------------|--------|
| $6 (floor) | ~681 | **−$1,561** (selling below cost) |
| **$24 (optimum)** | **~85** | **+$1,161** |
| $150 (ceiling) | ~5 | **+$573** |

The low-price boundary is a disaster — high volume but negative margin. The high-price boundary is profitable but leaves 50% of optimal profit on the table because almost no one buys. The optimum sits well inside the bounds, meaning the constraints didn't force this answer — the model found the true interior maximum.

---

### 4. Verification

**The solution passed verification with zero error (delta = 0.0000).**

This means an independent calculation using the optimal price $24.00 reproduced the exact same profit figure ($1,160.83) as the solver reported. The result is not a numerical artifact or rounding quirk — it is the correct answer for this model.

---

### 5. One Sensitivity to Explore

**What happens if customers become more price-sensitive — say, elasticity rises from 1.5 to 2.0?**

The optimal price would drop to $16 (= $8 × 2.0 / 1.0), and although you'd sell more units, each unit earns only $8 margin instead of $16. Profit would fall significantly. This scenario is realistic if a competitor enters the market or if customers gain more options. Running the solver at elasticity = 2.0 would tell you exactly how much buffer you have before your current $24 pricing strategy needs to change.
