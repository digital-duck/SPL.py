### geospatial_data (16.7s)

## Geospatial Data

You already know that maps show where things are. Geospatial data takes that idea and makes it *precise* — every location on Earth gets assigned numbers so a computer can reason about it.

**The Structure: Coordinates**

Every point on Earth is identified by two numbers: **latitude** ($\phi$) and **longitude** ($\lambda$). Latitude measures how far north or south you are from the equator, ranging from $-90°$ (South Pole) to $+90°$ (North Pole). Longitude measures east-west position from the Prime Meridian, ranging from $-180°$ to $+180°$.

So your school might be stored in a database as something like:

$$(\phi, \lambda) = (37.7749°,\ -122.4194°)$$

That's San Francisco — a single row in a spreadsheet, fully locating a city.

**Layers of Information**

Real geospatial datasets attach *attributes* to each coordinate. A city dataset might store:

| City | Latitude | Longitude | Population |
|------|----------|-----------|------------|
| San Francisco | 37.77 | −122.42 | 874,961 |
| Austin | 30.27 | −97.74 | 978,908 |

This lets you ask questions like "which large cities are within 500 km of each other?" — a calculation computers perform using the **distance formula** adapted for a sphere.

**Simple Rule**

Latitude runs north-south; longitude runs east-west. Negative latitude = Southern Hemisphere; negative longitude = Western Hemisphere.

### spatial_heatmap (14.2s)

## Spatial Heatmap

You've probably seen weather maps where deep red means scorching heat and deep blue means freezing cold. That color-coded map is a heatmap — a way of turning a table of numbers into a picture you can scan in seconds.

**What's actually going on under the hood?**

A spatial heatmap places data onto a 2D grid. Each cell $(i, j)$ holds a value $v_{ij}$, and a color function maps that value to a color. The simplest version is a linear scale: if values range from $v_{\min}$ to $v_{\max}$, a cell's intensity $c$ is

$$c = \frac{v_{ij} - v_{\min}}{v_{\max} - v_{\min}}$$

When $c = 0$ you get the "cold" color; when $c = 1$ you get the "hot" color; everything in between is interpolated. This is called a **colormap**, and the choice of colormap matters — a poorly chosen one can hide patterns or even mislead.

**The core pattern to remember:**

A heatmap is a matrix displayed as an image. Every number becomes a pixel. Clustering of high values appears as a "hot spot," and the eye naturally finds it faster than scanning rows of digits ever could.

This makes heatmaps powerful for *correlation matrices*, *geographic density*, and *confusion matrices* in machine learning — anywhere spatial or relational structure matters.

**Practice problem:**

You survey foot traffic in a 3×3 grid of a shopping mall. The visitor counts are:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 12 & 45 & 30 \\ 8 & 60 & 25 \\ 5 & 40 & 18 \end{bmatrix}$$

Using the formula above, compute the normalized intensity $c$ for the busiest cell and the quietest cell. What colors would those cells receive on a blue-to-red scale?

### choropleth_graph (12.8s)

## Choropleth Graph

You've probably seen a weather map where some states are shaded dark red for extreme heat and others are pale yellow for mild temperatures. That's a choropleth graph — a map where regions are colored to represent a measured value. The word "choropleth" comes from Greek: *choros* (region) + *plethos* (multitude). It's essentially a heat map draped over geography.

**How the structure works.** A choropleth graph has three components working together:

1. **Geographic regions** — countries, states, counties, or any bounded area.
2. **A quantitative variable** — population density, average income, election results, COVID case rates.
3. **A color scale** — a sequential or diverging palette that maps numeric values to colors.

The mapping is straightforward. If you have a value $v$ for each region and your data spans a range $[v_{\min}, v_{\max}]$, each region gets a color proportional to how $v$ sits in that range:

$$\text{intensity} = \frac{v - v_{\min}}{v_{\max} - v_{\min}}$$

An intensity of $0$ maps to the lightest shade; an intensity of $1$ maps to the darkest.

**The key rule.** Choropleth graphs reveal *spatial patterns* — clusters, borders, and gradients across geography. They work best with rates or normalized values (people *per square kilometer*) rather than raw totals, because large regions look artificially important if you color by count alone.

### gis_mapping (15.6s)

## Gis Mapping

You already know that a map shows where things are. GIS — Geographic Information Systems — goes further: it lets you stack *layers* of information on top of geography, like transparent slides placed over one another. One layer shows roads, another shows rainfall totals, another shows population density. Together they reveal patterns no single layer could show alone.

**The coordinate foundation.** Every location on Earth is pinned by two numbers: *latitude* $\phi$ (north–south, from $-90°$ to $90°$) and *longitude* $\lambda$ (east–west, from $-180°$ to $180°$). A GIS stores each feature — a building, a river, a weather station — as a record pairing real-world data with a coordinate pair $(\phi, \lambda)$. When you zoom in on a digital map and click a point, you are querying that database.

**Layers as sets.** Think of each map layer as a set of such records:

$$L_i = \{(d_j,\, \phi_j,\, \lambda_j)\}$$

where $d_j$ is the data value at location $j$. Overlaying two layers means asking questions like: *"Which points in layer A also appear in layer B?"* — a spatial version of set intersection. This is how analysts find, say, which flood-risk zones overlap with low-income neighborhoods.

**The key pattern.** More layers $\Rightarrow$ richer questions, but also more chances for misalignment. Every layer must share the same *coordinate reference system* (CRS) or distances and overlaps will be wrong.
