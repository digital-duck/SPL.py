### training_testing_set (0.1s)

## Training Testing Set

You already know that a model "learns" from examples. But here is a question worth taking seriously: *how do you know the model actually learned something general, rather than just memorizing the answers it was shown?*

That is exactly the problem the training/testing split solves.

**The structure.** Suppose you have a dataset of $n$ labeled examples. You divide it into two non-overlapping subsets:

$$D = D_{\text{train}} \cup D_{\text{test}}, \quad D_{\text{train}} \cap D_{\text{test}} = \emptyset$$

The model sees $D_{\text{train}}$ during learning — it adjusts its internal parameters based on these examples. Then, after training is completely finished, you evaluate the model on $D_{\text{test}}$. Because those examples were never used during training, performance on $D_{\text{test}}$ is a honest estimate of how well the model generalizes to new data.

A common split ratio is **80 / 20**: 80 % for training, 20 % for testing. If you have $n = 1000$ examples, that means $|D_{\text{train}}| = 800$ and $|D_{\text{test}}| = 200$.

**The rule to remember.** A model that scores 99 % on training data but only 60 % on test data has *overfit* — it memorized rather than learned. A good model closes that gap. Test accuracy is always the number that counts.

### labeled_unlabeled_data (0.2s)

## Labeled and Unlabeled Data

You've probably heard that machine learning models "learn from data." But not all data plays the same role — and the most important distinction to understand first is whether your data has *labels*.

**A label** is an answer attached to an example. A photo tagged `"cat"`, an email marked `"spam"`, a tumor scan annotated `"malignant"` — the label tells the model what the correct output should be. When you train on labeled data, the model compares its prediction $\hat{y}$ to the true label $y$ and adjusts itself to shrink the gap. This is called **supervised learning**.

Without labels, you just have raw input: a billion web pages, thousands of X-rays, millions of audio clips. The information is there, but nobody told the model what any of it *means*. That's unlabeled data — no answer attached.

Here's why this matters in practice: labeled data is expensive. A trained radiologist reviewing 10,000 scans costs real money and time. Unlabeled data, on the other hand, is everywhere and nearly free. You often end up with a small labeled set (say, $n_L = 200$ examples) and a much larger unlabeled pool ($n_U = 8{,}000$ examples). The label is what gives the model *direction*; without it, the model has no signal to adjust toward.

A simple rule: a label turns raw input into a training signal.

---

### machine_learning_model (0.2s)

## Machine Learning Model

You already have the intuition: a machine learning model is a program that *learns from examples* rather than following hand-written rules. Now let's look at what's actually happening under the hood.

A model is essentially a mathematical function. It takes some input $x$ — say, pixel values in an image — and produces an output $\hat{y}$, a prediction. The model has internal numbers called **parameters** (often written $\theta$) that control its behavior:

$$\hat{y} = f(x;\, \theta)$$

Training is the process of adjusting $\theta$ so that $f(x;\,\theta)$ gets as close as possible to the true answer $y$ across many examples. We measure "how wrong" the model is with a **loss function** $\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}, y)$ — a small loss means good predictions, a large loss means poor ones.

The simplest model you'll meet is **linear regression**, where:

$$\hat{y} = w \cdot x + b$$

Here the parameters are just a slope $w$ and an intercept $b$. Training finds the $w$ and $b$ that minimize the total loss over all training examples. Neural networks follow exactly the same idea — they just stack many layers of these functions, giving them millions of parameters and far more expressive power.

**The core pattern:** every supervised model shares the same three-part story — a function family $f$, a loss $\mathcal{L}$, and an optimization step that nudges $\theta$ to reduce $\mathcal{L}$.

---

### information_entropy (0.2s)

## Information Entropy

You've probably sensed that some messages carry more "news" than others. Hearing "the sun rose this morning" tells you almost nothing — you already knew that. But hearing "it snowed in July" is genuinely surprising. Information entropy is the mathematical way to measure exactly how much surprise, on average, a source of messages delivers.

**The structure of surprise.** Shannon's key insight: the rarer an event, the more information it carries when it happens. If an event has probability $p$, its *self-information* is

$$I(x) = -\log_2 p$$

(in bits). A coin flip landing heads ($p = 0.5$) gives $-\log_2 0.5 = 1$ bit of information. A die showing a six ($p = \tfrac{1}{6}$) gives $\log_2 6 \approx 2.58$ bits — more surprising, more information.

**Entropy averages the surprise.** For a source with $n$ possible outcomes, each with probability $p_i$, the *entropy* $H$ is the expected (average) self-information:

$$H = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log_2 p_i$$

A fair coin has $H = 1$ bit. A biased coin that lands heads 99 % of the time has $H$ close to 0 — you're almost never surprised.

**The pattern to remember.** Entropy is highest when all outcomes are equally likely (maximum uncertainty) and lowest when one outcome is nearly certain (minimum uncertainty).

---

Calculate the entropy $H$ of this traffic light in bits. Is it higher or lower than a fair three-sided die (where each color has probability $\tfrac{1}{3}$)? What does the difference tell you?

### supervised_learning (0.2s)

## Supervised Learning

You've probably already seen supervised learning in action: a spam filter that learns from thousands of labeled emails, or a photo app that recognizes faces. The key word is *labeled* — someone already told the system the right answer for each example. That's the "supervision."

**The structure underneath.** Formally, supervised learning works with a dataset of paired examples:

$$\{(x_1, y_1),\ (x_2, y_2),\ \ldots,\ (x_n, y_n)\}$$

Each $x_i$ is an **input** (a photo, a sentence, a set of measurements) and each $y_i$ is the corresponding **label** (the correct answer a human provided). The algorithm searches for a function $f$ such that $f(x) \approx y$ — meaning: given a new, unseen input, it predicts the right label.

There are two main flavors depending on what $y$ looks like:

- **Classification** — $y$ is a category (spam / not spam, cat / dog). The algorithm draws decision boundaries between groups.
- **Regression** — $y$ is a number (a house price, a temperature). The algorithm fits a curve through the data.

**The core pattern.** In both cases, the learning algorithm measures its mistakes using a **loss function** $L(\hat{y}, y)$ — a number that grows larger the further the prediction $\hat{y}$ is from the true label $y$. Training means adjusting $f$ to make $L$ as small as possible.

| $y$ | 3 | 4 | 6 | 7 | 9 |

### bootstrapping (16.1s)

## Bootstrapping

You already have the intuition: if you can't afford to run an experiment a thousand times, pretend your one sample *is* the population and draw from it over and over. That's the core trick — resample *with replacement* from the data you have.

**The structure.** Say you collect $n$ measurements: $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$. One bootstrap resample draws $n$ values from that list, allowing repeats. Each draw is equally likely to land on any of the $n$ originals, so some values appear twice (or more) and some not at all. You then compute your statistic — maybe the mean $\bar{x}^*$ — on this fake sample. Repeat that whole process $B$ times (commonly $B = 1000$), and you have $B$ bootstrap estimates:

$$\bar{x}^*_1,\ \bar{x}^*_2,\ \ldots,\ \bar{x}^*_B.$$

The spread of these values approximates how much your original estimate would vary if nature had given you a different sample.

**The rule.** A quick bootstrap confidence interval for the mean uses the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of your $B$ bootstrap means. Sort them; the middle 95% of values gives you a 95% interval — no formula memorization needed, just sorting.

**Why it works.** The sample mirrors the population's shape. Resampling from the sample mimics resampling from the population. The more data you have, the better that mirror is.

---

### decision_tree (0.2s)

## Decision Tree

You've probably played "20 Questions" — where you narrow down a mystery object by asking yes/no questions one at a time. A decision tree is exactly that game, made precise and automatic. Each question splits the possibilities into smaller and smaller groups until only one answer remains.

**The structure.** A decision tree is a flowchart built from three kinds of nodes:

- **Root node** — the very first question, chosen because it splits the data most evenly.
- **Internal nodes** — follow-up questions that further divide the remaining cases.
- **Leaf nodes** — the final answers (called *labels* or *classes*), reached when no more splitting is needed.

At each internal node, you pick a **feature** (like "Is the fruit red?") and a **threshold** (for numbers, like "Is the temperature $> 30°C$?"). The split sends each example left or right. After enough splits, every leaf holds examples that mostly belong to one class.

**A simple rule.** The most common splitting rule is to minimize *impurity* — a measure of how mixed the classes are in a node. One popular measure is the **Gini impurity**:

$$G = 1 - \sum_{k} p_k^2$$

where $p_k$ is the fraction of examples in class $k$. A pure node (all one class) gives $G = 0$; a perfectly mixed node gives the maximum value. You always pick the split that lowers $G$ the most.

**Practice problem.** A node contains 10 fruit examples: 6 apples and 4 oranges. Calculate the Gini impurity of this node. Then suppose you split it into two child nodes — one with 5 apples and 1 orange, another with 1 apple and 3 oranges. Calculate $G$ for each child. Does splitting reduce the overall impurity?

### bootstrap_aggregating (14.6s)

## Bootstrap Aggregating

You've probably heard that asking many people the same question and combining their answers often beats asking just one expert. Bootstrap aggregating — usually called **bagging** — turns that idea into a precise algorithm. Instead of relying on one trained model, you build many models and let them vote.

**How the structure works.** Start with a training dataset of $n$ examples. To create one "bootstrap sample," randomly draw $n$ examples *with replacement* — meaning the same example can be picked more than once. Do this $B$ times to get $B$ different training sets. Train a separate model on each one. Because each dataset is slightly different, each model learns a slightly different version of the pattern.

When a new input arrives, every model makes its own prediction. For a classification task (e.g., "is this email spam?"), the final answer is the **majority vote**:

$$\hat{y} = \text{mode}\bigl(h_1(x),\, h_2(x),\, \ldots,\, h_B(x)\bigr)$$

For a regression task (predicting a number), you average instead:

$$\hat{y} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} h_b(x)$$

**The key pattern.** One model can overfit — it memorizes noise in its training data. When you average across many models trained on *different* samples of that data, the noise cancels out while the true signal reinforces. More models generally means more stability.

---

**Practice problem.** You train $B = 5$ classifiers on bootstrap samples. For a new email, their spam predictions are: spam, not spam, spam, spam, not spam. What does the bagged model predict? Would the answer change if instead their outputs were probability scores $\{0.8,\, 0.4,\, 0.7,\, 0.6,\, 0.3\}$ and you averaged them?

### random_forest (16.8s)

## Random Forest

You already know that a single decision tree asks a sequence of yes/no questions to reach a prediction. The problem? One tree can memorize the training data too closely — it nails the examples it has seen but stumbles on new ones. The fix is surprisingly intuitive: ask many trees, then take a vote.

**Building the forest.** A random forest grows $T$ decision trees (often $T = 100$ or more), but with two deliberate doses of randomness injected at training time:

1. **Bootstrap sampling** — each tree trains on a *random sample* of the original dataset, drawn *with replacement* (so some rows appear twice, others not at all). If the full dataset has $n$ examples, each tree still sees roughly $n$ rows, but a different mix.
2. **Feature subsampling** — at every split inside a tree, only a random subset of $m$ features is considered (a common choice is $m = \lfloor \sqrt{p} \rfloor$ where $p$ is the total number of features).

These two steps mean each tree learns a slightly different version of the truth. At prediction time, every tree casts a vote for a class label, and the forest returns the **majority vote**:

$$\hat{y} = \text{mode}\bigl(\hat{y}_1,\, \hat{y}_2,\, \ldots,\, \hat{y}_T\bigr)$$

Because errors made by individual trees are largely uncorrelated — different trees trip on different examples — they tend to cancel out in the vote rather than pile up.

**The key pattern.** More diverse trees → more errors cancel → stronger ensemble. Randomness is not a weakness; it is the source of diversity that makes the forest more reliable than any single tree.

---

## Facial Recognition

You already do facial recognition constantly — you glance at a friend across the hallway and instantly know who they are. Your brain somehow maps their face to a name without any conscious effort. But how would you teach a computer to do the same thing?

**The structure underneath.** A face is just an image: a grid of pixels, each with a color value. That grid can be enormous — a 200×200 image has 40,000 pixel values. Directly comparing two grids is slow and fragile (lighting, angle, and expression all change the numbers). Instead, modern systems learn a **feature vector** — a compact list of numbers that captures the *geometry* of a face: the distance between your eyes, the width of your jaw, the ratio of nose length to face height, and dozens of other measurements.

If a face is mapped to a vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then two faces are compared by measuring the distance between their vectors:

$$d(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = \|\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(v_{1i} - v_{2i})^2}$$

A small $d$ means the faces are probably the same person; a large $d$ means they are different. A threshold $\tau$ is chosen so the system says "match" when $d < \tau$.

**The key pattern.** Face recognition reduces an identification problem to a *nearest-neighbor search* in a high-dimensional vector space — the same mathematical idea that powers music recommendations and spell-checkers.
