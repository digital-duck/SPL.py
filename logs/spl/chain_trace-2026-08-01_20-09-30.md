### bivariate_data (14.2s)

## Bivariate Data

You already know how to work with a single list of numbers — heights of students, daily temperatures, test scores. That's **univariate** data: one variable per observation. Bivariate data steps things up: now every observation carries *two* measurements at once.

Think of recording both the number of hours a student studied and the score they earned on the exam. Each student gives you a **pair** $(x, y)$, where $x$ is study time and $y$ is the score. Neither number alone tells the full story — the interesting question is how they relate.

**The structure.** A bivariate dataset is a collection of ordered pairs:
$$\{(x_1, y_1),\ (x_2, y_2),\ \ldots,\ (x_n, y_n)\}$$
We call $x$ the **explanatory variable** (the one we control or observe first) and $y$ the **response variable** (the outcome we're trying to understand). You plot these pairs on a **scatter plot** — $x$ on the horizontal axis, $y$ on the vertical — and look for a pattern.

**The key pattern to spot.** When larger $x$ values tend to go with larger $y$ values, we say there's a *positive association*. When larger $x$ values tend to go with *smaller* $y$ values, the association is *negative*. No clear pattern means little or no association.

The strength and direction of a linear association is measured by the **correlation coefficient** $r$, which always satisfies $-1 \le r \le 1$. Values near $\pm 1$ signal a strong linear relationship; values near $0$ signal a weak one.

### scatterplot_color_encoding (14.6s)

## Scatterplot Color Encoding

You already know a scatterplot maps two variables — one on the $x$-axis and one on the $y$-axis. Every dot encodes a data point as a position $(x, y)$. But what if you want to show a *third* variable without adding a third dimension? Color is the answer.

**How it works.** When you assign color to a dot based on a variable's value, you're adding a visual channel on top of position. There are two main flavors:

- **Categorical encoding**: each category gets a distinct hue. If dots represent cities colored by region (North, South, East, West), you're using four separate colors with no implied order.
- **Sequential encoding**: a numeric variable maps to a gradient — for example, pale yellow → deep red, where darker means higher. This works for variables like temperature or population density.

A useful rule: match the color scheme to the variable type.

$$
\text{variable type} \rightarrow \text{color scheme}: \quad
\begin{cases}
\text{categorical} & \rightarrow \text{distinct hues (qualitative palette)} \\
\text{numeric / ordered} & \rightarrow \text{color gradient (sequential palette)}
\end{cases}
$$

Mismatching — say, using a rainbow gradient for country names — forces the reader to hunt for meaning that isn't there.

**One thing to watch for**: roughly 8% of people have some form of color vision deficiency. Palettes like *viridis* or *ColorBrewer* are designed to be perceptually safe.

### three_d_visualization (15.1s)

## Three D Visualization

You already know how to locate a point on a flat grid using two numbers: how far right and how far up. That works beautifully in two dimensions. But the real world has *depth* — and mathematics has a way to capture it.

**Exploring the structure.** In 3D space we add a third axis, usually called $z$, that points straight out toward you (or away from you, depending on the convention). Every point is now described by an ordered triple:

$$P = (x, \, y, \, z)$$

The three axes meet at the **origin** $(0, 0, 0)$ and are mutually perpendicular — each one is at a right angle to the other two. This creates three coordinate *planes*: the $xy$-plane (flat ground, $z = 0$), the $xz$-plane, and the $yz$-plane. Any point in space lives in exactly one of the eight "octants" formed by these three planes, just as 2D points live in one of four quadrants.

**A simple rule.** To plot $(x, y, z)$, start at the origin, walk $x$ units along the $x$-axis, then $y$ units parallel to the $y$-axis, then $z$ units straight up (or down). The **distance** from the origin to any point follows a natural extension of the Pythagorean theorem:

$$d = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$$

This formula is your main tool — it measures how far a 3D point sits from the center.
