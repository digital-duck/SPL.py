### bivariate_data (11.9s)

## Bivariate Data

**Story time:** Mia noticed something strange — on hot days, the ice cream truck always had a long line. On cold days, hardly anyone showed up. "Is the weather *causing* people to buy ice cream?" she wondered. So she started writing two things down every day: the temperature and how many scoops were sold.

**What's going on?** Mia is collecting *two* pieces of information at the same time. That's the big idea — *bi* means two, and *variate* means variable (a thing that changes). Bivariate data is just when you track two changing things together, one pair per observation.

**A tiny bit of formality:** If temperature is $x$ and scoops sold is $y$, each day gives you one point: $(x, y)$. Plot all those points on a graph and you get a *scatter plot*. When the dots drift upward together, the two variables have a positive relationship.

**Now you try:** Pick two things you can measure each day for a week — maybe hours of sleep and your mood score from 1 to 10. Record $(x, y)$ each morning. Do you see a pattern in your points?

### scatterplot_color_encoding (9.2s)

## Scatterplot Color Encoding

**Motivating Story**

Imagine you're looking at a map of your school's candy sales. Every dot shows where a student stands. But wait — some dots are red, some blue, some green. Why? The red ones are students who love chocolate, blue love gummies, and green love sour candies. Suddenly the map tells you *two* things at once: where everyone is *and* what they love.

**Intuition**

A scatterplot already uses position to show two ideas (left-right and up-down). Color adds a *third* idea for free, without cluttering the picture. Your eye naturally groups things by color — you don't even have to think about it.

**Minimal Formalisation**

We call this *color encoding*: each dot gets a color based on a category (like candy flavor) or a number (darker = bigger). A small box called a *legend* tells you what each color means.

**Now You Try**

Draw five dots on paper. Pick three friends and give each a color. Plot dots showing their height (up-down) and age (left-right), colored by friend. What does color help you see instantly?

### three_d_visualization (9.5s)

## Three D Visualization

**A Robot Drawing Your Room**

Imagine a tiny robot that wants to draw a perfect map of your bedroom. It can measure how far left or right something is, how far forward or back, and how high up it floats. Those three measurements — left-right, front-back, up-down — give the robot everything it needs to place any object exactly.

**Why Three Directions?**

You already know two directions from maps: go across (East-West) and go up the page (North-South). But your bedroom isn't flat! Your ceiling is way up there, and your floor is down here. Adding that third direction — height — is what makes a map truly *lifelike*.

We call these three directions the $x$-axis, $y$-axis, and $z$-axis. Every point in your room gets three numbers: $(x, y, z)$. Change one number and the point slides along one direction, like a bead on a wire.

**Now You Try**

Stand in the corner of a room. That corner is your $(0, 0, 0)$. Take two steps forward — you changed $y$. Now reach your hand up — you changed $z$. What changed $x$?
