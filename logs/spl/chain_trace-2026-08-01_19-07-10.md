## Big Data

**Definition.** A dataset is colloquially called *big data* when its volume, velocity, or variety (the "three Vs" of Laney 2001) causes conventional in-memory, single-machine algorithms to fail in finite time or memory. More precisely, let $n$ denote the number of observations and $d$ the ambient dimension. Classical statistical learning theory — rooted in VC-dimension bounds and Hoeffding-type concentration — assumes $n$ and $d$ are fixed constants relative to available RAM. The big-data regime instead studies the limit $n, d \to \infty$ jointly, often with $d/n \to \gamma \in (0, \infty)$, or $n$ so large that a single pass over the data is the only feasible operation.

**A Central Theorem: Streaming Lower Bounds.** Much of the theoretical backbone of big-data computation comes from *communication complexity* and *sketching theory*. A foundational result is the following.

**Theorem (Alon–Matias–Szegedy, 1999).** Any randomized streaming algorithm that computes the $\ell_2$ frequency moment $F_2 = \sum_i f_i^2$ of a stream of $n$ elements drawn from universe $[m]$ with constant success probability requires $\Omega(\min(n, m))$ bits of space in the worst case.

*Proof sketch.* Reduce from the two-party communication problem INDEX: Alice holds $x \in \{0,1\}^m$, Bob holds index $j$, and they must compute $x_j$ with $O(1)$ rounds. Any streaming algorithm for $F_2$ can be converted to a protocol for INDEX by having Alice simulate the stream for her elements and send the memory state; Bob reads off the answer. Since INDEX requires $\Omega(m)$ bits of communication (Shannon lower bound on information), the space lower bound follows. $\square$

This result established that *exact* computation of basic statistics is information-theoretically expensive, forcing the field toward $\varepsilon$-approximation algorithms: the celebrated **Count-Min Sketch**, **AMS Sketch**, and **Johnson–Lindenstrauss random projections** all achieve $\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})$ space at the cost of a $(1 \pm \varepsilon)$ multiplicative error.

**Literature Context.** The AMS paper launched a program that now encompasses sublinear algorithms, compressed sensing (Candès–Romberg–Tao 2006; Donoho 2006), and the random-matrix theory of high-dimensional statistics (Marchenko–Pastur law; Bai–Silverstein 2010). Open questions remain sharp: optimal space-time tradeoffs for graph streaming (Assadi 2022), tight bounds for distributed statistical estimation under communication constraints (Han–Özgür–Weissman 2018), and whether stochastic gradient descent on non-convex losses provably converges in the $n \gg d$ streaming regime without multiple passes.

**Suggested Investigation.** Implement the AMS $F_2$ estimator and the Count-Min Sketch from scratch on a publicly available log or text corpus (e.g., Wikipedia page-view streams or the KDD Cup click-log). Measure empirical error as a function of the space parameter $w$ (number of hash buckets) and the number of independent hash functions $d$, comparing against the theoretical $\delta$-failure bound $\delta = e^{-d}$ and $\varepsilon$-error bound $\varepsilon = \sqrt{e/w}$. Then stress-test by injecting heavy-hitter skew (Zipf-distributed frequencies) and characterize the breakdown point. As an extension, benchmark against the mergeable summary framework of Agarwal et al. (2013), which supports parallel and distributed aggregation — directly relevant to MapReduce and Spark pipelines.

**Report-Writing Note.** A write-up of this investigation should: (1) state the space lower bound formally and identify which step in the proof is tight; (2) present empirical error curves with confidence intervals alongside the theoretical envelope; (3) discuss the gap between worst-case guarantees and average-case behavior on real corpora; and (4) position the findings relative to at least one system-level result — e.g., whether the theoretical constants translate to measurable latency differences at the scale of $10^8$ stream elements.

## Cloud Computing

**Definition.** Cloud computing is a model for on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources—processors, memory, storage, networks, and services—that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management overhead. Formally, let $\mathcal{R} = \{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n\}$ denote a finite set of heterogeneous resources, each characterized by a capacity vector $\mathbf{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d_{\geq 0}$ (CPU cores, RAM, bandwidth, etc.). A *cloud allocation* is a function $\alpha: \mathcal{W} \to 2^{\mathcal{R}}$ mapping a workload set $\mathcal{W}$ to subsets of resources, subject to the feasibility constraint $\sum_{w:\, r \in \alpha(w)} \mathbf{d}_w \leq \mathbf{c}_r$ for all $r$, where $\mathbf{d}_w$ is the demand vector of workload $w$. The cloud provider's optimization objective is typically a linear program minimizing cost $\sum_r p_r \mathbf{1}[r \text{ active}]$ subject to feasibility and quality-of-service (QoS) constraints on latency, throughput, and availability.

**Theorem (Optimal Bin-Packing is NP-Hard in General).** The decision problem of whether a feasible allocation exists for $|\mathcal{W}|$ workloads into $k$ resources of uniform capacity is NP-complete by reduction from bin packing (Garey & Johnson 1979). Consequently, practical cloud schedulers—including Google Borg, Apache Mesos, and Kubernetes—employ polynomial-time approximation algorithms. The First Fit Decreasing (FFD) heuristic achieves an asymptotic ratio of $\frac{11}{9}\text{OPT} + \frac{6}{9}$, while online algorithms must contend with an $\Omega(\log n)$ competitive ratio lower bound (Yao 1980) under adversarial workload arrival.

*Proof sketch.* Map $k$-bin packing to the 3-dimensional vector bin packing on $(\text{CPU}, \text{RAM}, \text{net})$. Any poly-time exact solver implies P = NP. The FFD ratio follows from the classical analysis of Johnson (1973), generalized to vector packing by Chekuri & Khanna (2004) via an LP relaxation whose integrality gap governs the approximation constant. $\square$

**Literature context.** The NIST SP 800-145 definition (Mell & Grance 2011) codified three service models (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS) and four deployment models. Subsequent theoretical work has focused on *stochastic* workloads: Azar et al. (2013) prove that under Poisson arrivals with known service-time distributions, a randomized online algorithm achieves $O(1)$ competitive ratio. The open question of *robustness*—maintaining QoS guarantees under adversarial, heavy-tailed, or correlated failure distributions—remains active. Foundational open problems include: tight competitive ratios for multi-dimensional vector packing under unknown distributions, optimal preemption policies for spot-instance markets, and the interaction between autoscaling laws and tail-latency SLOs at scale.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a discrete-event simulation (Python `simpy` or Julia `SimJulia`) of a cloud cluster with $n = 100$ nodes, each with capacity $\mathbf{c} = (16 \text{ vCPU}, 64 \text{ GB})$. Generate synthetic workloads from a Pareto distribution for service times (shape $\alpha \in \{1.2, 1.5, 2.0\}$) and measure tail latency (P99) as a function of cluster utilization $\rho \in [0.5, 0.95]$. Compare FFD, best-fit, and a learned policy (a simple neural network trained via policy gradient on simulated rollouts). Report how the performance gap between learned and classical schedulers widens as $\alpha$ decreases toward 1 (heavier tails).

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up should: (1) state the formal scheduling model with all constraints explicit; (2) derive or cite the FFD approximation ratio for vector packing; (3) present empirical CDF plots of P99 latency for each policy under each $\alpha$; (4) discuss statistical significance via bootstrap confidence intervals; (5) identify the regime where the learned policy fails (e.g., distribution shift) and connect this to the open robustness question. The conclusion should position the experiment within the broader question of when theoretical approximation guarantees translate to empirical gains under realistic workload statistics.
