## Labeled Unlabeled Data

Imagine you just got a big box of mystery crayons — no labels, no wrappers. You have to guess every color by trying it on paper first. That takes forever! Now imagine a friend already wrote "red," "blue," and "yellow" on most of them. Suddenly, sorting is easy.

Machine learning faces exactly this problem. A **labeled** example is like a crayon with a name on it: a photo of a cat with the word "cat" written underneath. The computer learns from those names. An **unlabeled** example is the mystery crayon — just a photo, no hint.

Here is the tricky part: labeling costs time and money (a human has to write each answer). But unlabeled data is everywhere and free. So scientists invented clever tricks that let a computer learn from *both* — using the labeled examples as guides and the unlabeled ones to notice patterns on its own.

Think of it as: labeled data teaches *what things are called*, unlabeled data teaches *how things are shaped*.

**Now you try:** Look at ten objects around you. Pick three and write their names. Could you still sort all ten into groups using only those three labels as clues?

## Machine Learning Model

**The Cookie-Tasting Robot**

Imagine you have a robot friend who has never tasted a cookie before. You give it one chocolate chip cookie and say, "This is delicious!" Then you give it a burnt cookie and say, "This is yucky!" At first, the robot guesses randomly. But each time you tell it whether it was right or wrong, it adjusts — it notices things like color, smell, and texture. After tasting hundreds of cookies, it gets really good at guessing!

That's exactly what a machine learning model does. Instead of being programmed with strict rules like "if brown = bad," it *learns from examples*. The more examples it sees, the better its guesses become.

A model is like a brain made of math — it finds hidden patterns in data that even humans might miss.

**More formally:** A machine learning model is a mathematical function that takes inputs (like cookie color) and produces outputs (like "tasty" or "yucky") by learning from labeled examples.

### training_testing_set (9.2s)

## Training Testing Set

**The Pancake Practice Problem**

Imagine you want to teach your little sister to flip pancakes. You show her the same three pancakes over and over until she gets them perfect. Then one morning, a guest arrives and you hand her a brand-new recipe — strawberry pancakes she's never seen before. Can she do it?

That's exactly the problem computers face when they learn.

**The Big Idea**

When we teach a computer to recognize pictures of cats, we show it thousands of examples. But if we *only* test it on the same pictures it studied, we're cheating — like letting a student read the test answers beforehand. The grade looks great, but nothing was really learned.

So we split our examples into two piles:

- **Training set** — the pancakes your sister practices on
- **Testing set** — the surprise strawberry pancakes she's never flipped before

The testing set stays hidden until the very end. Only then do we see if the computer *truly* learned — or just memorized.

**Now You Try**

You have 20 animal photos. How many would you use for practice, and how many would you save for the surprise quiz?

### supervised_learning (10.2s)

## Supervised Learning

**The Flashcard Teacher**

Imagine you are learning animal names with a stack of flashcards. Each card shows a picture of an animal, and your teacher flips it over to reveal the answer: "Cat." "Dog." "Elephant." After seeing enough cards *with their answers*, you start recognising animals on your own — even ones you have never seen before.

That is exactly how supervised learning works. A computer is shown thousands of examples where every example comes with a label — the correct answer attached. The computer studies the patterns: cats have pointy ears and whiskers; dogs have floppy ears and a wider nose. Gradually, it learns the rules by itself, just from the examples and their labels.

The word *supervised* simply means a teacher is watching — providing those correct answers during practice. Once training is finished, the teacher steps away and the computer tries on its own.

**Now you try:** Look at five photos of fruit. Can a friend guess which fruit you are thinking of if you only tell them "right" or "wrong" after each guess? That guessing game is supervised learning in action!

### binary_multiclass_classification (12.2s)

## Binary Multiclass Classification

**The Sorting Game**

Imagine you work at the post office on Animal Island. Every morning, a big bag of letters arrives. Your job: put each letter in the right mailbox.

At first, the island only had two animals — a cat and a dog. Every letter went into *one of two* boxes. Easy! That's called **binary classification** — choosing between exactly two things. "Is it this, or that?"

But then more animals moved in: a parrot, a fish, a rabbit, and a tortoise. Now you have *many* mailboxes. Each letter still goes into exactly one box — but now you're picking from a whole crowd. That's **multiclass classification** — choosing from three or more possibilities.

The clever part? The *rules* you use are the same. You look at clues on the letter — the handwriting, the smell, the little paw print — and match them to the right animal. More animals just means more clues to learn, more boxes to check.

In machine learning, binary is "yes or no," and multiclass is "which one?"

**Now you try:** Look around the room. Pick five objects and sort them into groups. How many groups did you need?

### information_entropy (11.3s)

## Information Entropy

**The Guessing Game**

Imagine your friend hides a coin under one of two cups. You get one guess — easy! But what if they hide it under one of a thousand cups? Now you need lots of guesses. The *harder* it is to guess, the more *surprising* the answer feels when you finally find it.

**What Surprise Has to Do with Information**

When something surprising happens, it tells you *a lot*. If your teacher says "school is open today," you already expected that — not much new information. But if she says "school is cancelled because a bear walked in," that's very surprising, so it tells you a ton!

Information entropy is just a way of measuring how surprising a situation is, *on average*. A fair coin flip is very surprising — heads or tails are equally likely, so you never know. A trick coin that always lands heads? No surprise at all. Zero entropy.

More randomness → more surprise → higher entropy.

**The Rule**

High entropy = hard to predict = lots of information when you find out.
Low entropy = easy to predict = boring, nothing new to learn.

**Now You Try**

Which has higher entropy: rolling a fair six-sided die, or flipping a coin? Why?

### sigmoid_function (15.6s)

## Sigmoid Function

**Motivating story:** Imagine your friend asks, "Are you *sure* you want pizza for lunch?" You might say "a little sure," "pretty sure," or "totally sure." You never say "infinity sure" — feelings like certainty always stay somewhere between *not at all* and *completely*.

**Intuition:** The sigmoid function works the same way. Feed it any number — even a huge one like 1 000 or a tiny one like −1 000 — and it politely squishes the answer between 0 and 1. Numbers near zero come out near 0.5 ("meh, maybe"). Very large numbers come out near 1 ("yes, definitely!"). Very negative numbers come out near 0 ("no way!"). Draw it and you get a smooth, lazy **S** shape — that's why it's sometimes called the *S-curve*.

**Minimal formalisation:** Mathematically we write:

$$\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$$

The $e^{-x}$ part is what does the squishing. You don't need to memorise that yet — just remember: *any input, output always between 0 and 1*.

**Now you try:** Guess what $\sigma(0)$ equals. Hint: plug in $x = 0$ and see what happens to the fraction. Does your answer feel right for "I'm exactly 50/50 on pizza"?

### decision_tree (9.1s)

## Decision Tree

**Picture this:** You wake up on a Saturday morning and wonder — should I play outside today? You peek at the sky. Is it sunny? If yes, great, let's go! If it's raining, you stay in. But wait — even if it's sunny, is it too hot? If it's scorching, maybe you'll swim instead of running around. Each question you ask leads you down a different path, until you land on a decision.

That's a decision tree — a flowchart of questions where each answer opens a new branch, just like a real tree splitting into branches.

**Why it feels right:** Your brain already does this naturally. "If hungry → eat. If tired → sleep." A decision tree just draws that thinking out loud, so everyone can see the path you took.

**The key idea:** Each branching point is called a *node*, each answer is a *branch*, and where you finally land is called a *leaf* — your decision!

**Now you try:** Draw your own "What should I eat for lunch?" decision tree. Start with: *Am I hungry?* What questions come next?

### logistic_regression (11.1s)

## Logistic Regression

**Will it rain today?** Your friend looks outside and says, "There are *a lot* of dark clouds — I'm pretty sure it will rain." She isn't saying *exactly* how much rain. She's saying: **yes or no, and how confident am I?**

That's logistic regression. Instead of predicting a number (like temperature), it predicts a *probability* — a number between 0 and 1 — of something being **true or false**.

Imagine a sliding dial. When you see zero dark clouds, the dial sits near 0 (probably no rain). As more clouds pile up, the dial slides toward 1 (almost certainly rain). The dial never goes past 0 or 1, because probability can't be less than "impossible" or more than "certain."

Mathematically, the dial follows an S-shaped curve called the **sigmoid**:

$$P = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

where $z$ combines all your clues (clouds, humidity, wind) into one score. The S-curve squishes any score into the $[0, 1]$ range.

**Now you try:** Think of three clues you'd use to predict whether your team wins a game. Which clue do you think matters most, and why?

## Heart Disease Classification

Imagine your school nurse sees hundreds of kids every day. After a while, she starts noticing patterns: kids who stay up late, eat lots of chips, and never run around tend to feel worse. She doesn't examine every cell in their body — she just looks at a few clues and makes a smart guess.

Doctors do the same thing with hearts. They measure simple things: How fast does your heart beat? Is your blood pushing too hard against your veins? Do you get tired climbing stairs? Each clue is like a puzzle piece.

A computer can learn from thousands of past patients — people whose hearts turned out healthy or unhealthy — and find the patterns a human might miss. This is called **classification**: sorting things into groups. Here, the two groups are "likely has heart disease" and "probably healthy."

The computer is like a very experienced nurse who has seen *millions* of kids, not just hundreds. It learns which combinations of clues matter most.

Formally, each patient becomes a list of numbers (features), and the classifier assigns a **label**: $y = 0$ (healthy) or $y = 1$ (disease).

**Now you try:** List three things a doctor might measure about your heart. Which one do you think matters most, and why?
