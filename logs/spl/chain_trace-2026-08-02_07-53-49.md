### big_data (0.1s)

## Big Data

**Definition.** A dataset is colloquially called *big data* when its volume, velocity, or variety (the "three Vs" of Laney 2001) causes conventional in-memory, single-machine algorithms to fail in finite time or memory. More precisely, let $n$ denote the number of observations and $d$ the ambient dimension. Classical statistical learning theory — rooted in VC-dimension bounds and Hoeffding-type concentration — assumes $n$ and $d$ are fixed constants relative to available RAM. The big-data regime instead studies the limit $n, d \to \infty$ jointly, often with $d/n \to \gamma \in (0, \infty)$, or $n$ so large that a single pass over the data is the only feasible operation.

**A Central Theorem: Streaming Lower Bounds.** Much of the theoretical backbone of big-data computation comes from *communication complexity* and *sketching theory*. A foundational result is the following.

**Theorem (Alon–Matias–Szegedy, 1999).** Any randomized streaming algorithm that computes the $\ell_2$ frequency moment $F_2 = \sum_i f_i^2$ of a stream of $n$ elements drawn from universe $[m]$ with constant success probability requires $\Omega(\min(n, m))$ bits of space in the worst case.

*Proof sketch.* Reduce from the two-party communication problem INDEX: Alice holds $x \in \{0,1\}^m$, Bob holds index $j$, and they must compute $x_j$ with $O(1)$ rounds. Any streaming algorithm for $F_2$ can be converted to a protocol for INDEX by having Alice simulate the stream for her elements and send the memory state; Bob reads off the answer. Since INDEX requires $\Omega(m)$ bits of communication (Shannon lower bound on information), the space lower bound follows. $\square$

This result established that *exact* computation of basic statistics is information-theoretically expensive, forcing the field toward $\varepsilon$-approximation algorithms: the celebrated **Count-Min Sketch**, **AMS Sketch**, and **Johnson–Lindenstrauss random projections** all achieve $\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})$ space at the cost of a $(1 \pm \varepsilon)$ multiplicative error.

**Literature Context.** The AMS paper launched a program that now encompasses sublinear algorithms, compressed sensing (Candès–Romberg–Tao 2006; Donoho 2006), and the random-matrix theory of high-dimensional statistics (Marchenko–Pastur law; Bai–Silverstein 2010). Open questions remain sharp: optimal space-time tradeoffs for graph streaming (Assadi 2022), tight bounds for distributed statistical estimation under communication constraints (Han–Özgür–Weissman 2018), and whether stochastic gradient descent on non-convex losses provably converges in the $n \gg d$ streaming regime without multiple passes.

**Suggested Investigation.** Implement the AMS $F_2$ estimator and the Count-Min Sketch from scratch on a publicly available log or text corpus (e.g., Wikipedia page-view streams or the KDD Cup click-log). Measure empirical error as a function of the space parameter $w$ (number of hash buckets) and the number of independent hash functions $d$, comparing against the theoretical $\delta$-failure bound $\delta = e^{-d}$ and $\varepsilon$-error bound $\varepsilon = \sqrt{e/w}$. Then stress-test by injecting heavy-hitter skew (Zipf-distributed frequencies) and characterize the breakdown point. As an extension, benchmark against the mergeable summary framework of Agarwal et al. (2013), which supports parallel and distributed aggregation — directly relevant to MapReduce and Spark pipelines.

**Report-Writing Note.** A write-up of this investigation should: (1) state the space lower bound formally and identify which step in the proof is tight; (2) present empirical error curves with confidence intervals alongside the theoretical envelope; (3) discuss the gap between worst-case guarantees and average-case behavior on real corpora; and (4) position the findings relative to at least one system-level result — e.g., whether the theoretical constants translate to measurable latency differences at the scale of $10^8$ stream elements.

## Machine Learning Model

**Definition.** A *machine learning model* is a parameterized family of functions $\mathcal{F} = \{f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} \mid \theta \in \Theta\}$, together with a learning algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ that, given a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n \subset \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$, produces a parameter estimate $\hat{\theta} = \mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})$ minimizing some loss functional $\mathcal{L} : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$. Formally, learning solves

$$\hat{\theta} \in \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x_i), y_i) + \lambda R(\theta),$$

where $R : \Theta \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is a regularizer and $\lambda \geq 0$ controls the bias–variance tradeoff. The triple $(\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{L}, \mathcal{A})$ constitutes the *model specification*; the result $f_{\hat\theta}$ is the *fitted model*. This formulation subsumes linear regression, kernel machines, and deep neural networks as special cases by varying $\Theta$, $\mathcal{L}$, and $\mathcal{A}$.

**Theorem (Generalization bound — PAC-Bayes, Catoni 2007).** Let $P$ be a prior over $\Theta$ independent of $\mathcal{D}$, and let $Q$ be any posterior. With probability at least $1-\delta$ over draws of $\mathcal{D}$,

$$\mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim Q}[R(f_\theta)] \;\leq\; \mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim Q}[\hat{R}(f_\theta)] + \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q \| P) + \ln(1/\delta)}{2n}},$$

where $\hat{R}$ denotes empirical risk and $R$ denotes population risk. The bound makes the usual assumption of i.i.d. draws from a fixed distribution $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply the change-of-measure inequality $\mathbb{E}_Q[g] \leq \mathrm{KL}(Q\|P) + \ln \mathbb{E}_P[e^g]$ to the random variable $g = n\hat{R}(f_\theta) - nR(f_\theta)$, then invoke Hoeffding's lemma on the bounded loss, and invert the resulting Chernoff bound. $\square$

**Literature context.** Vapnik's VC theory (1971) established dimension-based generalization, but PAC-Bayes bounds (McAllester 1999; Catoni 2007) are tighter in practice and admit data-dependent posteriors. A central open problem is the *overparameterized regime*: modern neural networks with $|\Theta| \gg n$ generalize well empirically yet violate classical bounds, a phenomenon Belkin et al. (2019) formalize as *double descent*. Implicit regularization by stochastic gradient descent — studied via the *edge of stability* (Cohen et al. 2021) and *sharpness-aware minimization* (Foret et al. 2021) — is an active research front without a complete theoretical account. The relationship between architecture inductive bias and the effective dimensionality of $\Theta$ remains an open question linking statistical learning theory to deep learning practice.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the double-descent curve on a controlled setup: train a two-layer MLP on MNIST as width $d$ increases from $d \ll n$ to $d \gg n$, recording test loss at the interpolation threshold $d^* \approx n$. Compute empirical PAC-Bayes bounds using a Gaussian prior and a Laplace-approximation posterior. Observe whether the bound tracks the actual test error across regimes, or fails to explain the benign overfitting region. Vary the label-noise rate to sharpen the discrepancy.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should (i) state precisely which version of the PAC-Bayes theorem is used and verify its assumptions hold; (ii) plot bound tightness versus model complexity on the same axes as the double-descent curve; (iii) discuss whether the bound's failure in the overparameterized regime is a fundamental limitation of the KL term, a prior-choice artifact, or an artifact of the bound's form; and (iv) connect findings to at least one current paper on implicit bias, citing specific theoretical gaps the experiment either supports or challenges.

## Data Mining

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\} \subset \mathcal{X}$ be a finite sample drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution $P$ over a measurable space $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F})$. Data mining is the algorithmic extraction of *statistically significant*, *previously unknown*, and *actionable* patterns from $\mathcal{D}$. Formally, a pattern is a statement $\varphi$ in some language $\mathcal{L}$ such that (i) $\varphi$ holds on a non-negligible fraction of $\mathcal{D}$, quantified by a *support* threshold $\sigma \in (0,1]$, and (ii) $\varphi$ would not be expected to hold under a null model $P_0$ at significance level $\alpha$. The tension between (i) and (ii) is not cosmetic: it defines the core statistical challenge of the field.

**Theorem (Bonferroni bottleneck).** Let $|\mathcal{L}|$ be the cardinality of the pattern language. If each pattern $\varphi_i$ is tested independently at level $\alpha$ under $P_0$, the expected number of false positives is $|\mathcal{L}| \cdot \alpha$. For association-rule mining with binary itemsets over $d$ items, $|\mathcal{L}| = 3^d - 2^{d+1} + 1$, which grows exponentially in $d$.

*Proof sketch.* By linearity of expectation and the union bound, $\mathbb{E}[\text{false positives}] = \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{L}|} \Pr[\text{reject } H_0^{(i)} \mid H_0^{(i)} \text{ true}] = |\mathcal{L}| \cdot \alpha$. The itemset count follows from inclusion-exclusion on the power set $2^{[d]}$, subtracting singletons and the empty set. $\square$

This observation underpins every modern correction strategy: Bonferroni control of the family-wise error rate (FWER), Benjamini–Hochberg control of the false discovery rate (FDR), and the more recent *pattern-team* approaches that treat the selected set as a random variable and bound its risk directly (Hämäläinen, 2012; Webb, 2007).

**Literature context.** The Apriori algorithm (Agrawal & Srikant, 1994) established the antimonotonicity of support as a pruning device, reducing exponential search to tractable lattice traversal. FP-Growth (Han et al., 2000) eliminated repeated database scans via a compressed tree structure, achieving near-linear scaling in practice. However, neither addresses statistical validity: a pattern with support $\sigma$ on $\mathcal{D}$ may not generalize to $P$. The *statistically valid pattern mining* (SVPM) programme (Terada et al., 2013; Llinares-López et al., 2015) reframes mining as a multiple-testing problem under a Westfall–Young permutation framework, enabling FWER control without sacrificing power by restricting the family to *testable* patterns — those whose support falls above the minimum testable threshold given $n$ and $\alpha$.

An open question concerns the *power–interpretability frontier*: deep representation-learning methods (autoencoders, transformer embeddings) identify latent patterns that classical itemset miners miss, but these patterns resist the propositional form that SVPM requires. Bridging distributional representations with statistically grounded significance tests remains unsolved.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement Apriori and FP-Growth on a real tabular dataset (e.g., UCI Adult or a local transaction log). For each discovered rule $X \Rightarrow Y$, compute the chi-squared statistic against the independence null and apply Bonferroni and BH corrections. Plot the number of surviving rules against $\sigma$ to locate the *elbow* at which inflation artifacts dominate. Optionally, replicate the LAMP algorithm (Terada et al., 2013) and compare its testable-pattern set against the Bonferroni-corrected set for the same data.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) the empirical relationship between $\sigma$ and rule count, demonstrating Bonferroni inflation; (2) a clean table comparing FWER-controlled and FDR-controlled rule sets; (3) a discussion of whether the surviving rules are interpretable and actionable by a domain expert. If the LAMP replication is included, quantify the power gain — number of rules recovered — relative to naïve Bonferroni at identical FWER, providing a concrete data point on the cost of ignoring testability.
