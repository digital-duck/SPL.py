### sample_data (11.9s)

## Sample Data

**The Cookie Taste Test**

Imagine your school is holding a bake sale and wants to know which cookie everyone likes best. But there are 500 students — you can't ask every single one before tomorrow!

So instead, you ask 20 friends from different grades. Their answers are your **sample data**.

**What's happening here?**

The whole school is called the *population* — every person you *could* ask. But asking everyone takes forever, costs too much, or simply isn't possible. A *sample* is a smaller group you actually do ask.

Sample data is the information you collect from that smaller group. The magic is this: if you pick your 20 friends fairly — not just your best friends who all love chocolate chip — your sample data can *predict* what the whole school thinks.

**The key rule:** your sample should be *representative*, meaning it looks like a mini-version of the whole group.

### population_data (11.9s)

## Population Data

**The Town That Counted Itself**

Imagine your whole town decides to answer one question: *How many people live here?* Every family fills out a form. Every apartment gets counted. When all those answers come together — that collection of numbers, names, and facts about *every single person* — that's called **population data**.

**What does "population" really mean?**

In everyday life, "population" means all the people in a place. But in math, population means *everyone or everything you're studying* — it could be every fish in a lake, every book in a library, or every student in your school.

**Population data** is simply the information gathered from *that whole group* — not just a few, but all of them.

**Why does this matter?**

If you want to know the most common birthday month in your class, you'd ask *every* classmate — not just your three best friends. Those answers from everyone form your population data, and it gives you the most complete, honest picture possible.

**Now you try**

Think of one question you'd want to ask everyone in your school. What group are you studying? What information would you collect? You just designed your first population data set!

### mean (10.6s)

## Mean

**Picture this.** Five friends go trick-or-treating. They come home with 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 candies. It feels unfair — some have lots, some have little. What if they pooled everything together and shared equally? How many would each friend get?

**The idea.** The *mean* answers exactly that question: *if everyone had the same amount, what would it be?* It is the "perfectly fair share."

**How it works.** Collect everything into one pile, then deal it out evenly.

$$\text{mean} = \frac{\text{sum of all values}}{\text{number of values}}$$

For our friends: $2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 10 = 30$ candies total, shared among $5$ friends.

$$\text{mean} = \frac{30}{5} = 6$$

Each friend would get **6 candies** — no one gains, no one loses; the total stays the same.

**Now you try.** Your class scored these points in a game: $3, 7, 5, 9, 1$. If you shared the points equally, how many would each person get? Add them up, count the players, divide — and check whether the total is preserved.

### median (10.0s)

## Median

Imagine five friends line up by height: short, shorter-still, medium, tall, tallest. Who stands exactly in the middle? The one with two friends on each side — that's the **median**.

It's the value right in the center of a sorted list, not the average. Why does that matter? Suppose four kids earn $1 in allowance and one lucky kid earns $100. The average is about $20 — but nobody actually *gets* $20. The median is $1, which tells the true story of what most kids experience.

The key move is always the same: **sort first, then find the middle**.

- Odd number of values: the middle one wins.  
  $\{3, 7, 9\}$ → median $= 7$

- Even number of values: average the two middle ones.  
  $\{3, 7, 9, 11\}$ → median $= \dfrac{7+9}{2} = 8$

**Now you try.** Five students scored $62, 95, 70, 88, 70$ on a quiz. Sort the scores from smallest to largest, then find the median. Does it feel like a "typical" score to you?

### percentiles (10.8s)

## Percentiles

**The race to the finish line**

Imagine 100 kids line up by height, shortest in front, tallest at the back. You walk to your spot and count how many kids are shorter than you. If 70 kids are shorter, you are at the **70th percentile**. That one number tells the whole story: you are taller than 70 out of every 100 kids.

**Why does this feel right?**

A plain height like "140 cm" is almost meaningless alone — is that tall or short? A percentile *compares* you to everyone else at once. It turns a lonely number into a position in the crowd.

**The idea in one sentence**

Your percentile is the percentage of people you *beat* in that ranking. More formally, if you are at the $p$-th percentile, then $p\%$ of the group scored at or below your value.

$$\text{percentile rank} = \frac{\text{number of values} \leq \text{your value}}{\text{total number of values}} \times 100$$

**Now you try**

Ten friends finish a spelling test. Their scores are 4, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10. You scored 8. How many scored at or below 8? What is your percentile rank?

### probability (11.1s)

## Probability

**The Cookie Jar**

Imagine you reach into a cookie jar without looking. There are 3 chocolate cookies and 1 plain cookie. Which kind are you *most likely* to grab?

You'd probably guess chocolate — and you'd be right. But why? Because most of the cookies are chocolate! The more of something there is, the better your chances of getting it.

**The Big Idea**

Probability is just a fancy word for *how likely something is to happen*. Think of it like a scale from "no chance" to "definitely will happen." If something is impossible, like rolling a 7 on a normal die, we say the probability is $0$. If something is certain, like the sun rising tomorrow, we say the probability is $1$. Everything else lives somewhere in between.

**A Little Math**

We measure probability as a fraction:

$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{ways it can happen}}{\text{total possible outcomes}}$$

Back to the cookie jar: $P(\text{chocolate}) = \frac{3}{4}$.

**Now You Try**

You have a bag with 2 red marbles and 3 blue marbles. If you grab one without looking, what is $P(\text{red})$? What is $P(\text{blue})$? Which color are you more likely to pick?

### random_variable (15.0s)

## Random Variable

**The mystery bag**

Imagine you have a bag with three marbles: one red, one blue, one green. You reach in without looking and grab one. Before you pull it out — what color do you have?

You don't know yet. It *could* be any of the three. The color you'll get is hiding, waiting to be revealed.

That mystery — a value that hasn't been decided yet — is what mathematicians call a **random variable**. A random variable isn't a fixed number like $5$ or $12$. It's more like a *question with several possible answers*, where chance decides which answer shows up. We write it with a capital letter, like $X$, to remind ourselves it still holds a surprise.

The moment you pull the marble out and see blue, we say the variable has been *realized*: $X = \text{blue}$. Before that moment, $X$ was all possibilities at once. After, it is exactly one thing.

### variance (10.6s)

## Variance

**Picture this.** Every morning, two friends race to school. Maya always arrives in exactly 10 minutes. Leo sometimes sprints in 5 minutes, sometimes dawdles for 15 — but his *average* is also 10 minutes. Same average, very different stories.

**The intuition.** Variance measures how *spread out* or *wiggly* a set of numbers is around their average. Maya's times are tight — low variance. Leo's times jump around — high variance. Think of it as asking: "How surprised will I be by the next number?"

**Formalising just a little.** If your average is $\mu$, variance asks how far each value $x$ strays from it. You square each gap (so negatives don't cancel positives), then average those squares:

$$\text{Variance} = \frac{(x_1 - \mu)^2 + (x_2 - \mu)^2 + \cdots}{n}$$

A small variance means values hug the average. A large variance means they wander far.

**Now you try.** Collect five friends' shoe sizes. Find the average. Which sizes are far from it? Do you think the variance will be large or small?

### probability_distribution (11.3s)

## Probability Distribution

Imagine you have a bag of 10 marbles: 5 red, 3 blue, and 2 yellow. Every time you reach in without looking, you pull one out, then put it back. Do this a hundred times — which color shows up most? Red, almost definitely. Yellow, barely at all.

That pattern — which outcomes show up and *how often* — is called a **probability distribution**. It's like a personality portrait of randomness. It doesn't tell you what *will* happen next, but it tells you what to *expect* over many tries.

Think of it as a scoreboard nature keeps for every possible outcome. Each outcome gets a share of 100%, and all the shares must add up to exactly $100\%$ (or $1$, in math language):

$$P(\text{red}) + P(\text{blue}) + P(\text{yellow}) = 1$$

Some distributions are lopsided (like our marble bag). Others are perfectly even, like a fair coin: heads and tails each get $\frac{1}{2}$.

**Now you try:** Roll a six-sided die 30 times and tally each number. Does each face show up about $\frac{1}{6}$ of the time? What does your tally chart look like — flat or bumpy?

### quartiles (8.6s)

## Quartiles

**Picture this:** Your class just finished a math test. You line up all 20 scores from lowest to highest along the floor like stepping stones. Now your teacher wants to find the "middle of the lower half" and the "middle of the upper half" — not just the overall middle. Those special spots are called **quartiles**.

**The intuition:** Quartiles slice your data into four equal groups — like cutting a sandwich into four pieces. The first cut gives you the lower quarter, the middle cut is the median, and the third cut separates the upper quarter from the rest.

**A little more precisely:** Sort your values from smallest to largest. Find the middle value — that's $Q_2$ (the median). Now find the middle of the lower half: that's $Q_1$. Find the middle of the upper half: that's $Q_3$. Together, $Q_1$, $Q_2$, and $Q_3$ divide your data into four equal parts.

Scores below $Q_1$: bottom 25%. Above $Q_3$: top 25%.

**Now you try:** Write down the ages of everyone in your family from youngest to oldest. Can you find $Q_1$, $Q_2$, and $Q_3$?

### standard_deviation (12.6s)

## Standard Deviation

**The cookie mystery.** Mrs. Rivera baked cookies for two classes. Class A got cookies weighing 10, 10, 10, 10, and 10 grams — perfectly identical. Class B got cookies weighing 2, 6, 10, 14, and 18 grams — wildly different, but the average was still 10 grams! Both classes averaged the same. So why did Class B feel so unfair?

**The spread is the secret.** The average tells you the *center*, but not how far things *wander* from it. Standard deviation is your "wandering score" — it measures how spread out values are around the average.

Think of it as asking: *On a typical day, how far am I from home?* If you always stay on your block, your wandering score is tiny. If you roam across town, it's huge.

For Class B, each cookie's distance from 10 grams is: $|2-10|=8$, $|6-10|=4$, $|10-10|=0$, $|14-10|=4$, $|18-10|=8$. Those gaps are big — so the standard deviation is big. For Class A, every gap is $0$.

**Now you try.** Five friends scored 5, 7, 5, 7, and 6 on a quiz. The average is 6. How far is each score from 6? Who has the smallest gap?

## Python Data Analysis

**The Lemonade Stand Mystery**

Imagine you sell lemonade all summer. Every day you write down how many cups you sold. By August, you have a notebook crammed with numbers — but you can't *see* anything. Which day was your best? Which week was slowest? Your eyes just swim.

**Here's the secret:** a computer can read all those numbers in one blink and show you the story hiding inside them.

That's what Python data analysis does. Python is a friendly programming language, and with it you can load your notebook of numbers, ask questions like "what's the average?" or "show me the worst day," and get a picture back — a chart, a table, a clear answer.

**Think of it like a super-powered magnifying glass.** Raw numbers are just dots. Python turns those dots into a picture you can understand.

In Python, we often use a tool called **pandas** to hold our data in neat rows and columns — like a spreadsheet that can talk back.
