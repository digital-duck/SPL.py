### neuron (0.1s)

## Neuron

You've probably heard that the brain is made of billions of neurons that "pass signals" to each other. That picture is right — but what does a neuron actually *look like* as a mathematical object?

The key idea is the **weighted sum**. An artificial neuron receives several input values, weighs each one by importance, and adds them up. If a neuron has inputs $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ with weights $w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n$, it computes:

$$z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_n x_n + b$$

The extra term $b$ is the **bias** — think of it as the neuron's personal offset, shifting when it "wants" to activate, like adjusting how sensitive a smoke alarm is. Together, the weights and bias are the only numbers a neuron *learns* during training; the inputs come from outside, but $w$ and $b$ are what the network tunes to get better answers.

Once $z$ is computed, the neuron passes it through an **activation function** to produce its output. You'll explore specific activation functions in the next section — for now, the important thing to know is that without one, stacking many neurons would still give you nothing more powerful than basic algebra. The activation function is what gives neural networks their reach.

The pattern to hold onto: every neuron takes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and transforms the result. Thousands of these steps, layered together, are what make deep learning work.

### bias (0.1s)

## Bias

You already have the right instinct: bias means something is *consistently* off in one direction. A bathroom scale that always reads two pounds too heavy isn't just inaccurate on a bad day — it is systematically wrong every single time you step on it. That persistence is what makes it bias, not just noise.

In statistics and machine learning, we make this precise. Suppose you are trying to estimate some true quantity — call it $\theta$ (a population mean, a model parameter, anything). You run an experiment and produce an estimate $\hat{\theta}$. The **bias** of that estimate is defined as

$$\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$$

where $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]$ is the *expected value* of your estimate — roughly, what you'd get on average if you repeated the experiment many times. If that average lands exactly on the truth, bias is zero and we call the estimator **unbiased**. If it consistently undershoots or overshoots, bias is nonzero.

A classic example: the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ is an unbiased estimator of the true population mean $\mu$, because $\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}] = \mu$. However, if you tried to estimate the population variance by dividing by $n$ instead of $n-1$, you would get a biased result — your estimate would be systematically too small.

The key insight is that bias is about the *average* error, not any single measurement. Random errors can cancel out; biased errors pile up.

### weight (0.2s)

## Weight

You already know that not everything counts equally. A final exam worth 40 % of your grade matters more than a single quiz worth 5 %. That everyday idea is exactly what mathematicians mean by *weight*.

### The Structure Behind It

A weight is a number that says *how much influence* one value has on a total. When you combine several values, each multiplied by its weight, you get a **weighted sum**:

$$S = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_n x_n$$

Here $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ are the values and $w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n$ are their weights. If you want a **weighted average**, divide that sum by the total weight:

$$\bar{x} = \frac{w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_n x_n}{w_1 + w_2 + \cdots + w_n}$$

When all weights are equal, this reduces to the ordinary average you already know. Weights just let you tip the scale in a controlled, precise way.

### The Pattern to Remember

Larger weight → larger pull on the result. If you double one weight and leave everything else unchanged, that term's contribution doubles. This makes weights incredibly useful anywhere priorities differ — grading, recipe scaling, training a neural network, even ranking search results.

### activation_function (0.2s)

## Activation Function

You already have the intuition: a neuron in a neural network receives signals, adds them up, and then decides whether to "fire." The activation function is the mechanism behind that decision — it takes the weighted sum arriving at a neuron and transforms it into an output that gets passed forward.

Without an activation function, stacking layers of a neural network would be pointless. Each layer would just compute a linear combination of its inputs, and no matter how many layers you add, the whole network collapses into a single linear map. Activation functions introduce **nonlinearity**, which is what lets deep networks learn curves, boundaries, and complex patterns.

One of the most common modern choices is the **ReLU** (Rectified Linear Unit):

$$f(x) = \max(0,\, x)$$

It is beautifully simple: negative inputs become zero, positive inputs pass through unchanged. Another classic is the **sigmoid**, which squashes any real number into the range $(0, 1)$:

$$\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$$

Sigmoid is useful when you need to interpret an output as a probability. Both functions share a key property: they are nonlinear, so composing many of them across layers creates expressive, flexible models.

A useful mental rule: **ReLU** is the default workhorse for hidden layers; **sigmoid** (or its cousin softmax) belongs at the output when you need probabilities.

### layers (0.2s)

## Layers

You have probably heard that a neural network is "deep" because it has many layers. But what is a layer, really — and why does stacking them matter?

Think of each layer as one transformation step: it takes the numbers coming in, mixes them using learned **weights**, and passes the result forward. One layer can catch a simple pattern; stack several, and each one learns to recognize features built on top of what the previous layer found — edges early on, shapes in the middle, objects by the end.

The key new object here is the **weight matrix** $W^{(l)}$. If the previous layer produced a vector $\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}$, then layer $l$ starts by computing

$$W^{(l)}\,\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}$$

That matrix–vector product is what "mixing" means precisely: each row of $W^{(l)}$ takes a dot product with the incoming vector and produces one output number. (Layers also add a small shift and pass the result through a bending function before sending it on, but the weight matrix is the engine of the transformation.)

Because $W^{(l)}$ has shape $(\text{output size} \times \text{input size})$, it can change how many numbers flow to the next layer. One rule follows immediately: the output size of layer $l$ must equal the input size of layer $l+1$. Mismatched dimensions are the most common error when building a network by hand.

### neural_network (0.2s)

## Neural Network

You've probably heard that neural networks are "inspired by the brain." True — but let's look past the metaphor at what they actually *do*.

**The structure.** A neural network is organized into **layers**: an input layer (say, pixel brightness values from an image), one or more **hidden layers** in between, and an output layer that produces a prediction (say, "cat" or "dog"). Each layer is made of **neurons**, and each neuron runs the same simple computation.

**The one new idea.** Here it is: a neuron takes a **weighted sum** of its inputs, adds a constant called a **bias**, and passes the result through an activation function. With two inputs $x_1$ and $x_2$:

$$z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$$

Then the neuron's output is $a = f(z)$. The activation function used almost everywhere today is **ReLU**: $f(z) = \max(0,\, z)$. If $z$ is negative, the neuron outputs 0; if $z$ is positive, it passes $z$ through unchanged.

**The simple rule.** Everything interesting in a neural network comes from stacking many of these neurons together. The weights $w_i$ and biases $b$ are the numbers the network gradually adjusts as it sees more and more examples — all of the network's "knowledge" lives in them.

**Practice problem.** A neuron has inputs $x_1 = 3$ and $x_2 = -1$, weights $w_1 = 0.5$ and $w_2 = 2$, and bias $b = 1$. Compute $z$. Then apply ReLU. What is the neuron's output $a$?

### perceptron (0.2s)

## Perceptron

You have probably heard that a neuron "fires" when it gets enough input. A perceptron captures that idea in math: it adds up weighted signals and decides **yes** or **no** based on the total.

Formally, suppose you have inputs $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ and matching weights $w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n$. The perceptron computes a **weighted sum**:

$$z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_n x_n + b$$

where $b$ is a **bias** — a constant that shifts the threshold, so the decision boundary does not have to pass through the origin. Think of $z$ as a total score. The perceptron then applies one simple rule:

$$\hat{y} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } z \geq 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } z < 0 \end{cases}$$

Score above zero → output 1 ("yes"). Score below zero → output 0 ("no").

The weights are the key: each $w_i$ controls how much input $x_i$ matters. A large positive weight means "this input pushes strongly toward yes." A negative weight means "this input pushes toward no." The bias lets you fine-tune the tipping point independently of the inputs themselves.

A single perceptron draws one straight boundary between two classes — it can separate "yes" examples from "no" examples only when that boundary is a line (or a flat plane in higher dimensions).

### depth (0.2s)

## Depth

You already sense what depth means: it's how far down something sits. A grandfather is closer to the top of a family tree than a grandchild. The CEO is one level from the root of an org chart; a junior analyst might be four levels down. That everyday intuition is exactly right — now let's sharpen it into something you can calculate.

In a **tree** (a connected graph with no cycles), every node has a precise depth. We define it recursively:

- The **root** has depth $0$.
- Every other node has depth equal to its parent's depth plus one.

So if node $A$ is the root and $B$ is $A$'s child, then $\text{depth}(B) = 1$. If $C$ is $B$'s child, $\text{depth}(C) = 2$, and so on. Formally:

$$\text{depth}(v) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } v \text{ is the root} \\ \text{depth}(\text{parent}(v)) + 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

This recursive rule is powerful because it works on trees of any shape — balanced or lopsided, wide or narrow.

One key pattern: the **height** of a tree is the maximum depth among all its leaves. A tree with height $h$ can have at most $2^{h+1} - 1$ nodes if it's a perfect binary tree. Depth tells you about a single node; height summarizes the whole tree.

### multilayer_perceptron (0.2s)

## Multilayer Perceptron

**Recap:** You already know that a single neuron takes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and passes the result through an activation function. That one neuron is a capable building block — but most real problems are far too tangled for it to handle alone.

**Explore the structure:** The fix is to stack neurons into *layers*. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) organizes neurons into three roles:

- **Input layer** — receives raw data (pixel values, sensor readings)
- **Hidden layers** — transform the data step by step into more useful patterns
- **Output layer** — delivers the final answer or prediction

Each neuron in a hidden layer looks at every output from the layer before it and computes a weighted sum plus a bias — exactly like the single neuron you already know, just wired to a whole layer instead of one input. If neuron $j$ in layer $\ell$ receives activations $a_i^{(\ell-1)}$ from the previous layer, its pre-activation is:

$$z_j^{(\ell)} = \sum_i w_{ji}^{(\ell)}\, a_i^{(\ell-1)} + b_j^{(\ell)}$$

Then the neuron applies its activation function and sends the result to the next layer. Data flows forward through every layer until it reaches the output.

One critical point: nonlinear activations are what make stacking layers worthwhile. Without them, any stack of layers — no matter how deep — collapses into a single linear rule, gaining nothing from the depth.

**The pattern:** Each layer takes a step further from raw data toward meaning. Early layers detect low-level details; later layers combine those into higher-level ideas. More layers → richer representations.

### artificial_intelligence (0.2s)

## Artificial Intelligence

You've probably heard that AI can write poems, recognize faces, or beat humans at chess. But what is it, actually, doing under the hood?

At its core, AI is about building systems that **learn patterns from data** and use those patterns to make decisions. Unlike a regular program — which follows explicit rules a programmer wrote — an AI program discovers its own rules by studying examples.

### From Examples to Rules

The most common approach is called **supervised learning**. You feed the system a large set of labeled examples: thousands of images tagged "cat" or "not cat." The system adjusts internal numbers called **weights** (written $w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n$) until its predictions match the labels. The goal is to minimize a **loss function** $L$, which measures how wrong the system is:

$$L = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2$$

Here $y_i$ is the correct answer, $\hat{y}_i$ is the system's guess, and $N$ is the number of examples. A smaller $L$ means better predictions.

### The Simple Pattern

AI training follows a loop: **predict → measure error → adjust weights → repeat**. Each pass through the data nudges the weights in a direction that reduces $L$. After thousands of passes, the system generalizes — it can handle examples it has never seen before.

### deep_learning (0.1s)

## Deep Learning

You already know that a single artificial neuron takes inputs, weights them, adds a bias, and passes the result through an activation function. Deep learning's central idea is to stack many such layers so that each one learns increasingly abstract features — early layers spot edges and short patterns, later layers combine those into shapes, then objects, then full concepts.

The structure to focus on is the **feedforward layer**. When a layer receives $n$ inputs packed into a vector $\mathbf{x}$, its learned weights form a matrix $W$ and its biases a vector $\mathbf{b}$. The layer computes:

$$\mathbf{a} = f(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$$

Here $f$ is an activation function applied entry-by-entry to the result. A popular choice is ReLU, which keeps positive values and zeroes out negatives — cheap to compute and surprisingly good at helping signals survive many layers without shrinking away.

Chain several such layers together and you get a deep network: $\mathbf{a}^{(1)} \to \mathbf{a}^{(2)} \to \cdots \to \hat{y}$. The golden rule: *depth lets a network compose simple transformations into complex ones*, the same way $(f \circ g \circ h)(x)$ can describe curves no single function handles cleanly.

### natural_language_processing (0.1s)

## Natural Language Processing

You already know that computers speak in 0s and 1s, while humans speak in words, sentences, and sarcasm. Natural Language Processing — NLP for short — is the field that bridges that gap: it gives machines the tools to read, interpret, and even generate human language.

**From words to numbers.** Computers can't work with the word *"cat"* directly — they need numbers. One foundational trick is called a **bag of words**: represent a sentence as a vector counting how many times each word appears. If your vocabulary has $V$ words, a sentence becomes a vector in $\mathbb{R}^V$. For example, with vocabulary {cat, sat, mat, dog}, the sentence *"the cat sat on the mat"* maps roughly to:

$$\mathbf{x} = [1,\ 1,\ 1,\ 0]$$

This loses word order but captures *what* words are present — surprisingly useful for tasks like spam detection.

**The pattern to notice.** Once language is a vector, all the algebra you're learning applies. Two documents with similar word-count vectors are likely about similar topics. You can measure this with the **cosine similarity**:

$$\text{similarity} = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|}$$

A value near $1$ means the documents are closely related; near $0$ means they share little vocabulary.

Modern NLP goes much further — transformers learn rich meaning from context — but every advanced system still builds on this core idea: *represent language as math, then do math on it.*

### speech_recognition_tts (16.3s)

## Speech Recognition Tts

You already know the basic idea: your phone "hears" you and turns your words into text, or reads a message out loud to you. What's actually happening under the hood is more interesting than magic — it's signal processing plus pattern matching, and the two directions are nearly mirror images of each other.

**From sound to text (ASR).** A microphone captures air-pressure changes as a waveform — a list of amplitude values sampled thousands of times per second. The system slices that waveform into short overlapping windows (typically 25 ms each) and extracts a compact fingerprint from each window called a **mel-frequency cepstral coefficient (MFCC)** vector. If a window has $D$ features, a whole utterance becomes a matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}$, where $T$ is the number of time steps. A neural network (often a transformer) maps this matrix to a sequence of tokens — first phonemes, then words.

**From text to speech (TTS).** The pipeline reverses: text is converted to phonemes using pronunciation rules, then a neural vocoder synthesizes a waveform that matches those phonemes with natural-sounding pitch and rhythm. Models like WaveNet predict each audio sample conditioned on the samples before it, producing surprisingly human output.

**The key pattern.** Both directions lean on the same idea: break the signal into small chunks, extract features from each chunk, and let a sequence model handle the ordering. The feature vector is the shared currency.
