### geospatial_data (27.7s)

## Geospatial Data

**Definition.** A *geospatial dataset* is a collection of observations $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{s}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ where each $\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ denotes a spatial location (typically $d = 2$ on the sphere $\mathbb{S}^2$ or in a projected plane) and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{R}^p$ for multivariate fields) is an attribute observed at that location. The defining feature is *spatial dependence*: the stochastic process $\{Y(\mathbf{s}) : \mathbf{s} \in \mathcal{S}\}$ generating the observations is assumed to satisfy Tobler's first law informally, and formally to violate the classical i.i.d. assumption in ways that are structured by proximity in $\mathcal{S}$.

**Core model and result.** The standard probabilistic framework models $Y(\mathbf{s})$ as a Gaussian process (GP), $Y \sim \mathcal{GP}(\mu(\cdot), k(\cdot, \cdot))$, with mean function $\mu$ and covariance kernel $k$. For a stationary and isotropic field, $k(\mathbf{s}_i, \mathbf{s}_j) = \sigma^2 \rho(\|\mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{s}_j\|)$ where $\rho : [0,\infty) \to [-1,1]$ is the correlation function. A central result governing valid choices of $\rho$ is Bochner's theorem:

> **Theorem (Bochner).** A continuous function $\rho : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ is positive definite if and only if it is the Fourier transform of a finite non-negative Borel measure $F$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$:
> $$\rho(\mathbf{h}) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i \boldsymbol{\omega}^\top \mathbf{h}} \, dF(\boldsymbol{\omega}).$$

This characterization is not merely theoretical machinery; it directly dictates which correlation families are permissible. The Matérn class $\rho_\nu(r) \propto r^\nu K_\nu(r)$, where $K_\nu$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind and $\nu > 0$ controls differentiability, is the canonical choice because it interpolates continuously between exponential ($\nu = 1/2$) and squared-exponential ($\nu \to \infty$) behavior and places $\nu$ as an estimable parameter. Stein (1999) established that the Matérn class is *optimal* for interpolation under infill asymptotics: only the microergodic parameters matter for prediction, not the full parameter vector.

**Literature context and open problems.** The classical treatment in Cressie (1993) and the modern statistical perspective in Stein (1999) treat $\mathcal{S}$ as Euclidean. When $\mathcal{S} = \mathbb{S}^2$ (global data), valid isotropic covariance functions are characterized by Schoenberg's representation in terms of Legendre polynomials, and the Matérn class does not directly transfer — an active research problem. A further open challenge is *non-stationarity*: real geospatial processes (land temperature, precipitation) exhibit spatial variation in their covariance structure. Approaches include spatially varying parameters (Paciorek & Schervish 2006), deformation models (Sampson & Guttorp 1992), and deep kernel learning, but scalable and theoretically grounded non-stationary models remain an open frontier. Computational scaling is a parallel challenge: naïve GP inference is $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$; sparse approximations (Vecchia, NNGP, inducing points) reduce this to $\mathcal{O}(n)$ or $\mathcal{O}(nm^2)$ but introduce approximation error whose spatial distribution is not fully characterized.

**Suggested investigation.** Obtain a publicly available spatial dataset (e.g., NOAA temperature stations, WorldClim precipitation grids). Fit a stationary Matérn GP by maximum likelihood, estimating $(\sigma^2, \nu, \ell)$ jointly. Then test for non-stationarity using the residual variogram computed over spatial quadrants. If evidence of non-stationarity is found, implement a simple spatially-varying lengthscale via a log-GP prior on $\ell(\mathbf{s})$ and compare predictive log-likelihoods on held-out stations.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) the estimated covariance parameters and their uncertainty (profile likelihood contours), (2) a variogram diagnostic plot comparing empirical and model-fitted semivariance, (3) a leave-one-out cross-validation comparison of stationary versus non-stationary models, and (4) a discussion of which parameters are microergodic and which are not identifiable under the sampling design used.

### spatial_heatmap (35.1s)

## Spatial Heatmap

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{D} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ be a bounded spatial domain and let $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ be a set of observations where $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathcal{D}$ and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar intensity. A *spatial heatmap* is any continuous surrogate $\hat{f} : \mathcal{D} \to \mathbb{R}$ that approximates an underlying field $f : \mathcal{D} \to \mathbb{R}$, rendered as a color-encoded surface. The construction of $\hat{f}$ from sparse observations is an interpolation or density estimation problem; the *display* of $\hat{f}$ is a perceptual mapping problem. Both carry non-trivial mathematical structure that is frequently collapsed into a single "plot a heatmap" step in applied work, obscuring important assumptions.

**Theorem (Kernel Density Estimator Bias–Variance Decomposition).** Let $f \in C^2(\mathcal{D})$ and let $\hat{f}_h(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{n h^2} \sum_{i=1}^n K\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i}{h}\right)$ be the kernel density estimator with bandwidth $h > 0$ and kernel $K$ satisfying $\int K = 1$, $\int \mathbf{u} K(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, $\int \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 K(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u} = \sigma_K^2 < \infty$. Then:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat{f}_h(\mathbf{x})\right] - f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{h^2 \sigma_K^2}{2} \nabla^2 f(\mathbf{x}) + O(h^4),$$

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\hat{f}_h(\mathbf{x})\right] = \frac{f(\mathbf{x}) \|K\|_2^2}{n h^2} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{n h^2}\right)^2.$$

The mean integrated squared error (MISE) is therefore $\operatorname{MISE}(h) \asymp \frac{h^4}{4} \sigma_K^4 \int (\nabla^2 f)^2 + \frac{\|K\|_2^2}{n h^2}$, minimized at the *optimal bandwidth* $h^* \propto n^{-1/6}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. This $n^{-1/6}$ rate is slower than the univariate $n^{-1/5}$, reflecting the curse of dimensionality that spatial visualization inherits from nonparametric density estimation.

**Literature context.** The classical treatment of bivariate KDE appears in Wand & Jones (1995, *Kernel Smoothing*) and Scott (1992, *Multivariate Density Estimation*). Bandwidth selection in two dimensions remains active: plug-in selectors (Duong & Hazelton 2003), cross-validation, and diffusion-based estimators (Botev et al. 2010) each make different regularity assumptions on $f$. Separately, the perceptual validity of color maps is addressed by Crameri et al. (2020, *Nature Communications*), who show that non-perceptually-uniform maps (e.g., the default "jet") introduce false feature artifacts — effectively distorting $\hat{f}$ in the viewer's interpretation. An open question links both threads: given a budget of $n$ samples and a perceptual color resolution of $K$ distinguishable levels, what is the optimal joint design of sampling strategy, bandwidth, and color map to maximize the probability that a human observer correctly localizes the global maximum of $f$?

**Suggested investigation.** Generate a synthetic $f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k \exp(-\|\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^2 / (2\sigma_k^2))$ with known peak locations. Sample $n \in \{50, 200, 1000\}$ points from $f$ (treating it as a density), and produce heatmaps under three bandwidth selection rules (Silverman's rule of thumb, least-squares cross-validation, and the Botev diffusion estimator). Quantify localization error as $\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}^* - \mathbf{x}^*\|_2$ where $\hat{\mathbf{x}}^*$ is the argmax of the rendered surface. Repeat under two color maps (jet vs. viridis) and assess whether the color choice biases the reported peak location when read by naive human subjects.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) the MISE convergence rate achieved empirically versus the theoretical $O(n^{-2/3})$ bound; (2) a bias map $\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x})$ at each sample size to visualize where the estimator fails; (3) a table of localization errors broken down by bandwidth method and color map; and (4) a critical discussion of whether heatmap construction choices constitute a researcher degree of freedom that should be preregistered in empirical studies using spatial data.

### choropleth_graph (34.1s)

## Choropleth Graph

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{R} = \{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n\}$ be a finite partition of a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ into simply connected regions (countries, census tracts, electoral districts). A *choropleth graph* is the triple $\mathcal{C} = (G, f, \tau)$, where $G = (V, E)$ is the planar adjacency graph with vertex set $V = \mathcal{R}$ and edge set $E = \{(r_i, r_j) : \partial r_i \cap \partial r_j \neq \emptyset,\; i \neq j\}$; $f : V \to \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar field assigning an aggregate statistical value to each region (e.g., population density, unemployment rate); and $\tau : \mathbb{R} \to \mathcal{C}_k$ is a *color transfer function* mapping real values to a $k$-color palette. The resulting visualization encodes $f$ through filled region color rather than through position or size.

**Theorem (Classification-Induced Distortion).** Let $\hat{f} = \tau^{-1} \circ \tau \circ f$ be the piecewise-constant approximation of $f$ induced by any finite classification scheme that partitions $\text{range}(f)$ into $k$ bins. Then for any Lipschitz loss $\ell$,

$$\mathbb{E}_{r \sim \mu}\bigl[\ell(f(r), \hat{f}(r))\bigr] \;\geq\; \frac{1}{4k^2}\,\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f),$$

where $\mu$ is the area measure on $\Omega$. *Proof sketch.* By the classical $k$-bin quantization lower bound, any uniform-width partition achieves worst-case squared error at least $(1/4k^2)\mathrm{Var}(f)$; Lipschitz $\ell$ with constant $L$ then gives the result by Jensen's inequality. Optimal bin placement (Jenks–Fisher natural breaks) minimizes within-class variance $\sum_{j=1}^k \sum_{r \in B_j}(f(r) - \bar{f}_{B_j})^2$, but the lower bound is tight for adversarial $f$.

**Literature context.** The choropleth as a statistical graphic traces to Dupin (1826) and was formalized by Tobler's *First Law of Spatial Autocorrelation* (1970): nearby regions tend to exhibit similar values. This is captured by Moran's $I$,

$$I = \frac{n}{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}} \cdot \frac{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}(f_i - \bar{f})(f_j - \bar{f})}{\sum_i (f_i - \bar{f})^2},$$

where $w_{ij} = \mathbf{1}[(r_i, r_j) \in E]$ is the adjacency weight matrix. $I \approx 1$ signals strong positive spatial clustering; $I \approx -1$ signals dispersion. Open questions center on: (i) perceptual nonlinearity — sequential colorbrewer palettes may introduce perceived discontinuities not present in $f$; (ii) modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP), whereby identical underlying distributions yield different $I$ values under different partitions $\mathcal{R}$; (iii) optimal-transport formulations of choropleth comparison, wherein the Wasserstein distance $W_2(\mu_f, \mu_g)$ between two scalar fields is a partition-invariant dissimilarity.

**Suggested investigation.** Obtain a public dataset with two coincident partitions at different spatial scales (e.g., US counties vs. states, or NUTS-2 vs. NUTS-3 European regions). Fix a variable $f$ (e.g., median household income). Compute $I$ at both scales and record the ratio $I_{\text{fine}} / I_{\text{coarse}}$. Repeat under three classification schemes (equal interval, quantile, Jenks). Measure perceptual accuracy by presenting choropleth renderings to naive raters and asking them to rank regions, then computing Kendall's $\tau$ against ground truth $f$. The question of interest: does Jenks minimization of quantization distortion translate to higher $\tau$ in perceptual rank recovery, or does scale dominate scheme?

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) an explicit mathematical definition of the partition and the transfer function used; (2) the value of $I$ at each scale with a permutation-test $p$-value under spatial randomization; (3) a table of within-class variance by scheme and scale; (4) a psychophysical result reporting Kendall's $\tau$ with confidence intervals; and (5) a discussion section identifying whether observed accuracy differences survive MAUP resampling, connecting findings to the MAUP literature (Openshaw 1984) and recent optimal-transport critiques of choropleth comparison.

## Gis Mapping

**Definition.** A *geographic information system* (GIS) map is a composition of three mathematical structures: a *geodetic datum* $(\mathcal{E}, \phi_0, \lambda_0, a, f)$ that fixes an ellipsoidal model of the Earth with semi-major axis $a$ and flattening $f$; a *map projection* $\pi: \mathcal{E} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ that carries geodetic coordinates $(\phi, \lambda)$ to Cartesian plane coordinates $(x, y)$; and a *spatial index* over the projected geometry, typically an R-tree or a space-filling curve. The projection $\pi$ is a smooth map between Riemannian manifolds, and its distortion properties are the central object of study.

**Theorem (Gauss, 1827).** No smooth map from a sphere $S^2$ (or any surface of non-zero Gaussian curvature) to the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ can preserve all of angle, area, and distance simultaneously. Equivalently, there is no isometric embedding of $S^2$ into $\mathbb{R}^2$.

*Proof sketch.* The Theorema Egregium states that Gaussian curvature $K$ is an intrinsic invariant: an isometry $f: (M, g) \to (N, h)$ satisfies $K_M = K_N \circ f$. Since $K_{S^2} = 1/R^2 > 0$ and $K_{\mathbb{R}^2} = 0$ everywhere, no such isometry can exist. $\square$

Distortion is quantified via **Tissot's indicatrix**: at each point $p$, the differential $d\pi_p$ maps the unit circle in the tangent plane $T_p \mathcal{E}$ to an ellipse with semi-axes $a(p)$ and $b(p)$. An *equal-area* projection satisfies $a(p)b(p) = \text{const}$; a *conformal* projection satisfies $a(p) = b(p)$ (locally angle-preserving). The classic result is that these two properties are mutually exclusive everywhere unless the domain is flat. The *angular distortion* $\omega$ at $p$ satisfies $\sin(\omega/2) = (a - b)/(a + b)$, and the *areal distortion* is $S = ab/\sigma_0^2$ where $\sigma_0$ is the reference scale factor.

**Computational geometry layer.** On the plane, spatial queries (containment, intersection, $k$-nearest-neighbor) are answered using R-trees, whose worst-case query time is $O(\sqrt{n})$ for $n$ polygons in 2D, or approximately $O(\log n)$ in practice with good packing heuristics. The *Dimensionally Extended 9-Intersection Model* (DE-9IM) gives a formal predicate calculus over topological relations (touches, crosses, overlaps, etc.) between geometric objects, forming the semantic backbone of spatial SQL in systems like PostGIS.

**Literature context.** Snyder (1987) remains the canonical reference for projection formulae; Bugayevskiy & Snyder (1995) extend the theory to ellipsoidal models. Open questions of current interest include: (1) optimal projection design for minimizing total distortion over a given region of interest (a variational problem over the space of smooth maps); (2) differential-geometric formulations of conformal mapping via the Beltrami equation $f_{\bar{z}} = \mu f_z$ with complex dilatation $\mu$, which appears in quasi-conformal cartography (Choi et al., 2015); (3) data-driven projection learning, where $\pi$ is parameterized by a neural network trained to minimize distortion metrics over a dataset of query polygons.

**Suggested investigation.** Choose two projections of the same region — e.g., Web Mercator (EPSG:3857) and Albers Equal-Area Conic (EPSG:5070) for the contiguous United States. (i) Compute and visualize Tissot's indicatrices on a $5° \times 5°$ grid for each. (ii) Measure the relative area error and angular error at each grid point. (iii) Execute a fixed spatial join (e.g., county polygon intersects census tract centroid) under both projections and record whether topology is preserved. (iv) Profile R-tree query time as a function of $n$ for both projected datasets and compare empirical slopes against $O(\sqrt{n})$ and $O(\log n)$ bounds.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: the theoretical distortion bound for each projection class (with derivation from Tissot's formula), the empirical distortion field measured from your grid experiment, a comparison of topological correctness across projections for the spatial join, and a performance table for the R-tree queries. The conclusion should argue which projection is more appropriate for the given analytical task and region, grounded in the distortion metrics rather than convention.
