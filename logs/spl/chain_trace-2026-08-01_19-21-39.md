### sample_data (16.2s)

## Sample Data

You already have the right instinct: when you want to know something about a huge group — every student in your country, every fish in a lake — you can't measure all of them. So you measure a carefully chosen *few* and reason from there. That smaller collection is your **sample**.

The full group you care about is called the **population**, usually written $N$ (total count). The sample is the slice you actually observe, written $n$ (sample count). If your school has $N = 800$ students and you survey $n = 40$ of them about sleep habits, those 40 responses *are* your sample data.

What makes a sample useful is two things working together: **size** and **randomness**. A larger $n$ generally gives you more reliable information, but only if each member of the population had a fair chance of being picked. A sample that accidentally includes only athletes, or only students who stay late, is *biased* — its numbers will not reflect the full school.

From the sample you can compute summaries. The sample mean

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$$

is the average of what you observed. It's your best single-number estimate for the whole population's typical value.

A useful rule of thumb: in most school-level problems, $n \geq 30$ is considered large enough to start trusting your sample mean as a reliable estimate.

### population_data (13.9s)

## Population Data

You already know that data is information we collect about the world. But *whose* information? That question leads to one of the most important ideas in statistics: the difference between a **population** and a **sample**.

A **population** is the complete set of every individual (or object) you care about. If you want to know the average height of every 7th grader in your country, the population is *all* of those students — millions of them. Measuring every single person would be ideal, but it's almost never practical.

That's why statisticians work with a **sample**: a carefully chosen subset of the population. If you measure 500 students selected fairly, you can use that data to *estimate* what the whole population looks like.

The key numbers describing a population are called **parameters** (often written with Greek letters), while numbers describing a sample are called **statistics**. For example:

- Population mean: $\mu$ (mu)
- Sample mean: $\bar{x}$ (x-bar)

The relationship looks like this:

$$\bar{x} \approx \mu \quad \text{(when the sample is large and fairly chosen)}$$

The larger and more representative your sample, the closer $\bar{x}$ gets to the true $\mu$. This is the core promise of statistics: you don't need to measure *everyone* to learn something reliable about *everyone*.

One critical rule: a sample must be **random** — every member of the population should have an equal chance of being selected. A biased sample (like only surveying students in one school) can lead to wrong conclusions about the whole population.

### mean (12.6s)

## Mean

You already know the mean as "add everything up, then divide by how many there are." That instinct is exactly right — but let's look at *why* that recipe works and what it actually measures.

The mean is the **balance point** of a data set. Imagine placing each value as a weight on a number line. The mean is the single spot where the whole thing balances perfectly — every value that sits above it "pulls" just as hard as every value below. That balance property is what makes the mean so useful in science, economics, and everyday life.

Formally, if you have $n$ values $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, the mean $\bar{x}$ (read "x-bar") is:

$$\bar{x} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$$

The $\Sigma$ (sigma) notation is just shorthand for "add up all the $x_i$ values as $i$ goes from 1 to $n$." Nothing mysterious — it compresses the addition into one clean symbol.

One key pattern: **every value in the list shifts the mean.** A single very large or very small number can drag the mean far from where most values cluster. This is why a billionaire moving into a neighborhood appears to raise the "average income" dramatically even though most residents are unchanged.

### median (11.8s)

## Median

You already know the median as "the middle value" — line up your numbers from smallest to largest, and the one sitting in the center wins. That intuition is exactly right. Now let's look at the structure underneath it.

**The actual rule.** Given a sorted list of $n$ values:

- If $n$ is **odd**, the median is the single middle value at position $\dfrac{n+1}{2}$.
- If $n$ is **even**, there is no single middle, so you average the two central values at positions $\dfrac{n}{2}$ and $\dfrac{n}{2}+1$.

For example, with five test scores $\{62, 71, 78, 85, 91\}$, the median is the value at position $\frac{5+1}{2} = 3$, which is $\mathbf{78}$.

With six scores $\{62, 71, 78, 85, 91, 95\}$, the two middle values are at positions 3 and 4 — that's $78$ and $85$ — so the median is $\dfrac{78 + 85}{2} = \mathbf{81.5}$.

**Why does this matter?** The median resists outliers. If one student scored $2$ instead of $62$, the median barely moves, but the mean drops noticeably. This is why median household income is reported instead of average income — a handful of billionaires can inflate the average while most families' reality stays near the median.

### percentiles (16.0s)

## Percentiles

You already know that the median splits a dataset in half — half the values are below it, half above. Percentiles generalize that idea. Instead of just the halfway point, they let you describe *any* position in a dataset.

**The structure.** The $p$-th percentile is the value below which $p\%$ of the data falls. So the 50th percentile is exactly the median. The 25th percentile (called $Q_1$) is the value where one quarter of the data sits below it. The 75th percentile ($Q_3$) is where three quarters sit below it. Together, $Q_1$, the median ($Q_2$), and $Q_3$ are called the **quartiles**, and they divide your data into four equal-sized groups.

To find the $p$-th percentile from $n$ sorted data points, compute the **rank**:

$$L = \frac{p}{100} \times n$$

If $L$ is a whole number, the percentile is the average of the $L$-th and $(L+1)$-th values. If $L$ has a decimal, round up and use that position.

**The key pattern.** Percentiles don't tell you a raw score — they tell you *where you stand relative to others*. A score of 72 on a test means very different things depending on whether it sits at the 40th or the 90th percentile.

### probability (13.1s)

## Probability

You already know that flipping a coin lands heads roughly half the time. That gut sense — *some outcomes are more likely than others* — is exactly what probability makes precise.

**From intuition to structure.** Probability assigns a number between 0 and 1 to every possible outcome of an experiment. An outcome that *never* happens gets a probability of $0$; one that *always* happens gets $1$. Everything else lives somewhere in between. We write $P(A)$ to mean "the probability that event $A$ occurs."

When all outcomes are equally likely — like rolling a fair six-sided die — there is a clean formula:

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{total number of equally likely outcomes}}$$

Rolling a 3 on a fair die: there is 1 favorable outcome out of 6 total, so $P(\text{roll a 3}) = \tfrac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$.

**A key rule: complement.** The probability that $A$ does *not* happen is

$$P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$$

If the chance of rain tomorrow is $0.3$, then the chance it stays dry is $1 - 0.3 = 0.7$. Complements let you solve problems from the "easier" direction.

**Probabilities always add up.** For any experiment, if you list every possible outcome, their probabilities must sum to exactly $1$. No probability is allowed to be negative, and no single probability can exceed $1$.

### random_variable (38.1s)

## Random Variable

You already know that randomness means you can't predict the exact outcome of an experiment — rolling a die, flipping a coin, drawing a card. A **random variable** is how mathematicians turn that unpredictability into something they can work with: it's a rule that assigns a **number** to every possible outcome.

### From Outcomes to Numbers

Suppose you flip two coins. The sample space is $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$. Define $X$ = "number of heads." Then $X$ maps each outcome to a number:

$$X(HH) = 2, \quad X(HT) = 1, \quad X(TH) = 1, \quad X(TT) = 0$$

$X$ isn't a single fixed value — it's a **function** on the sample space. The letter $X$ (or $Y$, $Z$) is the standard notation; lowercase $x$ usually denotes a specific value it might take.

### Reading Probabilities from a Random Variable

Once you have $X$, you can ask: *how likely is each value?* Since $X = 1$ whenever the outcome is $HT$ or $TH$, and each outcome has probability $1/4$, you get $P(X = 1) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2$. Similarly, $P(X = 0) = 1/4$ and $P(X = 2) = 1/4$.

Notice that those three probabilities add up to 1 — every outcome is covered, so all the chances must account for 100% of the possibilities. This is a natural check: if your values don't sum to 1, you've missed something.

### variance (13.4s)

## Variance

You already know that the *mean* tells you where a dataset's center sits. But two datasets can share the same mean while looking completely different — one tightly clustered, the other wildly spread out. Variance is how we measure that spread in a single, precise number.

**Exploring the structure.** The key idea is to look at how far each value strays from the mean. For a dataset $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ with mean $\mu$, the *deviation* of each point is $(x_i - \mu)$. If you just added all the deviations, they'd cancel to zero — positive and negative gaps balance out. So instead, we *square* each deviation to make everything positive, then average those squares:

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \mu)^2$$

The result, $\sigma^2$, is the **variance**. A small variance means the values huddle close to the mean; a large variance means they scatter far from it.

**The pattern to remember.** Squaring the deviations does two things at once: it removes the sign (so gaps in either direction count), and it *amplifies* outliers — a point twice as far from the mean contributes four times as much to the variance. This is why variance is sensitive to extreme values.

### probability_distribution (12.5s)

## Probability Distribution

You already know that probability measures how likely a single outcome is. A **probability distribution** takes that idea further: instead of asking "what's the chance of rolling a 3?", it asks "what does the entire picture of chances look like for every possible outcome?"

Think of it as a full report card, not just one grade.

**Exploring the structure.** For a fair six-sided die, every face has probability $\frac{1}{6}$. List all outcomes and their probabilities together, and you have a distribution:

$$P(X = k) = \frac{1}{6}, \quad k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$$

Here $X$ is called a **random variable** — a variable whose value depends on chance. The distribution tells you $P(X = k)$ for every possible $k$.

**The golden rule.** No matter what the distribution looks like, all probabilities must be non-negative and must sum to exactly 1:

$$\sum_{\text{all } k} P(X = k) = 1$$

This makes sense: *something* has to happen, and the total chance of all possibilities together is certainty.

Distributions don't have to be uniform. A loaded die might give $P(X = 6) = \frac{1}{2}$ and split the remaining $\frac{1}{2}$ across the other five faces. As long as everything adds to 1, it's a valid distribution.

### quartiles (10.7s)

## Quartiles

You already know that the **median** splits a sorted data set exactly in half — it's the middle value. Quartiles take that idea one step further: they slice the data into **four equal parts**, giving you a much richer picture of how values are spread out.

**The structure.** Sort your data from smallest to largest. The three quartile markers are:

- $Q_1$ — the **lower quartile**: the median of the lower half of the data
- $Q_2$ — the **median**: the middle of the whole data set
- $Q_3$ — the **upper quartile**: the median of the upper half of the data

These three values create four sections, each containing roughly 25% of the data. The gap between $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ is called the **interquartile range**:

$$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$$

The IQR is powerful because it tells you where the **middle 50%** of your data lives — ignoring extreme values at both ends.

**The pattern.** A small IQR means most values are clustered together (consistent data). A large IQR means the data is spread wide (variable data). Think of it like a crowd: a tight IQR means everyone is roughly the same height; a wide IQR means heights vary wildly.

### standard_deviation (13.8s)

## Standard Deviation

You already know that the **mean** tells you where a data set is centered. But two classes can have the same average test score yet look completely different — one class where everyone scored close to 75, another where half scored 50 and half scored 100. Standard deviation measures how *spread out* the values are around that center.

Here is how it is built. Start with a data set of $n$ values: $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ with mean $\mu$.

**Step 1 — Find each deviation:** subtract the mean from every value to get $(x_i - \mu)$.

**Step 2 — Square each deviation:** $(x_i - \mu)^2$. Squaring removes negative signs so that values below and above the mean do not cancel each other out.

**Step 3 — Average the squared deviations** (this is called the *variance*):

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2$$

**Step 4 — Take the square root** to return to the original units:

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2}$$

A small $\sigma$ means the data clusters tightly around the mean; a large $\sigma$ means the values are widely scattered. This is why $\sigma$ is the go-to measure whenever you want to compare how consistent two groups are.

**A useful pattern to remember:** roughly 68 % of values in many real-world data sets fall within one standard deviation of the mean — between $\mu - \sigma$ and $\mu + \sigma$.

## Python Data Analysis

**Quick recap:** You already know that data is everywhere — scores, temperatures, prices, populations. Python gives you tools to *talk* to that data systematically instead of staring at a spreadsheet.

**The structure beneath the surface**

Real data analysis follows a repeatable pipeline:

1. **Load** — read data from a file (CSV, Excel, database)
2. **Inspect** — check shape, types, and missing values
3. **Clean** — fix errors, fill gaps, standardize formats
4. **Analyze** — compute summaries, find patterns
5. **Visualize** — turn numbers into pictures

The two workhorses are **pandas** (data tables) and **matplotlib/seaborn** (charts). A pandas `DataFrame` is like a spreadsheet in code — rows are observations, columns are variables.

```python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("scores.csv")
print(df.shape)        # (rows, columns)
print(df.describe())   # count, mean, std, min, max per column
```

The `describe()` method gives you the five-number summary plus mean and standard deviation in one call. For a column of exam scores, the **mean** $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ and **standard deviation** $s$ tell you the center and spread immediately.

Filtering rows works like a math predicate:

```python
high_scorers = df[df["score"] > 90]
```

This keeps only the rows where the condition is `True` — exactly like set-builder notation $\{x \mid x > 90\}$.

**The rule to remember:** every analysis step maps to one clear action: load → inspect → clean → analyze → visualize. Skip a step and your conclusions may be built on messy data.
