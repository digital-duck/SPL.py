### point_estimate (9.6s)

## Point Estimate

**The Jar of Candies**

Imagine your teacher walks in holding a big glass jar packed with colorful candies. She asks, "How many candies do you think are in here?"

You can't count every single one — the jar is sealed! So you do something smart: you *guess one specific number*. Maybe you say, "I think there are 200 candies."

That single number — your best guess — is called a **point estimate**.

**Why "point"?** Think of a number line. A point estimate is like putting your finger down on *one exact spot* on that line. Not a range, not "somewhere between 150 and 250" — just one dot. One answer. Your best shot.

Every day you make point estimates without realizing it:
- "Dinner will be ready in **20 minutes**."
- "There are about **30** kids at recess."
- "That bag weighs **5 pounds**."

A point estimate won't always be *perfectly* right — but it gives everyone a clear, useful starting answer.

**Now you try:** Look at a handful of grapes. Without counting, say one number. That's your point estimate! Then count and see how close you were.

### null_hypothesis (12.7s)

## Null Hypothesis

**A cookie mystery.** Your friend says the new blue tin has *more* cookies than the old red tin. You're not sure. Before you count, you make a careful starting guess: *"Both tins have the same number of cookies."* That careful starting guess — "nothing is different" — is called the **null hypothesis**.

**Why start by assuming nothing changed?** Because it's the fairest place to begin. You don't want to cheat by expecting a winner before you look at the evidence. The null hypothesis is like a referee who calls the game tied at zero before anyone scores.

**Now you count.** If the blue tin has 23 cookies and the red has 22, that's a tiny difference — maybe just chance. But if blue has 40 and red has 20, that's hard to ignore! When the evidence is strong enough, you *reject* the null hypothesis and say, "Okay, something really is different here."

In symbols, scientists write:

$$H_0: \text{no difference exists}$$

**Now you try.** Your plant grew 5 cm this week. A friend says sunlight makes plants grow faster. What would your null hypothesis be before running the experiment?

### alternative_hypothesis (11.8s)

## Alternative Hypothesis

Imagine your little brother says, "The dog ate my cookie." You don't believe him — you think *he* ate it himself. That suspicion you have? That's called an **alternative hypothesis**: it's your challenger idea, the one that says "maybe things didn't happen the boring, normal way."

Scientists always start with a dull "nothing special is happening" idea (called the null hypothesis). The alternative hypothesis is the exciting one — it says *something real is going on*.

**Intuition check:** Say your teacher thinks boys and girls score the same on a spelling test (boring, normal). Your alternative hypothesis might be: "Actually, girls score higher." You're challenging the "nothing different" story with a new possibility.

We write it like this:

$$H_1: \text{something is different (or bigger, or smaller)}$$

The goal of an experiment is to gather enough evidence to decide: does the boring story hold, or does your challenger idea win?

**Now you try:** You think plants grow taller when you play music for them. Write your alternative hypothesis in one sentence — what exactly are you claiming is *different* from "music makes no difference at all"?

### critical_value (8.9s)

## Critical Value

**The Broken Thermometer Game**

Imagine your school has a rule: if the outdoor thermometer reads **below 32°F, school is cancelled**. The number 32 is special — it's the line between "go to school" and "stay home." Everything depends on whether the temperature crosses that number.

That magic boundary is called a **critical value**.

**Building the Idea**

A critical value is a threshold — a specific number that separates two different outcomes. Below it, one thing happens. Above it, something else happens entirely.

Think of a seesaw: perfectly balanced in the middle, but tip just a little past the balance point and — *whoosh* — everything shifts to one side. The balance point is the critical value.

In math and science, we use critical values to make decisions. Is this bridge safe? Is this medicine working? Is this result just lucky, or is it real? The critical value tells us where "maybe" ends and "yes" begins.

**Now You Try**

Your teacher says anyone scoring **above 70** passes the quiz. What is the critical value here? If you score 69, what happens? If you score 71?

### level_of_significance (17.9s)

## Level Of Significance

Imagine your friend says she can read minds. She guesses your secret number correctly — once. Lucky! She guesses it again. Maybe still luck. But she guesses it *ten times in a row*. At some point you stop saying "lucky" and start thinking "wait, something else is going on here."

That turning point — the moment when *too surprising* becomes *probably real* — is exactly what the **level of significance** captures.

Scientists ask the same question: "Could this just be a coincidence?" They draw a line in the sand before the experiment begins. A common choice is 5 chances in 100, written $\alpha = 0.05$. If something this surprising would happen by pure luck fewer than 5 times out of 100, they say: "This is real — not just a fluke."

Think of $\alpha$ as a suspicion dial. Set it low ($\alpha = 0.01$) and you need *very* strong evidence to be convinced. Set it higher and you trust surprising things more easily. The trick is choosing wisely before you look at the answer.

**Now you try:** If your friend guesses your card correctly 3 times in a row, would *you* be convinced? What about 10 times?

### scatterplot (9.6s)

## Scatterplot

**The Great Shoe Mystery**

Imagine your class is curious: do taller kids have bigger feet? You measure everyone's height and shoe size, then stare at a long list of numbers. It's a mess — impossible to see any pattern.

Now try this: draw a big square on paper. Make the bottom edge "height" and the side edge "shoe size." For each classmate, find their height on the bottom, go straight up, find their shoe size on the side, go straight across — and put a dot where those two lines would meet. Do this for everyone.

Suddenly, dots appear across your paper. If taller kids really do have bigger feet, the dots drift from the bottom-left toward the upper-right, like a gentle hill you could ski down.

That picture of dots is a **scatterplot**. Each dot is one person, holding two pieces of information at once. The *pattern* the dots make tells you whether two things travel together — or don't.

**Now you try:** Ask five friends how many books they read last month and how many hours of TV they watched. Plot each friend as a dot. What shape do your dots make?

### test_statistic (13.1s)

## Test Statistic

Imagine your friend claims she can always guess which hand you're hiding a coin in. You test her ten times — and she gets **eight right**. Is she psychic, or just lucky?

To find out, you need a single number that captures *how surprising* her score is. That number is called a **test statistic**.

Think of it like a thermometer for surprise. A normal guesser, getting answers right by chance, would score around five out of ten. Your friend scored eight — that's three steps away from normal. The test statistic measures those "steps away" in a precise way, so everyone agrees on how surprising the result really is.

The bigger the test statistic, the more the data is shouting: *"Something unusual is going on here!"* A small test statistic whispers: *"This could just be luck."*

Formally, a test statistic is a value calculated from your data that you compare against what chance alone would produce. It turns a messy pile of observations into one honest number.

**Now you try:** Flip a coin 20 times. You expect about 10 heads. If you get 17 heads, does that feel surprising? How many "steps away" from 10 is that?

### correlation_coefficient (11.5s)

## Correlation Coefficient

Imagine you notice something curious: every time it rains, your dog wants to stay inside. Every single time. That's not a coincidence — those two things *move together*. When one goes up (rain), the other goes up too (indoor time). Scientists call this a **correlation**.

But how *strongly* do things move together? That's what the correlation coefficient measures. Think of it like a friendship score between two things. A score of $1$ means they are perfect best friends — whenever one goes up, the other always goes up too. A score of $-1$ means they are perfect opposites — one goes up, the other always goes down (like studying more and making fewer mistakes). A score of $0$ means they have nothing to do with each other at all.

We write this score as $r$, and it always lives between $-1$ and $1$:

$$-1 \leq r \leq 1$$

The closer $|r|$ is to $1$, the stronger the connection.

**Now you try:** Think of two things in your life that seem to move together. Do you think their $r$ would be closer to $1$, $-1$, or $0$? Why?

### f_distribution (15.1s)

## F Distribution

Imagine two classes competing in a paper-airplane contest. Class A's planes land in a tight cluster near the target — very consistent. Class B's planes scatter all over the gym. Which class is *more* consistent?

To compare, we measure the **spread** of each class's throws. Spread tells us how far results wander from the average. Now here is the clever part: instead of looking at one class alone, we build a *ratio* — Class B's spread divided by Class A's spread.

$$F = \frac{\text{spread of Group 2}}{\text{spread of Group 1}}$$

If both classes are equally consistent, $F$ hovers close to $1$ — the spreads nearly cancel. If Group 2 is *much* messier, $F$ grows large. The **F distribution** is a picture of all the $F$ values you would get if you ran this contest over and over with two groups that are truly equally consistent.

Statisticians use this shape to ask: "Is our $F$ value surprisingly large, or perfectly ordinary?"

**Now you try:** A baker times two ovens. Oven A varies by $\pm 2$ minutes; Oven B varies by $\pm 6$ minutes. Which $F$ ratio would you expect — close to $1$, or much bigger than $1$? Why?

### hypothesis_testing (9.8s)

## Hypothesis Testing

**The Cookie Mystery**

You bake two batches of cookies — one with butter, one with margarine — and ask your class which tastes better. Most kids pick butter. But wait: maybe they just got lucky guessing! How do you *know* the butter cookies are actually better?

**The Big Idea**

A hypothesis is your best guess about the world. Hypothesis testing is how you check whether your guess is probably right — or just a lucky accident.

Start with the *opposite* of what you believe: "There's no difference between the cookies." This is called the **null hypothesis** — your "nothing interesting here" starting point.

Then you collect evidence. If almost everyone picks butter, the chances of that happening by pure luck are very small. When the evidence makes the null hypothesis look very unlikely, you can reject it and say: "The butter cookies really do taste better!"

**The Rule of Thumb**

Scientists ask: *If there were truly no difference, how surprising would my results be?* Very surprising results = strong evidence your hypothesis is right.

**Now You Try**

Flip a coin 20 times. Your friend says: "This coin is fair." That's the null hypothesis. What result would make you doubt it?

### margin_of_error (11.0s)

## Margin Of Error

Imagine you're guessing how many jellybeans are in a jar. You peek carefully and say, "I think there are about 200." But you're not *exactly* sure — maybe it's 195, maybe 210. So you say, "I think it's **200, give or take 15.**"

That "give or take" part? That's the **margin of error**.

It's your honest way of saying: *my best guess is this number, but the real answer is probably somewhere nearby.* It's not an admission of failure — it's a sign of careful thinking! A guess with no margin of error is actually suspicious, like someone claiming to count every jellybean without touching the jar.

Scientists, pollsters, and weather forecasters all use margins of error. When a news report says "60% of kids prefer pizza, $\pm 5\%$," that means the true number is probably somewhere between $55\%$ and $65\%$.

$$\text{estimate} \pm \text{margin of error} = \text{range of likely truth}$$

**Now you try:** Ask 10 friends their favourite colour. Guess what the whole school might say — then add a margin of error. How wide should it be, and why?

### anova (15.1s)

## Anova

Imagine three friends — Mia, Leo, and Sam — each grew sunflowers using different fertilizers. Mia's flowers are tall. Leo's are medium. Sam's are short. But here's the puzzle: *even within* Mia's garden, the flowers aren't all the same height. So how do we know if the fertilizer actually made a difference, or if the flowers just grew differently by chance?

That question — "is the difference between groups real, or just random wobble?" — is exactly what **ANOVA** answers. ANOVA stands for *Analysis of Variance*. Variance is just a fancy word for how spread out numbers are.

The big idea: ANOVA compares two kinds of spread.

- **Between groups**: how far apart are Mia's, Leo's, and Sam's average heights?
- **Within groups**: how wobbly are the heights *inside* each garden?

If the between-group spread is much bigger than the within-group wobble, something real is going on. If they're about the same size, the difference might just be luck.

We write this as an $F$-ratio:

$$F = \frac{\text{spread between groups}}{\text{spread within groups}}$$

A big $F$ means the groups are genuinely different.

**Now you try:** Ask three friends to count how many jumping jacks they can do in one minute — each person tries three times. Are the *averages* between friends more spread out than the tries *within* each friend?

### confidence_interval (12.2s)

## Confidence Interval

**The Jelly Bean Jar**

Imagine your teacher holds up a big jar of jelly beans and asks, "How many are inside?" You can't count them all — but you shake it, squint at it, and guess: "I think there are about 200… maybe between 180 and 220."

That "between 180 and 220" is your **confidence interval** — a range of answers you feel pretty sure contains the truth.

**Why a range?**

We rarely know things *exactly*. When scientists measure something — the height of fourth-graders, the temperature of a pond — they only look at a small sample. A single guess might be a little off. So instead of saying "the answer is exactly 200," they say "the answer is probably *somewhere in here*," and they draw an imaginary fence around their best guess.

The wider the fence, the safer you are — but a fence around the whole number line isn't very useful! Good thinkers find a fence that is **narrow enough to be helpful** and **wide enough to be honest**.

Formally, a confidence interval is written as:

$$[\hat{x} - e,\; \hat{x} + e]$$

where $\hat{x}$ is your best guess and $e$ is the wiggle room.

**Now you try**

Grab a handful of coins and count them. Ask a friend to guess how many you have. Did they give a single number or a range? Which kind of answer feels more trustworthy?

### linear_regression (11.2s)

## Linear Regression

Imagine you're growing a sunflower. Every week you measure how tall it is, and you notice something: the more weeks that pass, the taller it gets. You jot down your measurements on grid paper and draw a dot for each week. Step back and squint — those dots almost make a straight line!

Linear regression is the math trick that finds the *best* straight line through a cloud of dots. Think of it like stretching a rubber band across your scatter of points, letting it settle where it feels most "balanced" — not too high, not too low.

Why a line? Because a line lets us **predict**. If your sunflower grew 3 cm every week for six weeks, a good line lets you guess: "It'll probably be about 21 cm by week seven."

The line has two ingredients: where it **starts** (called the intercept) and how steeply it **climbs** (called the slope). In math we write it as:

$$y = mx + b$$

where $x$ is your input (weeks), $y$ is your prediction (height), $m$ is the slope, and $b$ is where the line crosses zero.

**Now you try:** Ask a friend to count their steps on five different walks and note the distance each time. Plot the dots — can you draw one straight line that fits them all?

### python_statistical_computing (10.8s)

## Python Statistical Computing

Imagine your class collects how many minutes everyone reads each night. You have 25 numbers scribbled on sticky notes. Finding the highest, lowest, and middle value by hand takes forever — and you might make a mistake!

That is exactly what Python does for you, instantly. Think of Python as a super-fast helper who never gets tired of counting. You hand it a list of numbers, and it answers any question you ask: *What is the biggest? What is the typical one? How spread out are they?*

The "typical" number is called the **mean** (average). If five friends eat $2, 3, 3, 4,$ and $8$ cookies, the mean is:

$$\bar{x} = \frac{2 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 8}{5} = 4$$

Python computes this in one line using a library called `statistics`. A library is like a toolbox full of ready-made tools so you do not have to build them yourself.
