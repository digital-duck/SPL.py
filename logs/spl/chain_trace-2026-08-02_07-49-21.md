### sequence (0.1s)

## Sequence

**Definition.** A *sequence* is a function $a : \mathbb{N} \to X$, where $X$ is a set (typically $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, or a metric space $(X, d)$). We write $(a_n)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ and say $(a_n)$ *converges* to $L \in X$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $n > N$ implies $d(a_n, L) < \varepsilon$. The limit, when it exists, is unique in a Hausdorff space.

**Theorem (Completeness and Cauchy Criterion).** In a complete metric space, $(a_n)$ converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that $m, n > N$ implies $d(a_m, a_n) < \varepsilon$.

*Proof sketch.* Convergence implies Cauchy by the triangle inequality: $d(a_m, a_n) \leq d(a_m, L) + d(L, a_n) < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2$. The converse is the definition of completeness; it fails in $\mathbb{Q}$ (where $a_n = \lfloor n\sqrt{2}\rfloor / n$ is Cauchy but has no rational limit), motivating the construction of $\mathbb{R}$ via Dedekind cuts or Cauchy equivalence classes. $\square$

**Literature context.** The Cauchy criterion is classical (Rudin, *Principles of Mathematical Analysis*, §3), but its generalisation to Banach and Fréchet spaces underpins functional analysis and numerical methods for PDEs. Open questions arise in non-Archimedean and $p$-adic settings, where the ultrametric inequality strengthens convergence dramatically (Robert, *A Course in $p$-adic Analysis*).

**Suggested investigation.** Construct explicit Cauchy sequences in $\mathbb{Q}$ converging to $\sqrt{2}$, $e$, or a root of $x^5 - x - 1 = 0$. Verify numerically that the sequence is Cauchy yet has no rational limit. Extend to the $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$: show that $\sum_{k=0}^{n} p^k$ is Cauchy under $|\cdot|_p$ and identify its limit.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should state the metric space explicitly, prove both directions of the Cauchy criterion, exhibit a concrete non-convergent Cauchy sequence over $\mathbb{Q}$, and identify what structural property of $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{Z}_p$) is responsible for completing the space — distinguishing the algebraic from the topological content of the argument.

### time_series (0.2s)

## Time Series

**Definition.** A *weakly stationary time series* is a stochastic process $\{X_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu$ for all $t$ and $\text{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) = \gamma(h)$ for a function $\gamma$ depending only on the lag $h \in \mathbb{Z}$, not on $t$. This single condition—that the second-order structure is time-invariant—is the operative primitive: it licenses both Fourier-analytic tools and linear prediction theory without requiring the stronger assumption of strict stationarity.

**Theorem (Wold Decomposition).** Every zero-mean purely nondeterministic weakly stationary process $\{X_t\}$ admits the representation
$$X_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j}, \quad \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j^2 < \infty,$$
where $\{\varepsilon_t\}$ is white noise with variance $\sigma^2$ and $\psi_0 = 1$. The coefficients $\{\psi_j\}$ are unique. *Proof sketch:* Project $X_t$ onto the closed linear span $\overline{\text{sp}}\{X_s : s < t\}$; the one-step prediction errors $\varepsilon_t = X_t - \hat{X}_t$ are mutually orthogonal, and successive Gram–Schmidt steps recover the $\psi_j$ uniquely. $\square$

**Literature context.** Wold's theorem establishes that weak stationarity is precisely the condition under which linear prediction is well-posed; the non-uniqueness that arises in non-stationary settings (unit roots, structural breaks) is an active research area. Brockwell and Davis (1991) give the canonical treatment; Anderson (1971) covers the multivariate extension. Open questions concern whether weak stationarity is the right regularity class for neural sequence models—Transformers and state-space models implicitly learn $\psi_j$ weights without enforcing the $\ell^2$ summability constraint.

**Suggested investigation.** Generate sample paths from a weakly stationary AR(2) process with roots near but strictly inside the unit circle. Estimate $\psi_j$ via the Durbin–Levinson recursion and verify empirically that the estimated coefficients lie in $\ell^2$. Then perturb one root to land on the unit circle and document precisely where the recursion breaks down and why.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should establish: the parameter configuration and its distance from the unit circle, the estimated $\psi_j$ sequence and its partial sums against the theoretical $\ell^2$ bound, and a precise statement of which step in the Wold proof fails when weak stationarity is violated.

### error (0.2s)

## Error

**Definition.** Given a true quantity $x^* \in \mathcal{X}$ and an approximation $\hat{x} \in \mathcal{X}$, the *absolute error* is
$$e = \|\hat{x} - x^*\|$$
under a chosen norm $\|\cdot\|$. In statistical learning, this extends naturally to the expected squared deviation of an estimator $\hat{f}$ from the Bayes-optimal predictor $f^*$, averaged over the randomness of the training sample.

**Theorem (Bias–Variance Decomposition).** For the squared loss $\ell(a,b) = (a-b)^2$ and any estimator $\hat{f}$,

$$\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f}(x) - y)^2] = \underbrace{\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f^*(x)\bigr)^2}_{\mathrm{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}(x))}_{\mathrm{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\mathrm{irreducible}},$$

where $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(y \mid x)$. *Proof sketch:* Decompose $\hat{f}(x) - y = [\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}\hat{f}(x)] + [\mathbb{E}\hat{f}(x) - f^*(x)] + [f^*(x) - y]$, square, and take expectations; cross terms involving $\epsilon = y - f^*(x)$ vanish by independence of $\hat{f}$ from the noise. $\square$

**Literature context.** The tradeoff is classical (Geman et al., 1992), yet its behavior under overparameterization is not settled: Belkin et al. (2019) showed that test error can *decrease again* past the interpolation threshold — the double-descent phenomenon — contradicting the standard U-shaped intuition. Decompositions for non-squared losses (cross-entropy, hinge) lack closed forms and remain active research territory (Neal et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020).

**Suggested investigation.** Fit polynomial regressors of degree $d = 1, \ldots, n-1$ to a synthetic dataset with known $f^*$ and $\sigma^2$; estimate the three components empirically via 500 bootstrap resamples. Plot each against $d$ and locate the double-descent regime near $d = n-1$. Replicate on a neural-network width sweep on MNIST to observe interpolation-threshold behavior in practice.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should specify the norm and loss governing the decomposition, provide analytic bias and variance expressions under the chosen model family, report confidence intervals on all empirical estimates, and connect observed regimes (underfitting, overfitting, interpolation) to theoretical bounds from VC dimension or Rademacher complexity.

### time_series_model (63.1s)

## Time Series Model

**Definition.** A *time series model* is a family of probability distributions over sequences $(X_t)_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$, where $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ is an ordered index set and each $X_t$ takes values in a measurable space $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F})$. The model specifies the joint law $\mathbb{P}(X_{t_1}, \ldots, X_{t_k})$ for all finite subcollections, subject to Kolmogorov consistency. The central modeling choice is how to factor this joint law: the most tractable assumption is *stationarity* — the law is invariant under time shifts, $\mathbb{P}(X_{t_1+h}, \ldots, X_{t_k+h}) = \mathbb{P}(X_{t_1}, \ldots, X_{t_k})$ for all $h$ — and *ergodicity*, which ensures time averages converge to ensemble averages almost surely.

**Core structural result.** The Wold decomposition theorem provides a canonical representation for any zero-mean, covariance-stationary process $(X_t)$ with $\mathbb{E}[X_t^2] < \infty$:

$$X_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j} + \eta_t,$$

where $(\varepsilon_t)$ is white noise with $\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_t) = \sigma^2$, the coefficients satisfy $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j^2 < \infty$ with $\psi_0 = 1$, and $\eta_t$ is a deterministic component perfectly predictable from its own past. The proof projects $X_t$ onto the closed linear span of its past, identifies the innovation $\varepsilon_t = X_t - \hat{X}_t$ as the residual of the best linear predictor, and iterates. The decomposition is unique up to the deterministic part. It motivates $\mathrm{ARMA}(p,q)$ models as rational approximations to the infinite MA representation: the transfer function $\Psi(z) = \sum_{j=0}^\infty \psi_j z^j$ is replaced by a ratio of polynomials $\Theta(z)/\Phi(z)$, with roots of $\Phi$ strictly outside the unit disk for stationarity and roots of $\Theta$ outside for invertibility.

**Literature context and open questions.** Classical theory — Box–Jenkins identification, Yule–Walker estimation, Ljung–Box diagnostics — is well consolidated (Brockwell & Davis, *Time Series: Theory and Methods*, 2nd ed.). The frontier that most directly strains the Wold framework is *long-memory*: fractionally integrated $\mathrm{ARFIMA}(p,d,q)$ processes with $d \in (0, 1/2)$ exhibit hyperbolic autocovariance decay not captured by any finite-order ARMA approximation, yet arise empirically in finance and climate data (Beran, 1994). The spectral density near the origin satisfies $f(\lambda) \sim C|\lambda|^{1-2d}$ as $\lambda \to 0$, and while this form is known, minimax-optimal estimation of the memory parameter $d$ — and its interaction with short-memory components — remains an active problem.

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable graduate project is an empirical fit of $\mathrm{ARMA}(p,q)$ to a dataset where long-memory is plausible — e.g., monthly industrial production or daily temperature anomalies. The student should: (1) select order by AIC/BIC and inspect residual autocorrelation via the Ljung–Box statistic $Q(h) = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^h \hat{\rho}_k^2/(T-k)$; (2) estimate the spectral density nonparametrically via a smoothed periodogram and compare to the fitted parametric $f(\lambda;\hat{\theta})$, looking specifically for divergence near $\lambda = 0$ that would signal long-memory misspecification; (3) assess whether the ARMA residuals are consistent with the white-noise innovation assumption demanded by Wold, or whether systematic structure remains.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up should state the stationarity and invertibility conditions verified for the fitted model, report the asymptotic distribution of parameter estimates under the chosen estimator (MLE or Yule–Walker), and quantify forecast uncertainty via prediction intervals rather than point forecasts alone. If the spectral comparison suggests long-memory, the write-up should identify this as a model violation and discuss what extension — not merely a refit — would be required to address it.

### forecasting (51.0s)

## Forecasting

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and let $\{Y_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}$ be a real-valued stochastic process adapted to a filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$. A *forecast* of $Y_{t+h}$ at horizon $h \geq 1$, given information up to time $t$, is a measurable function $\hat{Y}_{t+h|t} = f(Y_t, Y_{t-1}, \ldots)$. The *optimal point forecast* under squared-error loss is the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$; this is the single primitive introduced here. The choice of loss function is not incidental — it determines what "optimal" means structurally — but squared-error is the canonical starting point because it admits a clean algebraic bound via the Wold decomposition.

**Theorem (Wold Decomposition).** Every covariance-stationary process $\{Y_t\}$ with no deterministic component admits the unique representation
$$Y_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j} + \eta_t,$$
where $\{\varepsilon_t\} \sim \mathrm{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$, $\sum_j \psi_j^2 < \infty$, $\psi_0 = 1$, and $\eta_t$ is linearly deterministic. The *h*-step-ahead forecast error under this representation satisfies
$$Y_{t+h} - \hat{Y}_{t+h|t} = \sum_{j=0}^{h-1} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t+h-j},$$
so the irreducible forecast variance at horizon $h$ is $\sigma^2 \sum_{j=0}^{h-1} \psi_j^2$, which is non-decreasing in $h$. This is a structural lower bound; no estimator can do better in the linear class regardless of sample size.

**Literature context.** Classical parametric forecasting — ARIMA, exponential smoothing, VAR — operates by estimating $\{\psi_j\}$ from finite samples and relies on correct model specification. Box and Jenkins (1970) systematized this. The subsequent literature has two major threads. First, *model uncertainty*: Bayesian model averaging (Hoeting et al., 1999) and forecast combination (Timmermann, 2006) aggregate over a model space rather than commit to one; the surprising empirical finding is that equal-weight averaging often beats sophisticated selection. Second, *nonlinear and nonstationary extensions*: Clements and Hendry (1999) show that structural breaks dominate in practice, motivating differencing, intercept corrections, and robust methods. More recently, transformer-based sequence models (Nie et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2023) challenge the parametric paradigm, though whether attention mechanisms genuinely capture temporal dependencies or merely interpolate within training distributions remains actively contested.

An open research question sits at this boundary: under what regularity conditions does a data-driven (e.g., neural) forecaster achieve the Wold lower bound, and at what sample-size cost relative to a correctly specified parametric model? Formal results here are sparse.

**Suggested investigation.** Construct a simulation study with a known DGP — say, an $\mathrm{AR}(2)$ with a structural break at time $T^*$. Fit three competing forecasters: OLS-estimated AR, a rolling-window AR, and a simple LSTM. Track the multi-step mean squared forecast error (MSFE) as a function of $h$ and break magnitude $\delta$. Derive the theoretical MSFE for each under the break, and compare with simulation. This replicates the spirit of Clements and Hendry (1999, Chapter 4) at small scale and isolates where model rigidity hurts.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (i) the theoretical MSFE expressions for each method pre- and post-break as closed-form functions of $\delta$ and $h$; (ii) Monte Carlo coverage of those expressions; (iii) a calibration table comparing MSFE at $h = 1, 4, 8$; and (iv) a clear statement of which assumptions were required to derive each bound and which were violated in the break scenario. Claims about neural forecaster performance require held-out test sets with no look-ahead contamination — this is a common failure mode worth flagging explicitly.

## Linear Regression Forecasting

**Definition.** Let $\{(x_t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{T}$ be a time-ordered sequence of covariate-response pairs, where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and $y_t \in \mathbb{R}$. The linear regression forecasting model posits

$$y_t = x_t^\top \beta + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} (0, \sigma^2),$$

where $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is the unknown coefficient vector. Given the design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times p}$ and response vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^T$, the ordinary least-squares (OLS) estimator is $\hat{\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$, and the $h$-step-ahead point forecast at origin $T$ is $\hat{y}_{T+h} = x_{T+h}^\top \hat{\beta}$.

**Theorem (Gauss–Markov).** Under the assumptions of linearity, strict exogeneity $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0$, homoskedasticity $\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = \sigma^2 I$, and full rank $\mathrm{rank}(X) = p$, the OLS estimator $\hat{\beta}$ is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) of $\beta$: for any linear unbiased estimator $\tilde{\beta}$,

$$\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\beta} \mid X) - \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) \succeq 0.$$

**Proof sketch.** Write $\tilde{\beta} = Cy$ for some matrix $C$ with $CX = I$. Then $\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\beta}) = \sigma^2 CC^\top$. Set $D = C - (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top$; orthogonality $DX = 0$ gives $CC^\top = (X^\top X)^{-1} + DD^\top$, so $\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\beta}) - \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}) = \sigma^2 DD^\top \succeq 0$. $\square$

**Literature context.** The Gauss–Markov theorem is foundational (Aitken 1935; Greene 2012), but its assumptions are violated routinely in forecasting contexts: serial correlation invalidates efficiency, unit-root regressors introduce super-consistency and the risk of spurious regression (Granger & Newbold 1974; Phillips 1986), and forecast combination often outperforms individually optimized models (Timmermann 2006). A common first response to Gauss–Markov failure in high-dimensional settings is Ridge regression (Hoerl & Kennard 1970; Tibshirani 1996; De Mol, Giannone & Reichlin 2008), which replaces the OLS objective with

$$\hat{\beta}_\lambda = \argmin_\beta \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|^2 = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y.$$

The bias-variance trade-off introduced by $\lambda > 0$ is explicit: $\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\beta}_\lambda) = -\lambda(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}\beta$ grows with $\lambda$, while $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_\lambda)$ shrinks. Whether this exchange reduces mean-squared forecast error (MSFE) depends on the signal-to-noise ratio and the eigenspectrum of $X^\top X$, making it an empirically open question for any given panel and horizon.

**Suggested investigation.** Select a publicly available macroeconomic panel (e.g., FRED-MD, 128 monthly series, McCracken & Ng 2016). Fix a target series (e.g., industrial production growth). (1) Fit OLS and Ridge forecasters using an expanding window from 1960 to 2000 with $h \in \{1, 3, 12\}$ months, selecting $\lambda$ by time-series cross-validation within the expanding window to avoid leakage. (2) Compute out-of-sample MSFE relative to an AR(1) benchmark. (3) Test forecast encompassing via the Clark–West statistic to assess whether Ridge genuinely adds predictive content beyond OLS. (4) Plot the Ridge path — MSFE against $\lambda$ — and note at which effective degrees of freedom the minimum occurs, connecting this empirically to the eigenvalue distribution of $X^\top X$.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up should (i) state all Gauss–Markov assumptions explicitly and test them (Durbin–Watson for serial correlation, White test for heteroskedasticity), establishing where and why OLS fails before motivating Ridge; (ii) document the precise walk-forward protocol to preclude look-ahead bias, including that $\lambda$ is re-selected at each expanding-window origin; (iii) report point forecasts alongside calibrated prediction intervals derived from $\hat{\sigma}^2$ and covariate uncertainty at the forecast origin; and (iv) use the Diebold–Mariano test with Newey–West standard errors when $h > 1$, since multi-step forecast errors carry MA structure that invalidates unadjusted test statistics.
