### training_testing_set (0.1s)

## Training Testing Set

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote an input space, $\mathcal{Y}$ a label space, and $\mathcal{D}$ an unknown joint distribution over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$. A *dataset* $S = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ consists of $n$ i.i.d. draws from $\mathcal{D}$. A *training–testing split* partitions $S$ into disjoint subsets $S_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{te}}$, with $|S_{\mathrm{tr}}| = m$ and $|S_{\mathrm{te}}| = n - m$, such that a hypothesis $h : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is selected using only $S_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and evaluated using only $S_{\mathrm{te}}$. The *empirical risk* on each partition is
$$\hat{R}_{S'}(h) = \frac{1}{|S'|} \sum_{(x,y) \in S'} \ell(h(x), y),$$
for loss $\ell : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$. The fundamental quantity of interest is the *true risk* $R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(h(x), y)]$, which remains inaccessible; the test risk $\hat{R}_{S_{\mathrm{te}}}(h)$ serves as its surrogate.

**Generalization Bound (PAC framework).** Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a hypothesis class with finite VC dimension $d = \mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H})$. For any $\delta \in (0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over draws of $S_{\mathrm{tr}}$, every $h \in \mathcal{H}$ satisfies
$$R(h) \leq \hat{R}_{S_{\mathrm{tr}}}(h) + \sqrt{\frac{d \log(m/d) + \log(1/\delta)}{m}}.$$
This classical result (Vapnik–Chervonenkis, 1971; Blumer et al., 1989) is derived via a symmetrization argument and the Sauer–Shelah lemma. The proof proceeds by (i) constructing a ghost sample of size $m$ drawn independently from $\mathcal{D}$; (ii) bounding the probability that empirical and true risks deviate by exchanging real and ghost samples; and (iii) counting the number of dichotomies $\mathcal{H}$ can induce on a sample of size $2m$, which is at most $(2m)^d$ by Sauer–Shelah. The logarithmic factor $\log(m/d)$ reflects this combinatorial growth.

**What Is Known and What Remains Open.** The PAC bound is worst-case over $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{H}$, which makes it notoriously loose in practice — modern overparameterized neural networks generalize well despite violating classical complexity conditions, a phenomenon described by Zhang et al. (2017) as *benign overfitting*. Subsequent work (Bartlett et al., 2020; Belkin et al., 2019) established that interpolating estimators can still achieve near-Bayes-optimal risk under specific data geometry assumptions, yet a tight characterization of when and why this occurs remains open. A second open question concerns *distribution shift*: when $S_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{te}}$ are drawn from different marginals $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}} \neq \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{te}}$, the test risk no longer unbiasedly estimates $R(h)$ under $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, invalidating the standard split protocol. Domain adaptation bounds (Ben-David et al., 2010) introduce a divergence term $d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{te}})$, but estimating this term from finite samples is itself an open problem.

**Suggested Investigation.** Choose a fixed architecture (e.g., a two-layer ReLU network) and dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10 subsets of varying size $n$). Vary the split ratio $m/(n-m)$ across $\{0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95\}$ and, for each ratio, record the gap $\hat{R}_{S_{\mathrm{te}}}(h) - \hat{R}_{S_{\mathrm{tr}}}(h)$. Overlay the empirical gap against the VC bound's predicted $O(\sqrt{d/m})$ scaling. A secondary experiment: deliberately introduce covariate shift by training on one digit-rotation angle and testing on another; quantify how much the test risk inflates relative to the standard i.i.d. split.

**Report-Writing Note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (i) an explicit statement of the i.i.d. assumption and where it is or is not satisfied; (ii) a log–log plot of generalization gap versus $m$ with slope comparison to theory; (iii) discussion of whether observed gaps are consistent with VC-dimension-based bounds or suggest a tighter complexity measure; and (iv) a quantitative characterization of how covariate shift degrades the test-risk estimate as a proxy for true risk.

### labeled_unlabeled_data (0.1s)

## Labeled Unlabeled Data

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{X}$ be an input space and $\mathcal{Y}$ a label space. A *labeled dataset* is a set $\mathcal{L} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ drawn i.i.d. from a joint distribution $p(x, y)$. An *unlabeled dataset* is a set $\mathcal{U} = \{x_j\}_{j=1}^{m}$ drawn i.i.d. from the marginal $p(x)$, with $m \gg n$ in practice. The central question of *semi-supervised learning* (SSL) is: under what conditions does $\mathcal{U}$ improve a classifier trained only on $\mathcal{L}$?

**Theoretical framing.** In the PAC-learning setting, the labeled sample complexity for learning a hypothesis class $\mathcal{H}$ with VC dimension $d$ to error $\epsilon$ at confidence $1-\delta$ is $n = O\!\left(\frac{d + \log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon}\right)$. The unlabeled distribution $p(x)$ is irrelevant here because the PAC bound is worst-case over all $p(x,y)$ compatible with some $h^* \in \mathcal{H}$.

**Theorem (Ben-David et al., 2008).** *Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a hypothesis class and $p_S$, $p_T$ be source and target marginals on $\mathcal{X}$. The target error of any $h \in \mathcal{H}$ satisfies*
$$\epsilon_T(h) \leq \epsilon_S(h) + \frac{1}{2} d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(p_S, p_T) + \lambda,$$
*where $d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}$ is the $\mathcal{H}$-divergence between marginals and $\lambda$ is the combined optimal error.* This result makes the value of unlabeled target data explicit: estimating $d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}$ from $\mathcal{U}$ allows one to bound the domain-shift penalty, even without target labels. The unlabeled set is informative precisely because it reveals the geometry of $p_T(x)$.

The *cluster assumption* — that decision boundaries should not cross high-density regions of $p(x)$ — provides a complementary inductive bias. Under this assumption, unlabeled data tightens the hypothesis class by ruling out boundaries inconsistent with observed density. Rigorous formalization requires that $p(x)$ be locally smooth and that the true $h^*$ respects the cluster structure; both are non-trivial and can fail in practice (Lafferty & Wasserman, 2007).

**What is known and what remains open.** Successful SSL algorithms — label propagation, co-training (Blum & Mitchell, 1998), $\Pi$-models, MixMatch, and FixMatch — exploit either manifold structure or consistency regularization. The gains are well-documented on vision benchmarks. However, *when* unlabeled data hurts — a phenomenon observed empirically in high label-noise and covariate-shift regimes — lacks a tight theoretical characterization. The interaction between the cluster assumption, model capacity, and data augmentation policy is not yet fully understood.

**Suggested investigation.** Design a controlled experiment on a tabular or low-dimensional synthetic dataset where the cluster assumption can be manually violated. Train a neural network classifier with $n \in \{10, 50, 200\}$ labeled points and $m = 5000$ unlabeled points. Sweep two conditions: (i) $p(x)$ respects the cluster assumption and (ii) $p(x)$ deliberately places high density across the true boundary. Measure test error as a function of $m$ under both conditions, and estimate $d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}$ from the data using a domain discriminator. This operationalizes the Ben-David bound and gives a concrete diagnostic for when to trust unlabeled augmentation.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) the theoretical prediction under each condition, (2) empirical error curves with confidence intervals, (3) the estimated $\mathcal{H}$-divergence and whether it correlates with observed gain or loss, and (4) a discussion of whether the cluster assumption can be tested from data alone prior to committing to SSL. The write-up should situate findings against the FixMatch and FlexMatch papers, which represent the current empirical frontier on image benchmarks, and identify which aspects of those results depend on properties of $p(x)$ that may not transfer to your synthetic setting.

### machine_learning_model (0.2s)

## Machine Learning Model

**Definition.** A *machine learning model* is a parameterized family of functions $\mathcal{F} = \{f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} \mid \theta \in \Theta\}$, together with a learning algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ that, given a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n \subset \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$, produces a parameter estimate $\hat{\theta} = \mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})$ minimizing some loss functional $\mathcal{L} : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$. Formally, learning solves

$$\hat{\theta} \in \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x_i), y_i) + \lambda R(\theta),$$

where $R : \Theta \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is a regularizer and $\lambda \geq 0$ controls the bias–variance tradeoff. The triple $(\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{L}, \mathcal{A})$ constitutes the *model specification*; the result $f_{\hat\theta}$ is the *fitted model*. This formulation subsumes linear regression, kernel machines, and deep neural networks as special cases by varying $\Theta$, $\mathcal{L}$, and $\mathcal{A}$.

**Theorem (Generalization bound — PAC-Bayes, Catoni 2007).** Let $P$ be a prior over $\Theta$ independent of $\mathcal{D}$, and let $Q$ be any posterior. With probability at least $1-\delta$ over draws of $\mathcal{D}$,

$$\mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim Q}[R(f_\theta)] \;\leq\; \mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim Q}[\hat{R}(f_\theta)] + \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q \| P) + \ln(1/\delta)}{2n}},$$

where $\hat{R}$ denotes empirical risk and $R$ denotes population risk. The bound makes the usual assumption of i.i.d. draws from a fixed distribution $\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply the change-of-measure inequality $\mathbb{E}_Q[g] \leq \mathrm{KL}(Q\|P) + \ln \mathbb{E}_P[e^g]$ to the random variable $g = n\hat{R}(f_\theta) - nR(f_\theta)$, then invoke Hoeffding's lemma on the bounded loss, and invert the resulting Chernoff bound. $\square$

**Literature context.** Vapnik's VC theory (1971) established dimension-based generalization, but PAC-Bayes bounds (McAllester 1999; Catoni 2007) are tighter in practice and admit data-dependent posteriors. A central open problem is the *overparameterized regime*: modern neural networks with $|\Theta| \gg n$ generalize well empirically yet violate classical bounds, a phenomenon Belkin et al. (2019) formalize as *double descent*. Implicit regularization by stochastic gradient descent — studied via the *edge of stability* (Cohen et al. 2021) and *sharpness-aware minimization* (Foret et al. 2021) — is an active research front without a complete theoretical account. The relationship between architecture inductive bias and the effective dimensionality of $\Theta$ remains an open question linking statistical learning theory to deep learning practice.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the double-descent curve on a controlled setup: train a two-layer MLP on MNIST as width $d$ increases from $d \ll n$ to $d \gg n$, recording test loss at the interpolation threshold $d^* \approx n$. Compute empirical PAC-Bayes bounds using a Gaussian prior and a Laplace-approximation posterior. Observe whether the bound tracks the actual test error across regimes, or fails to explain the benign overfitting region. Vary the label-noise rate to sharpen the discrepancy.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should (i) state precisely which version of the PAC-Bayes theorem is used and verify its assumptions hold; (ii) plot bound tightness versus model complexity on the same axes as the double-descent curve; (iii) discuss whether the bound's failure in the overparameterized regime is a fundamental limitation of the KL term, a prior-choice artifact, or an artifact of the bound's form; and (iv) connect findings to at least one current paper on implicit bias, citing specific theoretical gaps the experiment either supports or challenges.

### information_entropy (29.9s)

## Information Entropy

**Definition.** Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ be a probability space and $X$ a discrete random variable taking values in a finite alphabet $\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ with distribution $p(x) = \Pr[X = x]$. The *Shannon entropy* of $X$ is

$$H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x) \log_2 p(x),$$

with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$. Units are bits when the logarithm is base-2, nats for base-$e$. The quantity $H$ is not an intrinsic property of $\mathcal{X}$ but of the *measure* $p$, a distinction that becomes load-bearing in the continuous generalisation, where the differential entropy $h(X) = -\int p(x)\log p(x)\,dx$ loses non-negativity and invariance under coordinate change.

**Theorem (Shannon, 1948).** Among all probability distributions on an alphabet of size $n$, the uniform distribution $p(x) = 1/n$ uniquely maximises $H$, achieving $H = \log_2 n$ bits. Moreover, $H$ is the unique functional (up to a positive constant) satisfying: (i) continuity in $p$, (ii) symmetry in the arguments, and (iii) the chain rule $H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y\mid X)$.

*Proof sketch.* Concavity of $-t\log t$ and Jensen's inequality give $H(p) \leq \log n$ with equality iff $p$ is uniform. Uniqueness via the axiomatic characterisation follows by showing any functional satisfying (i)–(iii) must agree with $H$ on rational probabilities and, by continuity, on all of $[0,1]^n$; see Khinchin (1957) for the complete argument. $\square$

**Literature context.** Shannon's 1948 paper *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* (Bell Syst. Tech. J.) established $H$ simultaneously as a measure of uncertainty, a lower bound on lossless compression, and—via the source coding theorem—an operational limit: no prefix-free code achieves expected length below $H(X)$ bits per symbol, and Huffman coding reaches $H(X) + 1$. The connection to statistical mechanics runs deep: Jaynes (1957, *Phys. Rev.*) showed that maximising entropy subject to moment constraints recovers the canonical ensemble, reframing thermodynamic equilibrium as an inference problem. What remains genuinely open is the *right* entropy for quantum and non-extensive systems: Tsallis entropy $S_q = (1 - \sum p_i^q)/(q-1)$ and Rényi entropy $H_\alpha = \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum p_i^\alpha$ each generalise Shannon's functional and collapse to it as $q,\alpha \to 1$, yet their operational coding theorems are not as tight, and their relationships to physical entropy in non-equilibrium settings remain active research (see Wilde, *Quantum Information Theory*, 2nd ed., 2017, Ch. 10–11).

**Suggested investigation.** A natural entry project is empirical verification of the source coding theorem under a controlled generative model. Fix a Markov chain on $\{A, C, G, T\}$ with known transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$; the entropy rate is $\bar{H} = -\sum_{i,j}\pi_i P_{ij}\log P_{ij}$ where $\pi$ is the stationary distribution. Generate $10^4$–$10^6$ symbol sequences, apply Huffman and arithmetic coding, and compare achieved compression rates against $\bar{H}$. A meaningful extension: vary $\mathbf{P}$ to interpolate between uniform (maximum entropy, incompressible) and deterministic (zero entropy, trivially compressed), and fit the empirical compression gap as a function of spectral gap $1 - \lambda_2(\mathbf{P})$. The convergence rate to the entropy rate is controlled by the mixing time, connecting to Markov chain theory in a way the standard coding-theory treatment elides.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should: state the entropy rate formula and derive it from the ergodic theorem for Markov chains; report both the theoretical $\bar{H}$ and empirical code lengths with confidence intervals across realisations; include a log-log plot of compression gap versus sequence length to characterise finite-sample convergence; and discuss where the arithmetic coder outperforms Huffman and why (arithmetic coding achieves $\bar{H}$ exactly in the limit, while Huffman has an integer-length constraint). Situate findings relative to the asymptotic equipartition property (AEP), which should be stated and verified empirically as a secondary result.

### supervised_learning (0.2s)

## Supervised Learning

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{X}$ be an input space and $\mathcal{Y}$ a label space. Supervised learning concerns the problem of finding a function $f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ from a finite sample $S = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ drawn i.i.d. from an unknown joint distribution $\mathcal{D}$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$. A learner selects $\hat{f}$ from a hypothesis class $\mathcal{H}$ by minimizing the empirical risk $\hat{\mathcal{R}}(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f(x_i), y_i)$ for a loss $\ell$, with the goal that the true (population) risk $\mathcal{R}(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(f(x),y)]$ is also small. The gap $\mathcal{R}(\hat{f}) - \inf_{f \in \mathcal{H}}\mathcal{R}(f)$ is the *estimation error*; the gap $\inf_{f \in \mathcal{H}}\mathcal{R}(f) - \inf_{f}\mathcal{R}(f)$ is the *approximation error*. The fundamental tension between these two quantities — the bias–variance tradeoff — is what makes supervised learning non-trivial.

**Theorem (Uniform Convergence / Finite-Class Bound).** Suppose $|\mathcal{H}| < \infty$ and $\ell(\cdot,\cdot) \in [0,1]$. Then for any $\delta > 0$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over the draw of $S$,

$$\sup_{f \in \mathcal{H}} |\mathcal{R}(f) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}(f)| \leq \sqrt{\frac{\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Fix $f$; Hoeffding's inequality gives $\Pr[|\mathcal{R}(f) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}(f)| > \epsilon] \leq 2e^{-2n\epsilon^2}$. A union bound over $|\mathcal{H}|$ hypotheses and solving for $\epsilon$ yield the stated bound. $\square$

This finite-class result generalizes to infinite $\mathcal{H}$ via the VC dimension $d_{\mathrm{VC}}(\mathcal{H})$, giving sample complexity $n = O\!\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{VC}} + \log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^2}\right)$ (Vapnik and Chervonenkis 1971; Blumer et al. 1989). Rademacher complexity provides tighter, data-dependent bounds (Bartlett and Mendelson 2002).

**Literature context.** Classical PAC learning theory (Valiant 1984) establishes what is learnable in polynomial time and sample; the No-Free-Lunch theorem (Wolpert 1996) establishes that without inductive bias, no learner dominates. Modern deep learning breaks many classical predictions: overparameterized networks generalize despite zero training loss, a phenomenon explained only partially by implicit regularization (Zhang et al. 2017; Belkin et al. 2019) and the *double descent* curve. Open questions include: (1) a tight characterization of the effective hypothesis class learned by gradient descent on neural networks; (2) the role of data distribution geometry in generalization; (3) extending uniform-convergence bounds to the interpolating regime without vacuousness.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the double-descent experiment of Belkin et al. (2019) on a tabular dataset using polynomial feature expansion or random Fourier features. Fix a training set of size $n$ and sweep model complexity (number of features $p$ from $p \ll n$ to $p \gg n$). Record training error, test error, and the norm of the minimum-norm interpolant. Plot the resulting risk curve and identify the classical peak and the second descent. Then compute VC-dimension-based generalization bounds at each $p$ and verify that they become vacuous ($> 1$) well before interpolation; this concretely demonstrates the gap between classical theory and empirical behavior. As an extension, apply early stopping and compare the risk profile to the explicit regularization (ridge regression) baseline.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should (i) state precisely which risk decomposition is being studied and define all quantities; (ii) give a self-contained proof of the bound used as a baseline; (iii) report the experiment with exact hyperparameters, dataset statistics, and confidence intervals over multiple random seeds; (iv) discuss whether the observations are consistent with any of the proposed theoretical explanations (implicit bias, benign overfitting); and (v) identify one specific open question the experiment does not resolve and propose a follow-up design.

### bootstrapping (40.1s)

## Bootstrapping

**Definition.** Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F$ for some unknown distribution $F$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$, and let $\theta = T(F)$ be a functional of interest (e.g., the mean, median, or a regression coefficient). The *nonparametric bootstrap* (Efron, 1979) estimates the sampling distribution of $\hat{\theta}_n = T(\hat{F}_n)$, where $\hat{F}_n$ is the empirical distribution, by the following procedure: draw $B$ independent bootstrap samples $X_1^*, \ldots, X_n^* \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \hat{F}_n$ (i.e., sample with replacement from the observed data), compute $\hat{\theta}_n^{*(b)} = T(\hat{F}_n^{*(b)})$ for each replicate $b$, and approximate the distribution of $\hat{\theta}_n - \theta$ by that of $\hat{\theta}_n^* - \hat{\theta}_n$ over the bootstrap replicates.

**Theorem (Bootstrap Consistency).** Suppose $T$ is Hadamard differentiable at $F$ tangentially to a set $\mathcal{D}_0$ containing the support of the limit process of $\sqrt{n}(\hat{F}_n - F)$, and let $\mathbb{G}_F$ denote the $F$-Brownian bridge. Then, conditionally on the data (in probability),

$$\sqrt{n}\bigl(\hat{\theta}_n^* - \hat{\theta}_n\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} T'_F(\mathbb{G}_F),$$

where $T'_F$ is the Hadamard derivative of $T$ at $F$, and the limit coincides with the unconditional limit $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} T'_F(\mathbb{G}_F)$.

**Sketch.** The key ingredient is that the empirical process $\sqrt{n}(\hat{F}_n - F)$ converges weakly to $\mathbb{G}_F$ in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal{F})$ (the Glivenko–Cantelli and Donsker theorems). The bootstrap empirical process $\sqrt{n}(\hat{F}_n^* - \hat{F}_n)$ converges to the same limit conditionally on the data, by Multinomial$(n; 1/n, \ldots, 1/n)$ fluctuations of the bootstrap weights. Hadamard differentiability then propagates convergence through the functional $T$ via the functional delta method. See van der Vaart and Wellner (1996, Chapter 3.9) for the complete argument under minimal measurability conditions.

**Literature context.** The bootstrap is theoretically well-understood for smooth functionals and first-order statistics: consistency and second-order accuracy (Beran 1988; Hall 1992) are classical. The picture grows richer—and more open—at the boundaries: (i) *non-smooth functionals* such as the maximum or quantile regression at non-unique solutions can fail to be Hadamard differentiable, invalidating standard consistency (Dümbgen 1993; Fang and Santos 2019); (ii) *high-dimensional and heavy-tailed settings* where $d/n \not\to 0$ require multiplier bootstrap corrections (Chernozhukov, Chetverikov, and Kato 2013); (iii) *bootstrap for dependent data* (block bootstrap, stationary bootstrap) introduces tuning choices whose optimal block length remains an active estimation problem.

**Suggested investigation.** Fix a non-smooth functional — the $\ell^\infty$ norm $T(F) = \|F\|_\infty$, or the $p$-th sample quantile at $p = 0.5$ with a tied data-generating distribution — and compare (a) the standard nonparametric bootstrap, (b) the $m$-out-of-$n$ bootstrap with $m = n^{2/3}$, and (c) subsampling (Politis and Romano 1994) at coverage level $1 - \alpha = 0.95$. Vary $n \in \{50, 200, 1000\}$ and empirically measure coverage of the resulting confidence intervals over $R = 2000$ Monte Carlo replications. Derive analytically which of (a)–(c) is consistent in each case using the Hadamard differentiability criterion, and verify that the empirical coverages align with the theoretical prediction.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should (1) state precisely which notion of bootstrap consistency is in play and what smoothness conditions $T$ must satisfy; (2) provide the functional delta method step explicitly rather than citing it as a black box; (3) separate Monte Carlo error from bootstrap error in the variance decomposition; and (4) document any discrepancy between the theoretical coverage guarantee and the finite-sample empirical coverage, framing it as a gap motivating higher-order or robust bootstrap corrections.

### decision_tree (25.6s)

## Decision Tree

**Definition.** A *decision tree* is a recursive partitioning of the input space $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ into axis-aligned rectangular regions, each assigned a constant prediction. Formally, a binary tree $T$ of depth $D$ defines a partition $\{R_1, \ldots, R_m\}$ of $\mathcal{X}$ such that the predictor takes the form

$$f_T(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j \,\mathbf{1}[\mathbf{x} \in R_j],$$

where $c_j \in \mathbb{R}$ (regression) or $c_j \in \{0,1\}^K$ (classification). Each internal node selects a coordinate $k \in [d]$ and a threshold $\tau$, routing left if $x_k \leq \tau$ and right otherwise. The tree structure and the split parameters $(k, \tau)$ at every node are jointly the object of learning.

**The Greedy Splitting Criterion and Its Optimality Gap.** The canonical training algorithm (CART; Breiman et al. 1984) greedily minimizes an impurity measure at each node. For regression, this is the weighted mean-squared error reduction:

$$\Delta_\text{MSE}(k,\tau) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \;-\; \frac{n_L}{n}\sigma^2_L(k,\tau) \;-\; \frac{n_R}{n}\sigma^2_R(k,\tau),$$

where $n_L, n_R$ are the counts in the left and right child and $\sigma^2_{L/R}$ their response variances. For classification the Gini impurity $G = \sum_k \hat{p}_k(1-\hat{p}_k)$ or information gain replaces the MSE term.

**Theorem (CART approximation ratio, informal; Klusowski 2021).** For a regression tree of depth $D$ learned on $n$ samples from a Lipschitz target $f^*$, the excess risk satisfies

$$\mathbb{E}[\|f_T - f^*\|^2] = O\!\left(n^{-\frac{2}{d+2}} \log n\right)$$

under standard regularity, matching minimax rates for piecewise-constant estimators up to logarithmic factors. This rate degrades exponentially with dimension $d$—the manifestation of the *curse of dimensionality* for decision trees—but the constant depends only weakly on the global Lipschitz constant.

**What Is Known and What Remains Open.** The CART greedy criterion is *not* globally optimal: finding the depth-$D$ tree minimizing training loss exactly is NP-hard (Hyafil & Rivest 1976). This gap has motivated a line of work on *optimal decision trees* (Bertsimas & Dunn 2017; Aglin et al. 2020, the GOSDT framework) that formulate tree learning as integer programming and prove tighter generalization bounds for the resulting trees. A current open question is whether the computational intractability can be circumvented under distributional assumptions (e.g., margin conditions, low intrinsic dimension). Separately, the *interpolation regime*—where fully-grown trees memorize training data—exhibits benign overfitting only under strong structural constraints, unlike neural networks or random forests, making single-tree generalization in the over-parameterized regime an active area.

**Suggested Investigation.** Replicate the Hyafil–Rivest hardness argument for depth-2 trees on a synthetic dataset, then compare CART against GOSDT on a UCI benchmark (e.g., `adult` or `recidivism`) at depth $D \in \{3, 4, 5\}$. Measure: (i) training loss gap between greedy and optimal solutions, (ii) test accuracy, (iii) tree size. Vary $n$ from $10^3$ to $10^5$ and record wall-clock time, since GOSDT's MIP formulation scales super-linearly.

**Report-Writing Note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (a) a precise statement of the hardness result and the depth at which it first applies; (b) the empirical optimality gap as a function of $n$ and $D$; (c) whether the accuracy difference between greedy and optimal trees is statistically significant at the chosen test sizes; and (d) a discussion of when the computational overhead of optimal methods is justified versus when CART suffices.

### bootstrap_aggregating (31.3s)

## Bootstrap Aggregating

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a training set drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution $P$. A *bootstrap sample* $\mathcal{D}^{*b}$ is obtained by sampling $n$ observations from $\mathcal{D}$ uniformly with replacement. Given a base learner $\hat{f}$ and $B$ such bootstrap replicates, the *bootstrap aggregating* (bagging) estimator is

$$\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}(x) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \hat{f}^{*b}(x),$$

where $\hat{f}^{*b}$ is the model trained on $\mathcal{D}^{*b}$. For classification, majority vote replaces the average. The construction was introduced by Breiman (1996) as a computationally tractable approximation to the *ideal aggregate predictor* $f_A(x) = \mathbb{E}_P[\hat{f}(x)]$, which averages over all training sets of size $n$ drawn from $P$.

**Theorem (Variance reduction via aggregation).** Suppose the base learner satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] = \mu(x)$ and $\operatorname{Var}[\hat{f}(x)] = \sigma^2(x)$, and that predictions from distinct bootstrap models have pairwise correlation $\rho$. Then

$$\operatorname{Var}[\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}(x)] = \rho \sigma^2(x) + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2(x).$$

*Proof sketch.* The variance of a mean of $B$ identically distributed random variables with pairwise correlation $\rho$ decomposes as $\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/B$ by straightforward expansion of $\operatorname{Var}[\sum_b Z_b / B]$. As $B \to \infty$ the irreducible floor $\rho\sigma^2(x)$ remains; bias is unchanged throughout. $\square$

The formula isolates the two levers of ensemble design: increasing $B$ drives the second term to zero, while reducing $\rho$ — by injecting randomness into the base learner — reduces the floor. This is the conceptual basis for Random Forests (Breiman 2001), where additional feature-subspace randomisation ($m \ll p$ features at each split) lowers $\rho$ at the cost of higher per-tree variance, trading off optimally for many practical regimes.

**Literature context.** The bias–variance decomposition for squared loss (Geman et al. 1992) gives the theoretical frame within which bagging operates: it attacks variance while leaving bias intact, so it is most effective for low-bias, high-variance learners such as deep unpruned decision trees. Bühlmann and Yu (2002) formalised this via the *subagging* analysis, showing that sampling fractions $s/n < 1$ without replacement achieves similar variance reduction with lower computational cost. The out-of-bag (OOB) error estimate — predictions formed from bootstrap replicates that did not contain a given observation — provides an approximately unbiased generalisation estimate without a held-out set; its consistency under mild regularity conditions was established by Wolpert and Macready (1999). Open questions remain around the optimal choice of $B$ for finite samples under distribution shift, and around theoretical guarantees for bagging in the interpolating (overparameterised) regime relevant to modern neural networks.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement bagging over unpruned CART trees on a moderately high-variance tabular dataset (e.g., UCI Abalone or California Housing). Vary $B \in \{1, 5, 10, 25, 100, 300\}$ and estimate $\hat{\rho}$ empirically as the average pairwise Pearson correlation of tree predictions on a held-out set. Plot $\operatorname{Var}[\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}]$ against $B$ and overlay the theoretical curve from the formula above using your estimated $\hat{\rho}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2$. Compare with subagging at $s = 0.5n$ and with Random Forest ($m = \lfloor\sqrt{p}\rfloor$). Separately, measure OOB error versus $K$-fold cross-validated error as a function of $B$ to probe OOB consistency.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should clearly state the bias–variance decomposition being operationalised, derive or cite the $\rho$–$\sigma^2$ formula, and report empirical $\hat{\rho}$ values alongside the variance curves — not just aggregate test error. The comparison between OOB and cross-validated error should include confidence intervals. If the theoretical and empirical variance curves diverge, the write-up should diagnose whether the discrepancy arises from model correlation heterogeneity, finite-$B$ effects, or violation of the i.i.d. bootstrap assumption.

## Random Forest

**Definition.** A random forest is an ensemble of $M$ decision trees $\{h_m(\mathbf{x}; \Theta_m)\}_{m=1}^{M}$, where each $\Theta_m$ encodes independent randomness injected at two stages: (i) bootstrap resampling of the training set (bagging), and (ii) feature subsampling at each split node, drawing $k \ll p$ candidate features uniformly at random before selecting the locally optimal split. The ensemble prediction is the majority vote (classification) or arithmetic mean (regression):

$$\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} h_m(\mathbf{x};\Theta_m).$$

The canonical choice is $k = \lfloor \sqrt{p} \rfloor$ for classification and $k = \lfloor p/3 \rfloor$ for regression, though these are empirical defaults, not theoretical optima.

**Bias–Variance Decomposition and the Strength–Correlation Trade-off.** The population mean-squared error of the ensemble decomposes as

$$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{f}) = \mathrm{bias}^2 + \frac{1 + (M-1)\bar{\rho}}{M}\bar{\sigma}^2,$$

where $\bar{\rho}$ is the average pairwise correlation between tree predictions and $\bar{\sigma}^2$ is the average per-tree variance. This is Breiman's (2001) fundamental identity. As $M \to \infty$ the variance term converges to $\bar{\rho}\,\bar{\sigma}^2$, so the irreducible variance floor is set entirely by inter-tree correlation. Feature subsampling reduces $\bar{\rho}$ at the cost of slightly increasing $\bar{\sigma}^2$ per tree (each tree sees a degraded feature set, raising individual tree bias). The practical algorithm navigates this trade-off without solving it analytically: the optimal $k$ is problem-dependent and unknown in general.

**Generalization Bound.** Breiman (2001) derived a margin-based bound: for any $\delta > 0$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$P_{XY}\!\left[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) \neq y\right] \leq P_{XY\Theta}\!\left[\mathrm{mr}(\mathbf{x},y) < 0\right] + O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\rho}\,s^2/\bar{\sigma}^2}{n}}\right),$$

where $\mathrm{mr}$ is the margin function and $s^2$ is the variance of the strength statistic. The bound is qualitatively informative but notoriously loose quantitatively, and tightening it remains open. More recent work (Wager & Walther 2015; Mentch & Hooker 2016) recasts individual trees as $U$-statistics, enabling asymptotically normal confidence intervals for forest predictions—an approach that connects random forests to classical nonparametric theory.

**Literature Context.** Breiman (2001, *Machine Learning* 45) is the founding reference; its theoretical analysis is partial and several claimed results were later clarified or corrected (e.g., Biau 2012, *JMLR*). Biau & Devroye (2010) establish $L^2$ consistency under mild regularity. Scornet, Biau & Vert (2015, *Ann. Statist.*) prove consistency of Breiman forests on additive models. The honest forest of Wager & Athey (2018) extends the framework to causal inference via heterogeneous treatment effects, with asymptotic normality guarantees. Open questions include: sharp minimax rates for forests on smooth function classes, optimal feature-subsample schedules, and theoretical justification for out-of-bag error as a generalization proxy.

**Suggested Investigation.** Fix a synthetic additive regression target $f^*(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{j=1}^{5} \sin(x_j)$ in $p = 20$ dimensions. Train forests over a grid of $k \in \{1, 2, 5, 10, 20\}$ and $M \in \{10, 50, 200\}$; measure test MSE, $\hat{\bar{\rho}}$ (estimated from out-of-bag predictions), and per-tree variance. Verify empirically that the Breiman identity holds: compute both sides independently and compare. Then fit an honest causal forest (Wager & Athey) on a synthetic randomized experiment and examine whether the 95% confidence intervals achieve nominal coverage.

**Report-Writing Note.** A rigorous write-up should state the Breiman identity as a formal proposition and derive it from first principles, clearly separating approximation ($M$ finite) from asymptotic ($M \to \infty$) regimes. The empirical section must report $\hat{\bar{\rho}}$ alongside MSE—presenting MSE alone conflates the two components of the trade-off. Discuss whether the Scornet–Biau–Vert consistency conditions hold for your chosen $f^*$, and note any gap between the theoretical bound and observed test error.

## Facial Recognition

**Definition.** Facial recognition is the computational problem of mapping a raw image $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$ to an identity $y \in \mathcal{Y}$ via a learned function $f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{S}^{d-1}$, where $\mathcal{S}^{d-1}$ is the unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Two images $\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j$ are declared a match when $\langle f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i), f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_j) \rangle \geq \tau$ for some threshold $\tau \in [-1,1]$. The central challenge is learning an embedding geometry in which intra-class distances are small and inter-class distances are large — a requirement formalized by metric learning objectives.

**Theorem (ArcFace margin condition).** Let $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times |\mathcal{Y}|}$ be a weight matrix with $\ell_2$-normalized columns and let $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) / \|f_\theta(\mathbf{x})\|$. The ArcFace loss (Deng et al., 2019) is

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{e^{s(\cos(\theta_{y_i} + m))}}{e^{s(\cos(\theta_{y_i} + m))} + \sum_{j \neq y_i} e^{s \cos \theta_j}},$$

where $\theta_j = \arccos(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^\top \mathbf{w}_j)$ is the angle between embedding and class weight, $m > 0$ is an additive angular margin, and $s > 0$ is a scale factor. For the loss to be well-defined with a positive gradient, one must have $\theta_{y_i} + m < \pi$, constraining admissible angles to $\theta_{y_i} \in [0, \pi - m)$. The geodesic margin on $\mathcal{S}^{d-1}$ is then $m$ radians, and the decision boundary is the great-circle locus $\cos(\theta_i + m) = \cos \theta_j$.

*Proof sketch.* The constraint follows directly from the monotonicity of cosine on $[0, \pi]$: the angular penalty $\theta_{y_i} \mapsto \theta_{y_i} + m$ must remain in the domain where the softmax denominator exceeds the numerator for incorrect classes. One verifies this by requiring the logit of the true class after penalty to be less than the unpunished logit at the margin angle; see Deng et al. (2019), Proposition 1, for the full derivation. $\square$

**Literature context.** The field progresses along two largely independent axes: representation quality and evaluation fairness. On the first axis, contrastive and angular-margin losses — DeepFace (Taigman et al., 2014), FaceNet (Schroff et al., 2015), ArcFace, and their successors — have driven verification error on LFW below 0.2 %, effectively saturating that benchmark. The open problem has shifted to distribution shift: recognition under extreme pose ($>60°$ yaw), low resolution ($<32 \times 32$ pixels), and heterogeneous modalities (NIR, thermal). On the second axis, Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) demonstrated that commercial systems exhibit accuracy disparities exceeding 30 percentage points across gender–race strata, and subsequent work (Raji and Buolamwini, 2019) shows these gaps persist in updated systems. Whether margin-based losses inherently encode demographic bias through imbalanced class statistics remains an active and contested question.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the ArcFace training on a subset of MS-Celeb-1M (or its cleaned variant, MS1MV3) with $d = 512$ and $m \in \{0.3, 0.4, 0.5\}$. Evaluate verification AUC on LFW and separately report TAR@FAR=0.001 stratified by the Fitzpatrick skin-tone scale using the BUPT-Balancedface partition. Vary the per-class sample count $n_c$ by sub-sampling majority classes and examine how the angular margin $m$ interacts with class imbalance — specifically, whether a larger $m$ widens or closes the inter-demographic gap. A lightweight ablation is feasible in under 48 GPU-hours with a ResNet-50 backbone.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should rigorously separate two claims: (1) that the chosen loss achieves competitive aggregate verification performance, supported by standard ROC metrics on LFW or IJB-C; and (2) that performance is equitable across demographic groups, supported by stratified TAR/FAR tables. The report should state the exact training data provenance, since MS-Celeb-1M has known label noise and consent issues that affect reproducibility and ethical standing. Discuss whether observed disparities are attributable to the loss geometry, the training data imbalance, or the evaluation set construction — these three hypotheses have distinct experimental signatures and should not be conflated.
