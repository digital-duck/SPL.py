## Sequence

You already know the intuition: a sequence is a list of things in a specific order. But there's more structure here than a plain list. Unlike a *set*, where order doesn't matter, a sequence cares deeply about position — the third term is different from the first, even if they happen to have the same value.

**Formal structure.** A sequence is formally written as $(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots)$, where each $a_n$ is called the **$n$-th term** and $n$ is its *index*. The index usually starts at $1$ (sometimes $0$). What makes a sequence powerful is that each term can be defined by a **rule** — a formula that takes an index and produces a value.

Two common types:

- **Explicit formula**: gives you $a_n$ directly. For example, $a_n = 2n + 1$ produces $3, 5, 7, 9, \ldots$
- **Recursive formula**: defines each term using the one before it. For example, $a_1 = 1$ and $a_n = a_{n-1} + 3$ produces $1, 4, 7, 10, \ldots$

**Pattern to remember.** If consecutive terms differ by a constant amount $d$, you have an *arithmetic sequence* and the explicit formula is always:

$$a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d$$

This single formula lets you jump directly to any term without listing everything before it.

### time_series (0.1s)

## Time Series

You already know that a sequence of numbers can tell a story — your height measured every birthday, the temperature at noon each day, or the closing price of a stock each Friday. When measurements are taken **at regular intervals over time**, the resulting list is called a **time series**. The key idea is that *order matters*: shuffling the values destroys the meaning.

**Structure of a time series**

A time series is written as $x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots, x_n$, where the subscript $t$ labels the time step and $x_t$ is the observation at step $t$.

One of the most important features to look for is a **trend** — a long-run direction (rising, falling, or flat). For example, global average temperature has shown an upward trend over recent decades. Spotting a trend in raw data can be tricky, though, because every real measurement comes with a little random noise that makes the picture jagged.

**A simple tool: the moving average**

To smooth out noise and reveal the trend, replace each value with the average of its neighbors:

$$\bar{x}_t = \frac{x_{t-1} + x_t + x_{t+1}}{3}$$

This three-point moving average is one of the simplest tools in data analysis — it keeps the overall direction while ironing out the bumps.

### error (0.2s)

## Error

You already know that measurements are never perfect — a ruler can only be so precise, and rounding $\pi$ to $3.14$ leaves something out. That leftover gap has a name: **error**. But in mathematics, error is not a mistake to be ashamed of; it is a quantity to be measured, managed, and minimized.

**Absolute error** is the simplest form. If the true value is $x$ and your estimate is $\hat{x}$, then:

$$\text{Absolute Error} = |x - \hat{x}|$$

The absolute value bars matter — error is always non-negative, because we care about *how far off* you are, not which direction.

Sometimes the raw gap is misleading. Being off by $1$ cm when measuring a pencil is very different from being off by $1$ cm when measuring a football field. **Relative error** puts the gap in proportion:

$$\text{Relative Error} = \frac{|x - \hat{x}|}{|x|}$$

Multiply by $100$ and you get **percent error**, the version most common in science classes.

A key pattern: errors *accumulate*. If you round two numbers before adding them, the errors in each can add together — or, in lucky cases, partially cancel. This is why mathematicians track error carefully through every step of a calculation, not just at the end.

## Time Series Model

**Recap:** You already know that data can be plotted over time — stock prices, daily temperatures, your monthly video game scores. A *time series* is just a sequence of values measured at regular intervals. The interesting question is: can we find a hidden pattern and use it to predict what comes next?

**Structure of the idea:** A time series model assumes that a future value depends, at least partly, on past values. The simplest version of this is the **autoregressive (AR(1)) model** — "autoregressive" because the series is regressed on itself:

$$x_t = \phi \cdot x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$$

Here $x_t$ is the value at time $t$, $\phi$ is a coefficient that controls how strongly the past influences the future, and $\varepsilon_t$ is random noise representing unpredictable fluctuations. The behavior of the series hinges entirely on $\phi$. When $|\phi| < 1$, disturbances gradually die out and the series stays stable — each jolt fades rather than compounding. When $|\phi| \geq 1$, small shocks accumulate and the series can drift or explode without bound.

**Simple rule:** Think of $\phi$ as a "memory dial." A value close to 1 (say, 0.95) means the series has a long memory — yesterday's value is nearly preserved today. A value close to 0 (say, 0.1) means the series forgets quickly — each observation is almost independent of the last.

## Forecasting

You already know forecasting from daily life — a weather app says "70% chance of rain," and you grab an umbrella just in case. That percentage is not a guess pulled from thin air; it is a number built from patterns in past data.

**The structure behind a forecast.** A forecast is a *probability distribution over future outcomes*, not just a single number. When a meteorologist says there is a 70% chance of rain, they mean: in all past situations that looked like today's atmosphere, it rained roughly 70% of the time. Formally, if $X$ is the outcome (rain or no rain), we write the forecast as $P(X = \text{rain}) = 0.70$.

For quantities that can take many values — like tomorrow's high temperature — forecasters build a *probability distribution*, often shaped like a bell curve centered at the most likely value $\mu$ with spread $\sigma$:

$$P(\text{temp} = t) \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu,\, \sigma^2)$$

A wider bell (large $\sigma$) means high uncertainty; a narrow bell means the model is confident.

**The key rule.** A good forecast must be *calibrated*: if you say 70% for rain on 100 different days, it should actually rain on about 70 of them. Overconfidence ($\sigma$ too small) and underconfidence ($\sigma$ too large) are both errors.

### linear_regression_forecasting (14.8s)

## Linear Regression Forecasting

You already know that a scatter plot can reveal a trend — when one variable goes up, another tends to follow. Linear regression takes that intuition and makes it precise: it finds the single straight line that best summarizes the relationship, so you can use it to **predict** values you have not measured yet.

### The Line's Anatomy

Every straight line has the form

$$\hat{y} = mx + b$$

where $x$ is the input (the thing you know), $\hat{y}$ is the predicted output (what you want to estimate), $m$ is the **slope** (how much $\hat{y}$ changes per one-unit increase in $x$), and $b$ is the **intercept** (the predicted value when $x = 0$).

The "best" line is the one that minimizes how far the predictions stray from the actual data points. Those gaps, called **residuals**, are the differences $y_i - \hat{y}_i$ for each observation $i$. Regression finds $m$ and $b$ so that the sum of squared residuals is as small as possible — this is called the **least-squares criterion**.

### Using the Line to Forecast

Once $m$ and $b$ are known, forecasting is just substitution. If you fit a line to weekly study hours vs. quiz scores and get $\hat{y} = 4.5x + 50$, then studying 8 hours predicts a score of $4.5(8) + 50 = 86$.

One caution: the line is only reliable **within** the range of your data. Predicting far outside that range — called *extrapolation* — is risky because the trend may not continue.
