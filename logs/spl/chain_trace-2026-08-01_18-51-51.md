### data (10.7s)

## Data

Imagine you and your friends each pick a favourite colour. You write them all down on sticky notes: red, blue, blue, green, red, red. That list — those sticky notes — is **data**.

Data is just a collection of facts about the world. It could be colours, names, temperatures, or how many jumping jacks you did each day this week. By itself, one sticky note isn't very interesting. But when you collect many of them together, something magical happens: you can start to see patterns. "Hey, most of us picked red!"

Think of data as raw ingredients in a kitchen. Flour, eggs, and sugar sitting in bowls don't tell you much. But once you look at them together and start asking questions — *How much of each do I have? Which one do I have the most of?* — you're already doing something powerful.

We use a simple idea to organise data: each piece of information is called an **observation**, and each type of information (like "colour" or "count") is called a **variable**.

**Now you try:** Ask five friends how many pets they have. Write down each answer. You just collected your first dataset!

### data_collection (10.3s)

## Data Collection

**The Bug Jar Experiment**

Imagine you and your friends want to answer a big question: "Which bugs live in our school garden?" You could just *guess* — but guesses aren't very reliable. Instead, you grab some jars and spend an afternoon actually looking, catching, counting, and writing down what you find. That careful looking-and-writing is **data collection**.

**Why does this feel right?**
Answers based on real observations are stronger than answers based on feelings. When you write down "3 ladybugs, 7 ants, 1 butterfly," those marks become *evidence* — something you can show someone else and they can check.

**The key idea**
Data collection means gathering real observations or measurements about the world in an organized way, so you can learn something true from them. Good data collection is *fair* (you check every corner, not just the sunny spot), *consistent* (everyone counts the same way), and *recorded* (written down before you forget!).

**Now you try**
Pick one question — "How many cars are red on our street?" or "How many students bring lunch vs. buy lunch?" — then design a simple tally sheet and collect your own data for one day.

### data_preparation (11.3s)

## Data Preparation

**The Messy Backpack Problem**

Imagine your teacher asks everyone to bring their favorite snack to share. Kids arrive with chips still in crinkled bags, apples with stickers on them, and cookies crumbled at the bottom of a lunchbox. Before anyone can eat, someone has to sort, clean, and arrange everything neatly on a tray.

That's exactly what *data preparation* is.

A computer is like that teacher — it can only work well with information that is clean, organized, and in the right shape. Raw data is messy: numbers written differently, missing pieces, or things mixed up that shouldn't be. Preparing data means washing away the mess so the computer can actually learn something useful.

Think of it as three kitchen steps: **clean** (remove the dirt), **sort** (put like things together), and **measure** (make sure everything is the right size). If you skip any step, the recipe goes wrong.

Formally, we say data preparation transforms a raw dataset $D$ into a ready dataset $D'$ where every entry is complete, consistent, and comparable.

**Now you try:** Look at this list of ages — `7, "eight", 9, ?, 10` — which entries need fixing before a computer could add them up?

### data_analysis (10.5s)

## Data Analysis

**The Mystery of the Missing Cookies**

Imagine you bake cookies every day for a week. Monday: 12 cookies disappear. Tuesday: 8. Wednesday: 15. Thursday: 7. Friday: 20. Something strange is happening — but what?

**Noticing Patterns**

Data analysis is simply the art of *looking carefully at numbers to find the story hiding inside them*. Those cookie counts are data — raw facts. But when you line them up and ask questions, magic happens. Which day had the most disappearing cookies? Which had the fewest? Is there a pattern?

You might notice cookies vanish most on days ending in "y" — well, that's every day! But maybe *more* disappear on Fridays. That's a real pattern worth investigating.

**Finding the Story**

Data analysis means collecting, organising, and questioning information to answer "What's really going on?" You turn a messy pile of numbers into clear answers.

**Now you try**

Ask five friends how many books they read last month. Write down the numbers. Which is biggest? Smallest? What's the most common answer? Congratulations — you just did data analysis!

### predictive_analytics (9.9s)

## Predictive Analytics

**The Weather Guesser Game**

Have you ever looked at a dark, cloudy sky and thought, "I should bring an umbrella today"? You didn't *know* it would rain — but you made a smart guess based on what you'd seen before. That's predictive analytics!

**What's the Big Idea?**

Predictive analytics means using *patterns from the past* to make smart guesses about the future. Your brain does this all the time. If you always feel tired after skipping breakfast, you *predict* tomorrow will be rough if you skip it again.

Computers do the same thing — but with millions of examples. A computer might study thousands of basketball games and notice: "Teams that lead by 20 points at halftime win 95% of the time." Now it can *predict* the winner before the game ends!

**The Recipe**

1. Collect past data (what happened before)
2. Find patterns (what tends to follow what)
3. Apply to new situations (make your prediction)

**Now You Try**

Keep a week-long diary: did you sleep well? How did your next day go? After seven days, look back — can *you* predict a good day from a good night's sleep?
