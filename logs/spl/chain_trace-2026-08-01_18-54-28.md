### data (27.2s)

## Data

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P_\theta)$ be a probability space parametrized by $\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$. A *dataset* of size $n$ is a measurable map $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \ldots, X_n) : \Omega \to \mathcal{X}^n$, where $\mathcal{X}$ is the *sample space*. The realized values $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in \mathcal{X}^n$ constitute the *observed data*. This deceptively simple definition conceals a rich structure: data are not merely numbers but a sample from a geometry — the statistical manifold $\mathcal{M} = \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}$ equipped with the Fisher–Rao metric $g_{ij}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i \log p_\theta \, \partial_j \log p_\theta]$.

**Theorem (Fisher–Neyman Factorization).** A statistic $T(\mathbf{X})$ is *sufficient* for $\theta$ if and only if the likelihood factors as
$$
p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = h(\mathbf{x}) \cdot g_\theta(T(\mathbf{x}))
$$
for non-negative measurable functions $h$ and $g_\theta$. The proof follows by verifying that the conditional distribution $P_\theta(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x} \mid T(\mathbf{X}) = t)$ is independent of $\theta$, so $T$ retains all information about $\theta$ that $\mathbf{X}$ carries. The exponential family $p_\theta(x) = h(x)\exp(\eta(\theta)^\top T(x) - A(\theta))$ attains the minimum sufficient statistic $T(\mathbf{X}) = \sum_{i=1}^n T(x_i)$, which by the Cramér–Rao bound achieves variance $[n \, \mathcal{I}(\theta)]^{-1}$, where $\mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_\theta[\nabla^2_\theta \log p_\theta]$ is the Fisher information.

**Literature context.** Classical statistics treats data as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples, but this assumption breaks in nearly every modern setting. Temporally correlated sequences, graph-structured observations, and distributional shift between training and deployment are the rule, not the exception. Amari's information geometry (2016) reframes the question: what is the intrinsic geometry of the data manifold, and how does it constrain estimation? Recent work in *data-centric AI* (Ng et al., 2021; Zha et al., 2023) argues that model performance saturates faster when data quality is improved than when architectures are scaled — a claim that lacks a tight formal theory. The question of *how much data is enough*, formally posed as the sample complexity of learning a concept class $\mathcal{H}$ to error $\epsilon$ with probability $1-\delta$, is answered by VC theory: $n = O(\epsilon^{-1}(\text{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}) + \log \delta^{-1}))$. Yet this bound is distribution-free and often vacuous for deep networks, whose effective complexity adapts to data geometry in ways still not fully understood.

**Suggested investigation.** Generate synthetic data from a two-component Gaussian mixture $P_\theta = \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma^2) + \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma^2)$ with known $\theta = (\mu_1, \mu_2, \sigma^2)$. For varying $n$, compute the MLE $\hat\theta$ and measure the empirical Fisher information matrix from the Hessian of the log-likelihood. Compare the observed variance of $\hat\theta$ against the Cramér–Rao lower bound. Then introduce a deliberate covariate shift by drawing test data from a perturbed mixture and quantify the degradation in estimation error. Extend to a kernel density estimator and compare bandwidth selection criteria (Silverman's rule vs. cross-validation) as a function of $n$.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a precise statement of the model and identifiability conditions, (2) a derivation of $\mathcal{I}(\theta)$ for the mixture in closed form or via numerical quadrature with error bounds, (3) empirical convergence plots with confidence intervals across repeated trials, and (4) a discussion of how covariate shift invalidates the standard Cramér–Rao guarantee and what corrections (importance weighting, domain adaptation) partially restore it.

### data_collection (26.8s)

## Data Collection

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a measurable space (the *feature space*) and $\mathcal{Y}$ a response space. A *data collection protocol* is a triple $\mathcal{D} = (\Pi, \mu, n)$, where $\Pi : \mathcal{X} \to [0,1]$ is a sampling distribution over candidate instances, $\mu : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is the (unknown) labeling mechanism, and $n \in \mathbb{N}$ is the budget. The output is a dataset $S = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with $(x_i, y_i) \sim \Pi \otimes \mu$. The central question is not merely *how* to draw samples, but *which* $\Pi$ minimizes downstream estimation error under budget constraints — a question that remains incompletely answered for heterogeneous, high-dimensional, or sequentially-acquired data.

**Theorem (Bias–Variance Decomposition under Covariate Shift).** Let $\hat{f}_S$ be an estimator trained on $S$ and evaluated under a *test* distribution $Q \neq \Pi$. Define the importance weight $w(x) = dQ/d\Pi(x)$. Then for squared-error loss:

$$\mathbb{E}_S\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}} (\hat{f}_S(x) - \mu(x))^2 \, dQ(x)\right] = \underbrace{\int w(x)^2 \,\mathrm{Var}_S[\hat{f}_S(x)] \, d\Pi(x)}_{\text{weighted variance}} + \underbrace{\int (\mathbb{E}_S[\hat{f}_S(x)] - \mu(x))^2 \, dQ(x)}_{\text{bias}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply the standard bias–variance identity pointwise under $Q$, then write $dQ = w \, d\Pi$ and interchange expectation and integral by Fubini (justified when $w \in L^2(\Pi)$). $\square$

The theorem makes the cost of a poorly chosen $\Pi$ explicit: large $w(x)$ inflates variance at regions where $\Pi$ under-samples. Active data collection — choosing $\Pi$ adaptively to reduce $\max_x w(x)$ — is therefore a principled design objective, not an engineering afterthought.

**Literature context.** Classical survey sampling (Cochran 1977) characterizes optimal stratified designs for finite populations, but assumes the model class and test distribution are known. The modern machine-learning literature reframes the problem under *active learning* (Settles 2009; Balcan et al. 2006), *optimal experimental design* (Fedorov 1972; Atkinson et al. 2007), and *distribution shift* (Sugiyama & Kawanabe 2012). Open problems include: (i) optimal $\Pi$ under *unknown* $Q$ with only unlabeled target samples available; (ii) budget allocation when labels arrive with delay or noise; (iii) sequential collection protocols that are provably near-optimal without oracle access to $\mu$.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement stratified vs. uncertainty-sampling active learning on a synthetic regression task where $Q$ is a shifted Gaussian. Fix $n = 200$ and vary $\Pi$ from uniform to oracle-optimal (derived analytically from $w$). Measure the integrated mean-squared error under $Q$ at each checkpoint $n \in \{20, 50, 100, 200\}$. Replicate the covariate-shift correction of Sugiyama et al. (2007) using kernel mean matching, and compare corrected vs. uncorrected estimators. Quantify how quickly the bias term in the decomposition above dominates variance as shift severity increases.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: the precise sampling distribution $\Pi$ used at each stage and the rationale for its choice; an empirical verification of the bias–variance decomposition (plot each term separately across $n$); and a comparison against at least one baseline from the literature with matching hyperparameter budget. The conclusion should state whether the observed gap between sampling strategies is statistically significant (bootstrap confidence intervals) and identify which regime — low $n$, high shift, or noisy labels — most severely degrades the naive uniform baseline.

### data_preparation (28.4s)

## Data Preparation

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ be the feature space and $\mathcal{Y}$ the label space. A *data preparation pipeline* is a measurable map $T : \mathcal{D}_{\text{raw}} \to \mathcal{D}_{\text{clean}}$, where $\mathcal{D}_{\text{raw}}$ is a multiset of partially-observed, possibly corrupted tuples drawn from an unknown source distribution $P_{\text{src}}$, and $\mathcal{D}_{\text{clean}}$ is a dataset suitable for downstream learning. The pipeline $T$ decomposes into a composition of operators — imputation $\phi$, normalization $\nu$, encoding $\epsilon$, and selection $\sigma$ — each of which introduces its own inductive bias into the learning problem. Formally, $T = \sigma \circ \epsilon \circ \nu \circ \phi$, though in practice the operators may be interleaved or conditioned on each other.

**Theorem (Bias Propagation).** Let $\hat{P}$ be the empirical distribution of $\mathcal{D}_{\text{clean}} = T(\mathcal{D}_{\text{raw}})$. If $T$ is not surjective onto the support of $P_{\text{src}}$, then any empirical risk minimizer $\hat{f}$ trained on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{clean}}$ satisfies

$$\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{src}}}[\ell(\hat{f}(X), Y)] \geq \mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}[\ell(\hat{f}(X), Y)] + \delta(T, P_{\text{src}}),$$

where $\delta(T, P_{\text{src}}) \geq 0$ is a preparation-induced distributional gap that vanishes only if $\hat{P} \ll P_{\text{src}}$ and $T$ preserves the sufficient statistics of $P_{\text{src}}$ for predicting $Y$.

*Proof sketch.* By the data processing inequality (Cover & Thomas, 2006, §2.8), any deterministic map $T$ cannot increase mutual information: $I(X; Y) \geq I(T(X); Y)$. The gap $\delta$ captures the information destroyed by $T$, and positive $\delta$ means the Bayes error on the cleaned distribution is strictly lower than on the source distribution — the model has been trained on a problem that is artificially easier or structurally different from deployment. $\square$

**Literature context.** The formal study of data preparation as a first-class modeling decision, rather than a preprocessing afterthought, is relatively recent. Sculley et al. (2015) empirically documented *hidden technical debt* arising from undocumented pipeline choices in large-scale ML systems. Breck et al. (2017) proposed data validation schemas to formalize distribution expectations. On the theoretical side, Goel et al. (2020) analyzed the interaction between imputation strategy and downstream generalization under missing-at-random (MAR) versus missing-not-at-random (MNAR) assumptions, showing that mean imputation is consistent under MAR but introduces systematic bias under MNAR. The *AutoML* and *AutoFE* literature (Feurer et al., 2015; Kanter & Veeramachaneni, 2015) frames operator selection as a hyperparameter search problem, but largely sidesteps the question of what $\delta$ is incurred by automated choices. Key open questions include: (1) when is $\delta$ estimable without access to $P_{\text{src}}$? (2) can a preparation pipeline be jointly optimized end-to-end with the downstream model under a unified loss?

**Suggested investigation.** Take the UCI Adult or Credit-G dataset, artificially inject missingness under both MAR and MNAR mechanisms at rates $r \in \{0.05, 0.15, 0.30\}$, and apply four imputation strategies (mean, median, k-NN, iterative/MICE). For each combination, train a fixed classifier (e.g., logistic regression) and measure (a) in-sample AUC, (b) AUC on held-out data drawn from the original distribution, and (c) calibration error. Estimate $\delta$ empirically as the gap between (a) and (b). Plot $\delta$ as a function of $r$ and missingness mechanism for each imputation strategy. Extend the experiment to include a normalization ablation: compare min-max scaling against standardization and assess their interaction with imputation on the bias gap.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should precisely state the missingness mechanism assumed, provide the full pipeline specification $T$ as pseudocode, report both in-distribution and out-of-distribution metrics separately, and quantify $\delta$ with confidence intervals via bootstrap. The report should argue — not merely observe — whether the observed gaps are explained by the information-theoretic bound above, and if not, propose a hypothesis for the discrepancy.

### data_analysis (30.2s)

## Data Analysis

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space and let $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ be a sequence of random variables with values in a measurable space $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{B})$. *Data analysis* is, formally, the study of functionals $T : \mathcal{X}^n \to \Theta$ — estimators, test statistics, predictors, or exploratory summaries — together with their sampling distributions, asymptotic behavior, and decision-theoretic optimality properties. The objects of primary interest are the bias–variance decomposition of squared-error risk, the convergence of empirical measures to the population measure, and the sensitivity of conclusions to model misspecification. Crucially, none of these properties are fully settled in the high-dimensional, dependent-data, or distribution-shift regimes that now dominate applied work.

**Theorem (Uniform Law of Large Numbers / Glivenko–Cantelli).** Let $F_n(x) = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[X_i \leq x]$ denote the empirical distribution function of an i.i.d. sample from $F$. Then
$$\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |F_n(x) - F(x)| \xrightarrow{a.s.} 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

*Proof sketch.* Fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose a finite $\varepsilon$-net $\{x_1, \ldots, x_k\}$ in the sense that every interval $(-\infty, x]$ is approximated to within $\varepsilon$ in $F$-measure. By the strong law, $F_n(x_j) \to F(x_j)$ almost surely for each $j$; monotonicity of $F_n$ and $F$ then propagates pointwise convergence to uniform convergence. The Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality sharpens this to a finite-sample exponential bound: $P(\sup_x |F_n(x) - F(x)| > \varepsilon) \leq 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$. $\square$

The DKW bound is sharp up to the constant 2 (Massart, 1990), and its generalization to VC classes — the fundamental theorem of statistical learning — underpins PAC learning theory. The analogous result for dependent sequences requires mixing conditions; for $\phi$-mixing processes the bound degrades by a factor related to the mixing coefficients, an area of active research under the rubric of *non-stationary statistical learning* (Kuznetsov and Mohri, 2017).

**What is known and what remains open.** Classical multivariate analysis (PCA, regression, MANOVA) assumes $n \gg p$. The high-dimensional regime $p/n \to \gamma \in (0, \infty)$ is now well-characterized by random matrix theory: the Marchenko–Pastur law describes the empirical spectral distribution of $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}/n$, and the BBP transition (Baik, Ben Arous, Péché, 2005) locates the phase transition for detectability of low-rank signal. Open questions include finite-sample rates for spiked covariance estimation under heavy tails, optimal multiple-testing correction under arbitrary dependence, and causal identifiability from observational data under unmeasured confounding.

**Suggested investigation.** Take a real tabular dataset (e.g., UCI repository, $n \approx 500$–$2000$, $p \approx 20$–$100$). First, compute the empirical spectral distribution of $\hat{\Sigma} = n^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ and overlay the Marchenko–Pastur law for the measured $\gamma = p/n$. Identify which eigenvalues lie outside the bulk — candidate signal directions. Second, apply the DKW-based bootstrap to construct distribution-free confidence bands for the marginal CDFs; compare coverage against a Gaussian-assumption confidence band. Third, introduce a mild distribution shift by sub-sampling a covariate-stratified split and measure how rapidly the dominant principal components rotate.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (i) whether the observed bulk spectrum is consistent with the null (all-noise) Marchenko–Pastur prediction, and what $p$-value a Tracy–Widom test assigns to the largest eigenvalue; (ii) empirical coverage of bootstrap confidence bands at the nominal $95\%$ level across 500 simulation replicates; (iii) a quantitative measure (e.g., principal angle between subspaces) of how the leading eigenspace shifts across distribution splits, with a discussion of whether the shift exceeds what sampling variability alone predicts. These three results together constitute a minimal reproducible audit of a dataset prior to any modeling step.

## Predictive Analytics

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and let $\{(X_t, Y_t)\}_{t=1}^{T}$ be a stochastic process where $X_t \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is the feature vector and $Y_t \in \mathcal{Y}$ is the target at time $t$. Predictive analytics is the task of constructing a measurable function $f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ that minimizes the expected loss $\mathcal{R}(f) = \mathbb{E}[\ell(f(X), Y)]$ over a hypothesis class $\mathcal{H}$, where $\ell : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is a prescribed loss function. The challenge is not merely fitting $f$ to historical data but bounding the *generalization gap* $\mathcal{R}(f) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f)$, where $\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n$ denotes the empirical risk on $n$ observations.

**Theorem (Uniform Convergence, Vapnik–Chervonenkis).** Let $\mathcal{H}$ have VC dimension $d_{\mathrm{VC}} < \infty$. Then for any $\delta \in (0,1)$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$\sup_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \left| \mathcal{R}(f) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f) \right| \leq \sqrt{\frac{d_{\mathrm{VC}} \ln(2n/d_{\mathrm{VC}}) + \ln(4/\delta)}{2n}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply a symmetrization argument to reduce the sup over $\mathcal{H}$ to its expected value over ghost samples, bound the resulting expression by the growth function $\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(2n)$, invoke the Sauer–Shelah lemma $\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(m) \leq (em/d_{\mathrm{VC}})^{d_{\mathrm{VC}}}$, and conclude via a union bound over the $2n$-point discretization. $\square$

This classical result is finite-sample and distribution-free, but it is notoriously loose in practice and says nothing about the *bias* of $\hat{f}$ — the irreducible misfit arising from misspecification of $\mathcal{H}$.

**Literature context.** The VC bound and its PAC-learning descendants (Blumer et al., 1989; Vapnik, 1998) established the theoretical skeleton. Modern refinements include Rademacher complexity bounds (Bartlett & Mendelson, 2002), which are data-dependent and tighter for structured hypothesis classes, and margin theory for ensemble methods (Schapire et al., 1998). For temporal and distribution-shifted settings — the regime most predictive-analytics deployments actually inhabit — the i.i.d. assumption underlying VC theory breaks down; mixing-time extensions (Yu, 1994) and recent work on distribution shift (Quinonero-Candela et al., 2009; Koh et al., 2021) attempt to repair this. A genuinely open question is how to obtain *tight, actionable* generalization bounds for large neural predictors under realistic covariate shift — current bounds often require impractically conservative confidence thresholds.

**Suggested investigation.** Take a real-world tabular dataset exhibiting temporal drift (e.g., the M5 Forecasting competition data or UCI Electricity). Fit three predictors — regularized linear regression, gradient-boosted trees, and a two-layer MLP — under a rolling-window evaluation protocol. For each model, compute empirical Rademacher complexity estimates via Monte Carlo sign-flipping (Bartlett & Mendelson, 2002, §3) and compare the resulting generalization bounds against the measured test loss gap. Investigate how aggressively the bounds tighten as the window grows, and whether variance dominates bias in the gap at realistic sample sizes.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up of this investigation should (i) state the data-generating process assumptions precisely and justify the rolling-window protocol relative to stationarity tests, (ii) report both the empirical gap and the theoretical bound with their confidence levels, (iii) identify at what sample size each bound becomes vacuous, and (iv) articulate a concrete failure mode — a distribution-shift scenario under which the bound's assumptions visibly break, motivating further research into non-stationary generalization theory.
