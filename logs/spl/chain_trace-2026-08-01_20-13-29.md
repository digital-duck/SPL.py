## Audience

You already know that you talk differently to your little sibling than to your science teacher. That shift is you instinctively adjusting for your *audience* — the person or group you are communicating with. But audience isn't just about tone. It shapes every decision a writer or speaker makes: word choice, how much background to give, what examples to pick, even how long to be.

**What "audience" actually controls**

Think of a message as having three layers:

1. **What you say** — the raw content.
2. **How you say it** — vocabulary level, formality, sentence length.
3. **What you assume** — the knowledge you expect the reader already has.

A skilled communicator adjusts all three layers at once. A doctor writing a medical journal article assumes the reader knows what "myocardial infarction" means. The same doctor writing a pamphlet for patients swaps it for "heart attack." Same fact, completely different text.

A useful shorthand: imagine one specific person from your audience and ask, *"Would this sentence make sense to them on a first read?"* If yes, keep it. If no, revise.

**The core rule**

> Match your vocabulary, assumed knowledge, and detail level to what your audience already knows — not to what *you* know.

This means experts write *shorter* explanations for each other (shared vocabulary does the work) but *longer* ones for beginners (every term needs unpacking).

### data_visualization_choice (13.5s)

## Data Visualization Choice

You already know that a picture is worth a thousand words — but the *wrong* picture can be worth negative a thousand. Choosing how to display data is not decoration; it is a communication decision that either reveals the truth or hides it.

Every dataset has a **shape** — a relationship between variables — and different chart types are built to show different shapes. Think of it as matching the tool to the job:

- **Bar charts** compare discrete categories. If you want to show how many students prefer each pizza topping, bars let you rank and compare at a glance.
- **Line charts** show change over a continuous variable, usually time. Stock prices, temperature across a day, or your test scores across a semester all have an order that bars would destroy.
- **Scatter plots** reveal relationships between two numeric variables. Plotting height vs. shoe size lets you see whether a pattern (correlation) exists — something a bar chart cannot show.
- **Pie charts** show parts of a whole, but only work cleanly when you have fewer than five or six slices and the differences are large enough to see.

A useful rule: ask yourself *what question this chart must answer*. "Which is biggest?" → bar. "How does it change?" → line. "Do these two things move together?" → scatter. "What share does each part take?" → pie (sparingly).

Choosing wrong — say, a pie chart with twenty slices — does not just look bad; it makes the data unreadable and can mislead.

## Executive Dashboard

You already know that a dashboard in a car shows the driver only the most critical information — speed, fuel level, engine temperature — without drowning them in every sensor reading from every bolt. An executive dashboard does the same thing for a business: it compresses hundreds of numbers into a handful of **key performance indicators (KPIs)** that a decision-maker can scan in thirty seconds.

### Structure of a Dashboard

A well-designed executive dashboard is built around three layers:

1. **Metrics** — the raw numbers (total sales, number of users, server uptime).
2. **Aggregations** — summaries that collapse many rows into one value, such as a sum, average, or percentage. If daily sales for a week are $\{s_1, s_2, \dots, s_7\}$, the weekly total is $S = \sum_{i=1}^{7} s_i$ and the daily average is $\bar{s} = S/7$.
3. **Thresholds** — target values that turn a number green or red. If the goal is $\bar{s} \geq \$5{,}000$, a tile instantly signals whether the team is on track.

The dashboard itself does **no new computation** — it just selects, aggregates, and compares. All the work happens in the data pipeline before the visual layer.

### The Signal-to-Noise Rule

Good dashboards minimize **noise** (numbers that don't drive decisions) and maximize **signal** (numbers that do). A useful test: *"If this metric changed by 20 %, would I act differently?"* If yes, keep it. If no, cut it.
