## Bias

**Definition.** Let $\hat{\theta}_n$ be an estimator of a parameter $\theta \in \Theta$ based on $n$ i.i.d. observations $X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim P_\theta$. The *bias* of $\hat{\theta}_n$ is

$$
\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n) = \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}[\hat{\theta}_n] - \theta,
$$

where the expectation is taken under the true data-generating measure $P_\theta$. An estimator with $\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n) = 0$ for all $\theta \in \Theta$ is called *unbiased*; one for which $\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ is called *asymptotically unbiased*. The mean squared error decomposes as

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_n) = \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) + \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n)^2,
$$

a decomposition that immediately frames bias not as an isolated defect but as one term in a fundamental trade-off.

**Theorem (Cramér–Rao lower bound for biased estimators).** Suppose $P_\theta$ satisfies standard regularity conditions (Fisher information $I(\theta)$ finite and positive, interchange of differentiation and integration). Let $b(\theta) = \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n)$. Then

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) \geq \frac{(1 + b'(\theta))^2}{n\, I(\theta)}.
$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\theta}_n,\, \partial_\theta \log p(X;\theta))$, noting that $\mathbb{E}[\partial_\theta \log p] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_n \cdot \partial_\theta \log p] = 1 + b'(\theta)$ by differentiation under the integral sign. $\square$

The bound reveals that a *carefully chosen* bias — one with $|b'(\theta)| < 0$ — can shrink the lower bound on variance, permitting a biased estimator to achieve smaller MSE than any unbiased competitor. The James–Stein estimator (1961) makes this precise: for estimating a mean vector $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^p$ with $p \geq 3$ under squared-error loss, the shrinkage estimator $\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{JS}} = (1 - (p-2)/\|\bar{X}\|^2)\bar{X}$ dominates the MLE uniformly, despite being biased toward the origin.

**Literature context.** Stein's inadmissibility result overturned the century-old presumption that unbiasedness was a design virtue rather than an incidental property. It spawned the Stein phenomenon literature and, more recently, motivated regularization in high-dimensional statistics: ridge regression introduces bias $-(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}\lambda\beta$ to reduce variance when $p/n$ is large (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970). Contemporary open questions include the optimal bias schedule in neural network training (implicit regularization via SGD; Gunasekar et al., 2017) and the formal characterization of *algorithmic bias* — systematic deviation arising from training data distributions rather than estimator design — a setting where the parameter space $\Theta$ itself may be ill-defined.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the bias–variance trade-off empirically on synthetic Gaussian data ($p = 50$, $n = 100$): compare the MLE, ridge, and James–Stein estimators over a grid of signal strengths $\|\mu\|$. Measure MSE by Monte Carlo averaging over $10^4$ trials. Identify the crossover $\|\mu\|^*$ at which shrinkage loses its MSE advantage. Then derive $\|\mu\|^*$ analytically from the Cramér–Rao bound and compare to your simulation.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should state the estimand and loss function precisely, reproduce the Cramér–Rao derivation for the biased case, report Monte Carlo standard errors alongside MSE estimates, and locate the analytic $\|\mu\|^*$ relative to the empirical crossover. Discussion should address whether the qualitative finding extends to non-Gaussian families and what it implies for the default choice of regularization strength in practice.

## Fairness

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote a feature space, $A \in \{0,1\}$ a protected attribute (e.g., group membership), $Y \in \{0,1\}$ a true label, and $\hat{Y} \in \{0,1\}$ a classifier's prediction. Three group-fairness criteria dominate the literature:

- *Demographic parity* (DP): $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1)$.
- *Equalized odds* (EO): $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=y, A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=y, A=1)$ for $y \in \{0,1\}$.
- *Calibration*: $P(Y=1 \mid \hat{p}=s, A=a) = s$ for all score values $s$ and groups $a$.

Individual fairness (Dwork et al., 2012) takes a metric-based view: a randomized classifier $M$ is $(D, d)$-fair if $D(M(x), M(x')) \leq d(x,x')$ for all $x,x' \in \mathcal{X}$, where $D$ is a divergence on distributions over outcomes and $d$ is a task-specific similarity metric on individuals.

**Impossibility Theorem** (Chouldechova, 2017; Kleinberg, Mullainathan, Raghavan, 2016). Suppose base rates differ across groups, i.e., $P(Y=1 \mid A=0) \neq P(Y=1 \mid A=1)$, and the classifier is not perfect ($0 < P(\hat{Y} \neq Y) < 1$). Then demographic parity, equalized odds, and calibration cannot all hold simultaneously.

*Proof sketch.* Calibration ties positive predictive value to the score. With unequal base rates, equalizing true positive rates across groups forces unequal false positive rates (or vice versa), violating equalized odds; simultaneously, achieving equal selection rates (DP) requires distorting the score away from its calibrated value. A one-page algebraic proof follows from writing each criterion as a linear constraint on the $2 \times 2$ confusion matrix per group and observing that the constraint system is overdetermined when base rates differ. See Barocas, Hardt, and Narayanan (2023, §3) for the full derivation.

**Literature context.** The impossibility results are well-established, but the field is far from settled. Hardt, Price, and Srebro (2016) give a post-processing algorithm that enforces equalized odds optimally by solving a linear program over the ROC convex hull — this is one of the few fairness interventions with an exact optimality guarantee. The individual-fairness framework of Dwork et al. (2012) sidesteps group criteria but requires specifying the metric $d$, which is itself a normative and empirical problem: Yurochkin et al. (2020) propose learning $d$ from proxy tasks, but whether learned metrics encode the intended similarity is an open question. A deeper open problem concerns *long-run dynamics*: Liu et al. (2018) show that equalizing opportunity in a myopic model can reduce the long-run representation of the disadvantaged group if feedback between predictions and future feature distributions is ignored. Causal formulations (Kusner et al., 2017; counterfactual fairness) attempt to disentangle direct and indirect discrimination via structural causal models, but identification assumptions are rarely testable from observational data.

**Suggested investigation.** Take the ProPublica COMPAS dataset (Angwin et al., 2016) and a logistic regression classifier trained without the protected attribute. Compute all three group-fairness measures empirically. Then apply Hardt et al.'s post-processing to enforce equalized odds and re-measure DP and calibration, quantifying the trade-off numerically. As an extension, derive the Pareto frontier of accuracy vs. equalized-odds violation as the enforcement threshold varies, and compare against the theoretical bound implied by the base-rate gap. A second thread: implement the individual-fairness constraint using a Mahalanobis distance on non-protected features and assess whether the resulting classifier agrees with the equalized-odds solution on disputed cases.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should: (i) state precisely which fairness criterion is targeted and why, connecting the choice to the deployment context; (ii) derive or cite the impossibility result as motivation for the observed trade-offs; (iii) report confusion matrices and criterion values per group, not just aggregate accuracy; and (iv) discuss what normative assumption underlies the chosen criterion — the technical result alone does not resolve which criterion is appropriate.

### explainable_ai (26.1s)

## Explainable Ai

**Definition.** Let $f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ be a trained model and $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$ a query point. An *explanation* of $f$ at $x_0$ is a function $g : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ drawn from an interpretable class $\mathcal{G}$ (e.g., sparse linear models, decision stumps) together with a locality measure $\pi_{x_0} : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ such that $g$ minimises the fidelity-complexity trade-off

$$\xi(f, g, \pi_{x_0}) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{x_0}}\bigl[\ell(f(z), g(z))\bigr]}_{\text{local fidelity}} + \Omega(g),$$

where $\ell$ is a task loss and $\Omega : \mathcal{G} \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ penalises complexity. This formulation subsumes LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016) and connects to the broader problem of *post-hoc local approximation*.

**Theorem (LIME consistency; informal).** If $f$ is Lipschitz continuous on $\mathcal{X}$ with constant $L$, $\pi_{x_0}$ is an isotropic Gaussian kernel with bandwidth $h$, and $\mathcal{G}$ is the class of $k$-sparse linear models, then as $n \to \infty$ the LIME estimator $\hat{g}_n$ converges in probability to the minimiser of $\xi$ at rate $O(n^{-1/2})$, independently of $\dim(\mathcal{X})$, provided $k$ is fixed.

*Proof sketch.* The empirical risk $\hat{\xi}_n$ is an average of i.i.d. bounded terms (bounded because $\ell$ is bounded on a compact neighbourhood of $x_0$ and $\pi_{x_0}$ has light tails). By Hoeffding's inequality the deviation $|\hat{\xi}_n - \xi|$ is $O(n^{-1/2})$ uniformly over $\mathcal{G}$ (finite VC dimension for $k$-sparse linear models). The Lipschitz assumption on $f$ ensures $\xi$ has a unique minimiser; a standard $M$-estimation argument gives parameter consistency. $\square$

**Literature context.** The fidelity–interpretability decomposition above is contested. Lundberg and Lee (2017) showed that Shapley values — derived from cooperative game theory — are the *unique* attribution satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity axioms; this result is rigorous but computationally exponential in the number of features $p$, motivating the KernelSHAP approximation ($O(p^2)$). A separate thread concerns *global* explanations: concept activation vectors (Kim et al., 2018) probe intermediate representations for human-defined concepts, but their reliability degrades under distribution shift (Ghassemi et al., 2021). Open questions include: (i) whether any local explanation can be faithful when $f$ is discontinuous (e.g., near adversarial examples); (ii) the interplay between explanation stability and data privacy (membership inference can be mounted through repeated explanation queries); (iii) formal guarantees for transformer attention as explanation.

**Suggested investigation.** Choose a tabular dataset (UCI Adult or COMPAS recidivism) and a fixed black-box $f$ (gradient-boosted trees, $\geq 100$ estimators). Implement LIME and KernelSHAP from scratch — avoid wrapper libraries. For each test point $x_0$, compute the $\ell_2$ *stability radius*: the minimum perturbation $\delta$ such that the top-$k$ attributed features change. Plot stability radius against prediction confidence and local Lipschitz estimate. Test whether Shapley axiom violations appear under correlated features by constructing a synthetic dataset with known redundancy structure.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up should: state the approximation class $\mathcal{G}$ and bandwidth $h$ precisely; report both in-distribution fidelity and stability radius with confidence intervals; include an ablation varying $n$ (number of neighbourhood samples) to empirically verify the $O(n^{-1/2})$ convergence; and discuss whether the explanations are faithful to $f$ or only to its local linearisation — a distinction that matters for any downstream decision reliance on the explanation.
