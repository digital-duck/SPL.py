### neuron (0.1s)

## Neuron

**The Telephone in Your Brain**

Imagine your brain is a giant city, and every building needs to talk to every other building. How do they do it? They use a special messenger — called a **neuron**.

A neuron is like a tiny telephone with a really long wire. It has a round body in the middle, lots of little branches reaching out like a tree (called **dendrites**), and one long tail stretching away (called an **axon**).

Here is how it works: when something happens — you touch a hot stove, you hear your name, you see a puppy — a tiny electrical spark fires through one neuron, then jumps to the next, then the next, all the way to your brain. Like a chain of falling dominoes, each one knocking the next.

You have about **86 billion** neurons in your brain. They talk to each other millions of times every second, which is how you think, feel, move, and dream.

**Now you try:** Hold two fingers together lightly, then separate them. That tiny feeling you noticed? That was neurons firing — all the way from your fingertips to your brain and back!

### bias (0.2s)

## Bias

**The lopsided scale**

Imagine you run a lemonade stand and ask only your best friends, "Is my lemonade good?" Every single one says "Yes!" — but your friends *want* to make you happy. Their answers lean in one direction before they even taste a drop. That lean has a name: **bias**.

**Why does this happen?**

A bias is a hidden thumb on the scale. It quietly pushes results away from the true answer. Your friends didn't lie — they just had a reason (being kind to you) that tilted their opinion. Any time something pushes answers consistently in *one* direction, that something is a bias.

**The key idea**

Think of the real answer as the center of a target. Random mistakes scatter arrows all around the center — sometimes left, sometimes right. Bias moves *every* arrow the same way, so they all miss in the same direction.

$$\text{what you measure} = \text{true value} + \text{bias} + \text{random noise}$$

Bias is the part that doesn't cancel out, no matter how many times you measure.

**Now you try**

Survey ten classmates: "Do you like homework?" Then survey ten teachers. Do the two groups lean differently? What might be pushing each group's answers in its own direction?

### weight (0.2s)

## Weight

Imagine holding a feather in one hand and a rock in the other. The rock pulls your arm down much harder than the feather does. That pulling feeling — Earth tugging things toward itself — is what we call **weight**.

Earth acts like a giant magnet for stuff. The more *matter* something has packed inside it (we call that its **mass**), the harder Earth pulls on it, and the heavier it feels.

Here is the key idea: weight is not a thing *inside* the object. It is a relationship — a conversation between the object and Earth. If you took that same rock to the Moon, where the Moon's pull is weaker, the rock would feel lighter, even though nothing inside the rock changed.

We measure weight in **newtons** (written $\text{N}$). A rough rule: on Earth, every kilogram of mass feels about $9.8\,\text{N}$ of pull downward.

$$W = m \times g$$

where $m$ is mass and $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ on Earth.

**Now you try:** Hold a full water bottle, then pour half out. Which pull changed — the bottle's mass, Earth's pull, or both?

### activation_function (0.2s)

## Activation Function

Imagine you are a guard at the gate of a secret clubhouse. Kids knock on the door all day long — some knock softly, some bang loudly. You have one rule: **only let in kids who knock at least five times.** One knock? Stay out. Ten knocks? Welcome in!

Your brain's tiny messengers, called neurons, work the same way. A neuron collects signals from its neighbors — little whispers of numbers being added up. But it does not pass every whisper along. It waits. Only when the total is big enough does it shout the message forward. That "wait, then shout" decision is the **activation function**.

The most popular one is called **ReLU** (say it: *ree-loo*). Its rule is beautifully simple:

$$\text{output} = \max(0,\ x)$$

If the incoming signal $x$ is zero or below, output nothing — total silence. If $x$ is positive, pass it straight through. Like a guard who ignores all quiet knocks but opens the door instantly for loud ones.

Without an activation function, neurons would just add numbers forever — and a pile of additions can never learn to recognise a cat or a word. The activation function is the moment of *decision* that makes learning possible.

**Now you try:** Draw a number line. Mark every number below zero in red and every number above zero in green. That colour map *is* ReLU — you just drew a neural network's decision gate!

### layers (0.2s)

## Layers

**The Birthday Cake Story**

Imagine you're baking a birthday cake. You start with a layer of chocolate sponge, spread strawberry jam on top, add vanilla cream, then another sponge, more cream, and finally colorful frosting. Each layer does something different — but together they make something delicious and complete.

**Why Layers Make Sense**

Layers let us build something complex by stacking simple things. Each layer has one job. It doesn't need to know what the other layers are doing — it just does *its* part.

Think about getting dressed on a cold day: underwear, then a t-shirt, then a sweater, then a coat. Each layer adds something. If you're too warm, you remove one layer. You don't throw away the whole outfit.

**The Big Idea**

A *layer* is a level that sits on top of another level, adding something new without replacing what came before. Layers build on each other, and together they create something neither could do alone.

**Now You Try**

Stack five sheets of paper, each a different color. What does each "layer" add? What would happen if you removed the middle one?

### neural_network (0.2s)

## Neural Network

**The brain that learns by doing**

Imagine you are learning to catch a ball. The first time, you miss completely. But your brain notices — *too far left* — and adjusts. You try again. A little better. After a hundred throws, you catch it without even thinking.

A neural network works the same way. It is a computer program built to *learn from mistakes*, just like you.

Inside, it has layers of tiny switches called **neurons** (named after brain cells). Information flows in through the first layer, passes through middle layers that transform it, and comes out the other side as an answer — like "that's a cat" or "that's a dog."

At first, the network guesses randomly. It checks if it was right, then quietly adjusts its switches to do better next time. Do this millions of times, and the network gets surprisingly good.

No one programs it to recognise cats. It *figures it out* from examples — the same way you learned to recognise your own face in a mirror.

**Now you try:** Show a friend ten photos of apples and ten of oranges. Can they guess the next one? That's neural-network learning — in human form!

### perceptron (0.1s)

## Perceptron

**The World's Simplest Decision-Maker**

Imagine you're deciding whether to bring an umbrella to school. You peek outside and notice two things: *Is the sky grey?* and *Did it rain yesterday?* If both answers are "yes," you grab the umbrella. If neither is "yes," you leave it behind.

Your brain just did what a **perceptron** does — it looked at a few clues, weighed them up, and made a yes-or-no decision.

A perceptron is a tiny machine that mimics this. It takes some inputs — each one carrying a number — and gives each input a **weight**, like saying "grey sky matters more than yesterday's rain." Then it adds everything up. If the total is big enough, it shouts **"Yes!"** If not, it stays quiet.

We can write this simply. If your inputs are $x_1$ and $x_2$ with weights $w_1$ and $w_2$, the perceptron checks:

$$w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 \geq \text{threshold}$$

That's it — one addition, one comparison.

**Now you try:** You're deciding if a fruit is an apple. Your clues are *red colour* (weight 2) and *round shape* (weight 1). The threshold is 2. A red, round fruit scores $2+1=3$. Apple? **Yes!**

### depth (0.1s)

## Depth

Imagine you drop a coin into a swimming pool. You watch it spin and sink — down, down, down — until it rests on the bottom. How far did it fall? That distance from the surface to the bottom is called **depth**.

Now think about a family tree. Your mom is one step above you. Your grandma is one step above your mom. If someone asked "how deep is Grandma in this family?", you'd count: *two steps down from the top.* Depth is just counting how many steps you are from where things began.

This idea shows up everywhere: how many floors underground a subway goes, how many questions deep you have to ask before you reach the simplest answer, how many layers an onion has.

The key insight is this — **depth tells you how far from the start you are**, measured in steps, not in centimetres or miles.

We write the depth of something as a number. The very top — the surface, the root, the beginning — always has depth **0**. One step below is depth **1**, two steps is depth **2**, and so on.

**Now you try:** Draw a simple tree with a trunk and three branches. What is the depth of the trunk? What is the depth of each branch tip?

### multilayer_perceptron (0.2s)

## Multilayer Perceptron

Imagine you are sorting Halloween candy. First you check: *is it chocolate?* Then you check: *is it wrapped?* Then: *is it your favourite brand?* Each check uses the answer from the one before. That chain of questions — layer by layer — is exactly how a **multilayer perceptron** thinks.

A single question on its own misses a lot. But stack several rows of tiny yes/no checkers, and suddenly the machine can recognise handwriting, tell a cat from a dog, or translate your words.

Each row is called a **layer**. The first layer looks at raw clues (pixels, letters). The middle layers — called **hidden layers** — find bigger patterns from those clues. The last layer gives the final answer.

Formally, every checker computes a weighted sum of its inputs, then squashes the result through a small curve called an **activation function** $f$:

$$\text{output} = f\!\left(\sum_i w_i \cdot x_i + b\right)$$

Stack enough of these layers and the network can learn almost anything from examples.

**Now you try:** Draw three boxes in a row. Label them *eyes*, *brain*, *mouth*. What does each box do when you recognise a friend's face?

### artificial_intelligence (0.2s)

## Artificial Intelligence

Have you ever taught a younger sibling how to sort Lego bricks? At first you showed them: *"Red bricks go here, blue bricks go there."* After seeing enough examples, they stopped asking you — they just *knew*. That moment when they figured it out on their own? That is exactly what we are trying to get computers to do.

A computer is very fast at following rules, but it cannot invent rules by itself — not yet. **Artificial intelligence** (AI) is the idea of teaching a machine to learn from examples, just like your sibling learned from Lego bricks, until it can make smart guesses on its own.

Think of it like a guessing game. The computer sees thousands of pictures of cats, thousands of pictures of dogs, and keeps score of when it guesses right. Each wrong guess teaches it something new. Slowly, its guesses get better and better.

At its core, AI is simply *practice plus feedback* — the same recipe you use when learning to ride a bike, only happening inside a computer millions of times per second.

**Now you try:** Name one thing *you* learned by making mistakes and getting better over time. Could a computer learn that same thing by watching enough examples?

### deep_learning (0.2s)

## Deep Learning

Your brain gets better at catching a ball the more you practice. The first time, you miss. The tenth time, you're pretty good. The hundredth time, your hands just *know* where to go — without thinking.

Deep learning works the same way, but inside a computer. Imagine a chain of friends passing a note. The first friend looks at a picture of a cat and writes what they notice — pointy ears, whiskers. The second friend reads that and adds more details. The third friend decides: "Yes, that's a cat!" At first they're terrible at it. But show them a million cat pictures, and each time they're wrong, they fix their notes a little. After enough practice, the chain of friends becomes *very* good at recognizing cats.

Each "friend" in the chain is called a **layer**. Having many layers stacked together is what makes it *deep*. More layers means the computer can learn more subtle patterns — like telling a tabby from a tiger.

**Now you try:** Think of something you got better at by practicing. What do you think your brain was "adjusting" each time you made a mistake?

### generative_art (9.7s)

## Generative Art

**The robot that draws**

Imagine you gave a robot a box of colored pencils and one simple rule: "Draw a spiral, but every time you finish a loop, pick a random color." You press *go* — and the robot draws something you've never seen before, something *you* couldn't have planned yourself. That's generative art!

**Why does it feel like magic?**

Because you wrote the *rules*, but not the *picture*. The rules are like a recipe, and the computer follows them thousands of times, making tiny choices along the way. The result surprises even the person who wrote the recipe.

Artists have always used tools — brushes, chisels, cameras. A computer is just a new kind of tool. The artist's job is to design the rules; the computer's job is to run them and show you what they make.

**The big idea**

Rules + Randomness = Endless surprises.

You control the *how*; the computer discovers the *what*.

**Now you try**

Grab a pencil and paper. Write three rules for a drawing — like "always turn left," "make a new dot every centimeter," "flip a coin to go up or down." Follow your own rules and see what shape appears!
