### bivariate_data (26.6s)

## Bivariate Data

**Definition.** A bivariate dataset is a collection of $n$ ordered pairs $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ drawn from a joint probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$, where $X, Y : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ are random variables with joint distribution $F_{X,Y}(x,y) = \mathbb{P}(X \leq x, Y \leq y)$. The central object of study is the dependence structure between $X$ and $Y$: how information about one variable constrains inference about the other, and in what senses that constraint is linear, monotone, or entirely general.

**Theorem (Decomposition of Joint Entropy).** For discrete $(X, Y)$ with joint pmf $p(x,y)$,
$$H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y),$$
where $H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)$ and $H(Y \mid X) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log p(y \mid x)$. Independence holds if and only if $H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y)$, equivalently $I(X;Y) = 0$, where $I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \geq 0$ is the mutual information. The non-negativity follows from the Gibbs inequality (Klein's inequality for the KL divergence) applied to $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x,y) \| p(x)p(y)) \geq 0$.

**Literature context.** The classical treatment of bivariate data — sample covariance, Pearson's $r$, ordinary least squares — rests on the assumption that $F_{X,Y}$ is bivariate Gaussian, under which linear correlation fully characterizes dependence (Anderson, 1958). Departures from normality expose the inadequacy of $r$: Anscombe's quartet (1973) demonstrates four datasets sharing identical $(n, \bar{x}, \bar{y}, s_x^2, s_y^2, r)$ yet exhibiting radically different geometric structure, making the quartet a canonical argument for exploratory visualization before inference. Beyond Pearson's $r$, rank-based measures — Spearman's $\rho_s$ and Kendall's $\tau$ — capture monotone dependence under weaker distributional assumptions, while copula theory (Sklar, 1959; Nelsen, 2006) separates marginal behavior from dependence structure entirely: $F_{X,Y}(x,y) = C(F_X(x), F_Y(y))$ for a unique copula $C$ when the marginals are continuous. Open problems include minimax-optimal estimation of $C$ under sparsity or smoothness constraints and efficient nonparametric testing of independence in high-dimensional bivariate summaries.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate Anscombe's quartet computationally, then extend it: generate five synthetic bivariate datasets with identical first- and second-order moments but distinct copulas (Gaussian, Clayton, Gumbel, Frank, and empirical). For each, compute $r$, $\rho_s$, $\tau$, and a kernel-based mutual information estimator (Kraskov et al., 2004). Fit a linear model $\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x$ by OLS, compute residuals, and apply a Breusch–Pagan heteroskedasticity test. Quantify how badly the OLS confidence intervals for $\beta_1$ are miscalibrated when the Gaussian copula assumption fails. Finally, apply a nonparametric independence test (e.g., distance correlation, Székely & Rizzo, 2009) and compare power across the five settings.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up of this investigation should: (1) state the moment-matching construction explicitly (showing the five datasets share $\bar{x}, \bar{y}, s_x, s_y, r$ to three significant figures); (2) tabulate all dependence measures with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals; (3) include scatter plots and contour plots of the estimated bivariate density for each dataset; (4) derive analytically — or cite — the breakdown condition under which OLS standard errors are inconsistent; and (5) discuss what additional structure (copula family, tail index) would need to be assumed or estimated to restore valid inference.

## Scatterplot Color Encoding

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i, z_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a dataset in which $(x_i, y_i) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ are spatial coordinates and $z_i \in \mathcal{Z}$ is an additional attribute. A *color encoding* is a mapping $\phi : \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{C}$, where $\mathcal{C}$ is a perceptual color space (typically CIELAB or a submanifold thereof), such that the visual representation of the scatterplot preserves semantically relevant structure of $z$. When $\mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is ordinal, $\phi$ is a *sequential* or *diverging* colormap; when $\mathcal{Z}$ is a finite categorical set, $\phi$ is a *qualitative* palette. The central design problem is selecting $\phi$ so that human perception of $\mathcal{C}$ is a faithful proxy for the statistical structure of $\mathcal{Z}$.

**Theorem (Perceptual uniformity and discrimination).** Let $\Delta E_{ij} = \|\phi(z_i) - \phi(z_j)\|_{\mathrm{CIELAB}}$ denote the perceptual distance between colors assigned to values $z_i$ and $z_j$. A colormap $\phi$ is *perceptually uniform* if there exists a constant $k > 0$ such that
$$\Delta E_{ij} = k \cdot |z_i - z_j| \quad \forall\, z_i, z_j \in \mathcal{Z}.$$
For qualitative encodings, the optimal palette maximizes the minimum pairwise distance: $\phi^* = \arg\max_{\phi} \min_{i \neq j} \Delta E_{ij}$, subject to $|\mathcal{C}| = |\mathcal{Z}|$ and legibility constraints (luminance bounds, color-deficiency accommodation).

*Remark.* Perceptual uniformity is a necessary but not sufficient condition for effective encoding. The human visual system exhibits non-isotropic discrimination thresholds in CIELAB — the just-noticeable difference (JND) varies with hue angle and luminance — so strict uniformity in CIELAB coordinates does not guarantee uniform discriminability across the visible gamut. This remains an active calibration problem in applied perception research (Szafir, 2018; Reda & Szafir, 2021).

**Literature context.** The foundational framework for visual variable theory originates with Bertin (1967), who classified color (hue, value, saturation) as a selective but non-ordered visual channel. Cleveland & McGill (1984) established the hierarchy of perceptual tasks, situating color below position and length in accuracy. Subsequent empirical work (Brewer, 1994; Harrower & Brewer, 2003) produced the ColorBrewer system — still widely used — though its design criteria predate modern crowdsourced perceptual experiments. More recent work has revisited these palettes through large-scale participant studies (Liu & Heer, 2018) and deep-learning-based colormap optimization (Gramazio et al., 2017). Open questions include: (1) how context effects (surrounding hues, plot density) modulate effective discrimination; (2) whether perceptual uniformity metrics generalize across display technologies and ambient lighting; and (3) how to encode *uncertainty* jointly with value using dual-channel hue–saturation mappings.

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable graduate project is a replication-and-extension of Liu & Heer (2018). Using a custom scatterplot stimulus set in which $(x_i, y_i)$ are sampled from a bivariate Gaussian and $z_i$ from a uniform grid on $[0,1]$, recruit participants (or use Amazon Mechanical Turk) to perform value-discrimination tasks across five colormaps: viridis, plasma, cividis, a custom perceptually-uniform palette, and a non-uniform legacy palette (e.g., rainbow/jet). Measure mean absolute error in reported $z$-value as a function of $\Delta z$. Fit a psychometric function $P(\text{correct}) = \sigma(\alpha \cdot \Delta E / \sigma_{\mathrm{noise}})$ to extract effective JND per colormap. Test the hypothesis that $\Delta E_{\mathrm{CIELAB}}$ predicts discrimination accuracy better than $\Delta E_{\mathrm{RGB}}$.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should: (1) state the colormap selection criteria formally using the perceptual-uniformity definition above; (2) report psychometric curves with 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals; (3) discuss whether the CIELAB uniformity assumption held empirically or required a correction term; (4) situate the findings against at least one existing empirical benchmark (Liu & Heer, or Szafir); and (5) propose one concrete revision to colormap design guidelines that the data would support or refute.

## Three D Visualization

**Definition.** Three-dimensional visualization is the discipline of constructing faithful, perceptually interpretable representations of geometric objects, scalar/vector fields, or abstract data embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (or a curved three-manifold) onto a two-dimensional display surface. Formally, a visualization pipeline is a composition of maps:

$$V = R \circ P \circ T : \mathcal{X} \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^2,$$

where $\mathcal{X}$ is the data domain (a subset of $\mathbb{R}^3$ or a discrete mesh), $T$ is a rigid or affine world-to-camera transformation, $P$ is a projection (orthographic or perspective), and $R$ is a rasterization or ray-casting operator that resolves occlusion and lighting. The mathematical content of each stage is non-trivial, and the composition $V$ is neither injective (occlusion destroys information) nor continuous at silhouette edges, which is why visualization remains an active research area rather than an engineering checklist.

**Projection geometry.** The perspective projection from camera coordinates $(x, y, z)$ to image coordinates is the rational map:

$$\pi(x, y, z) = \left(\frac{f x}{z},\, \frac{f y}{z}\right),$$

with focal length $f > 0$ and the convention $z > 0$. In homogeneous coordinates this is a linear map represented by the $3\times 4$ camera matrix $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{K}[\mathbf{R}\mid\mathbf{t}]$, where $\mathbf{K}$ is the intrinsic matrix, $\mathbf{R} \in SO(3)$, and $\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Orthographic projection corresponds to $f \to \infty$ with rescaled coordinates and eliminates foreshortening; the choice between them affects depth perception in a measurable, user-study-quantifiable way (Ware, 2004).

**Theorem (Radon, 1917; Natterer, 2001 — X-ray transform).** Let $f \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^3)$. The X-ray transform $\mathcal{X}f(\ell) = \int_\ell f\, ds$ over lines $\ell \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ uniquely determines $f$, and the inversion formula is:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{1}{8\pi^2} \int_{S^2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial s^2}\left[\int_{\xi \cdot \mathbf{y} = s} f(\mathbf{y})\, d\mathbf{y}\right]_{s = \xi \cdot \mathbf{x}} d\xi.$$

This theorem underpins both volume rendering (where $f$ is an opacity or emission density) and computed tomography reconstruction. The practical consequence for visualization is that a single 2D projection discards the remaining two degrees of freedom; interactive rotation (varying $\mathbf{R}$) provides the observer with an equivalent of integrating over $S^2$, recovering full structural understanding in a perceptual sense — though the information-theoretic equivalence is not strict and depends on the observer's prior.

**Literature context.** Classical references (Foley et al., *Computer Graphics: Principles and Practice*; Shirley & Morley, *Realistic Ray Tracing*) establish the rendering pipeline, while Ware's *Information Visualization* (2004, 2020) connects the geometry to cognitive science. Current open questions include: (1) optimal view selection — given $f$, which rotation $\mathbf{R}^*$ maximizes feature salience under a perceptual metric? (2) interactive visualization of implicitly defined surfaces (signed-distance functions, neural radiance fields); (3) uncertainty visualization — rendering a probability distribution over $\mathbb{R}^3$ rather than a point estimate. The NeRF literature (Mildenhall et al., 2020) re-opened the view-synthesis problem by parameterizing $f$ with a neural network, blurring the line between visualization and reconstruction.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a minimal ray-marcher for a signed-distance field $\phi: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ (e.g., a gyroid surface defined by $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \sin x_1 \cos x_2 + \sin x_2 \cos x_3 + \sin x_3 \cos x_1$). Vary the camera orbit uniformly over $SO(3)$ and compute, for each view, the projected silhouette length and the fraction of surface area visible. Fit the visibility fraction $\eta(\mathbf{R})$ as a function of orbit angles; determine empirically whether a single "best view" exists or whether the distribution is multimodal. Compare orthographic versus perspective modes at several focal lengths and report the effect on perceived curvature of the saddle regions.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should clearly state the SDF definition and the ray-marching step-size criterion used for convergence; derive or cite the sphere-tracing bound $\Delta t \leq \phi(\mathbf{x}(t)) / \|\nabla\phi\|_\infty$; present visibility-fraction curves with error bars over multiple orbit discretizations; and discuss what the multimodality (or lack thereof) implies about interactive versus static presentation of the surface. The write-up should situate the result relative to the view-entropy literature (Vázquez et al., 2001) and identify one extension — such as applying the same analysis to a data-driven implicit surface learned from a point cloud.
