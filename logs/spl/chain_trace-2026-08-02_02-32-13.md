## Data

You already know that data means "information we collect." But once you start working with it seriously, you realize data has *structure* — and understanding that structure is what lets you actually do something with it.

Think of data as a table. Each **row** is one observation (one student, one experiment, one moment in time). Each **column** is a **variable** — a measurable attribute of that observation. If your class recorded everyone's height, eye color, and test score, you'd have 3 variables and as many rows as students.

Variables come in two fundamental types:

- **Quantitative** — numbers you can do arithmetic with. Height in centimeters, temperature in °C, test scores. These can be *discrete* (counted: $0, 1, 2, \ldots$) or *continuous* (measured: any value in a range).
- **Categorical** — labels or groups. Eye color, favorite subject, city name. Arithmetic on these makes no sense; you count how often each category appears instead.

A single dataset with $n$ observations and $p$ variables is often written as a matrix $\mathbf{X}$ with shape $n \times p$:

$$\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{1,1} & x_{1,2} & \cdots & x_{1,p} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ x_{n,1} & x_{n,2} & \cdots & x_{n,p} \end{pmatrix}$$

where $x_{i,j}$ is the value for observation $i$, variable $j$.

## Data Collection

You already have the right instinct: before you can learn anything from data, you need data. But *how* you collect it turns out to matter just as much as what you do with it afterward.

**The structure underneath the intuition**

Every data collection effort answers three questions: *What* are you measuring? *Who* or *what* are you measuring it from? *How* are you selecting your subjects?

The things you measure are called **variables**. If you're tracking students' study hours and test scores, both are variables — one you control (or observe), one you care about predicting. We usually write these as $x$ for an input variable and $y$ for an output (or outcome) variable.

The group you *want* to learn about is the **population**. Because you can't always measure everyone, you collect a **sample** — a smaller subset. The key rule:

$$\text{sample must represent the population}$$

A biased sample gives you a confident-sounding but wrong answer. If you survey only students who stayed after school, you've missed everyone who didn't — your $x$ and $y$ data no longer reflect the full population.

**One simple pattern to remember**

Random selection is the safest way to avoid bias. When every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen, the sample tends to mirror the whole group, even if it's small.

**Practice problem**

A researcher wants to know the average sleep time of students at a 1,000-person school. She surveys 40 students from the school's swim team.

1. What is the population? What is the sample?
2. Identify one reason this sample might be biased.
3. Suggest a more representative way to choose 40 students.

## Internet of Things

**The connected world around you.** You already know that your phone can talk to the internet — but what if your thermostat, doorbell, sneakers, and refrigerator could too? That is exactly what the Internet of Things (IoT) describes: everyday physical objects embedded with sensors and small computers that collect data and communicate over a network, usually without a human pressing a button.

**How it actually works.** Each IoT device follows a three-step loop:

1. **Sense** — a sensor measures something physical: temperature, motion, light, heartbeat, soil moisture.
2. **Process** — a tiny microcontroller (think of a very small computer) decides what to do with the reading.
3. **Transmit** — the device sends data to a hub, cloud server, or directly to another device using a wireless protocol such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or Zigbee.

A smart thermostat, for example, senses the room temperature $T$, compares it to the target $T_{\text{set}}$, and transmits a signal to turn heating on whenever $T < T_{\text{set}}$.

**The simple pattern.** IoT systems are built on the idea of continuous feedback: sensor reads → decision made → action taken → sensor reads again. This loop runs automatically, making buildings smarter, farms more efficient, and health monitoring more continuous.

**A useful number to know.** If one sensor samples temperature every 10 seconds, it produces $\frac{60}{10} = 6$ readings per minute, or $6 \times 60 \times 24 = 8{,}640$ readings per day.
