## Bias

You already have the right instinct: bias means something is *consistently* off in one direction. A bathroom scale that always reads two pounds too heavy isn't just inaccurate on a bad day — it is systematically wrong every single time you step on it. That persistence is what makes it bias, not just noise.

In statistics and machine learning, we make this precise. Suppose you are trying to estimate some true quantity — call it $\theta$ (a population mean, a model parameter, anything). You run an experiment and produce an estimate $\hat{\theta}$. The **bias** of that estimate is defined as

$$\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$$

where $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]$ is the *expected value* of your estimate — roughly, what you'd get on average if you repeated the experiment many times. If that average lands exactly on the truth, bias is zero and we call the estimator **unbiased**. If it consistently undershoots or overshoots, bias is nonzero.

A classic example: the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ is an unbiased estimator of the true population mean $\mu$, because $\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}] = \mu$. However, if you tried to estimate the population variance by dividing by $n$ instead of $n-1$, you would get a biased result — your estimate would be systematically too small.

The key insight is that bias is about the *average* error, not any single measurement. Random errors can cancel out; biased errors pile up.

## Fairness

You already sense when something feels unfair — a coin that lands heads nine times in a row, or a game where one player always wins. But intuition alone won't let you *prove* unfairness. Math gives you the tools to do that precisely.

### What Fairness Really Means

A random process is called **fair** (or *unbiased*) when every outcome has an equal chance of occurring. For a six-sided die, each face has probability $\frac{1}{6}$. No face is favored — that's fairness in its simplest form.

A **fair game** goes one step further: not just equal chances, but equal *expected payoffs*. The **expected value** $E$ of a game is the average outcome you'd get if you played infinitely many times:

$$E = \sum_i p_i \cdot v_i$$

where $p_i$ is the probability of outcome $i$ and $v_i$ is what you win (or lose) in that outcome. A game is fair when $E = 0$ — neither player gains an advantage over the long run.

### The Pattern to Remember

If a game costs $c$ dollars to play and pays out $w$ dollars on a win with probability $p$, then:

$$E = p \cdot w + (1 - p) \cdot (-c)$$

Set $E = 0$ to find what a fair payout looks like: $w = \frac{c(1-p)}{p}$.

## Explainable Ai

You already know that AI models can make surprisingly good predictions — a model might correctly flag a suspicious bank transaction or recommend the perfect playlist. But here's a deeper question: *why* did it make that call? If the model can't tell you, that's a problem — especially in high-stakes situations like medical diagnoses or loan approvals.

**Explainable AI (XAI)** is the field that studies how to make model decisions transparent and interpretable.

One of the most widely used tools is **feature importance**. Suppose a model predicts whether a student will pass an exam using three inputs: hours studied ($x_1$), sleep hours ($x_2$), and attendance rate ($x_3$). After training, we can ask: how much did each feature *contribute* to the prediction? A technique called **SHAP** (SHapley Additive exPlanations) assigns each feature a score $\phi_i$ so that:

$$\hat{y} = \phi_0 + \phi_1 + \phi_2 + \phi_3$$

Here $\phi_0$ is the baseline (average prediction), and each $\phi_i$ shows how much feature $x_i$ pushed the prediction up or down. A large positive $\phi_1$ means studying a lot helped; a large negative $\phi_2$ means poor sleep hurt.

The key insight: **a model is only trustworthy if you can audit its reasoning.** A black-box model that happens to be right isn't necessarily safe — it might be right for the wrong reasons.
