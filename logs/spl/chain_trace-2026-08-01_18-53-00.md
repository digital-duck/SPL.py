### data (13.8s)

## Data

You already know that data means "information we collect." But once you start working with it seriously, you realize data has *structure* — and understanding that structure is what lets you actually do something with it.

Think of data as a table. Each **row** is one observation (one student, one experiment, one moment in time). Each **column** is a **variable** — a measurable attribute of that observation. If your class recorded everyone's height, eye color, and test score, you'd have 3 variables and as many rows as students.

Variables come in two fundamental types:

- **Quantitative** — numbers you can do arithmetic with. Height in centimeters, temperature in °C, test scores. These can be *discrete* (counted: $0, 1, 2, \ldots$) or *continuous* (measured: any value in a range).
- **Categorical** — labels or groups. Eye color, favorite subject, city name. Arithmetic on these makes no sense; you count how often each category appears instead.

A single dataset with $n$ observations and $p$ variables is often written as a matrix $\mathbf{X}$ with shape $n \times p$:

$$\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{1,1} & x_{1,2} & \cdots & x_{1,p} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ x_{n,1} & x_{n,2} & \cdots & x_{n,p} \end{pmatrix}$$

where $x_{i,j}$ is the value for observation $i$, variable $j$.

### data_collection (13.2s)

## Data Collection

You already have the right instinct: before you can learn anything from data, you need data. But *how* you collect it turns out to matter just as much as what you do with it afterward.

**The structure underneath the intuition**

Every data collection effort answers three questions: *What* are you measuring? *Who* or *what* are you measuring it from? *How* are you selecting your subjects?

The things you measure are called **variables**. If you're tracking students' study hours and test scores, both are variables — one you control (or observe), one you care about predicting. We usually write these as $x$ for an input variable and $y$ for an output (or outcome) variable.

The group you *want* to learn about is the **population**. Because you can't always measure everyone, you collect a **sample** — a smaller subset. The key rule:

$$\text{sample must represent the population}$$

A biased sample gives you a confident-sounding but wrong answer. If you survey only students who stayed after school, you've missed everyone who didn't — your $x$ and $y$ data no longer reflect the full population.

**One simple pattern to remember**

Random selection is the safest way to avoid bias. When every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen, the sample tends to mirror the whole group, even if it's small.

### data_preparation (12.8s)

## Data Preparation

You've probably heard the phrase "garbage in, garbage out." Before any algorithm can learn from data, the data itself has to be clean, organized, and in the right shape. This step — data preparation — is where most of the real work in machine learning actually lives.

**The structure underneath the cleanup**

Raw data collected from the world is almost always messy. It might have missing values (a survey respondent skipped a question), inconsistent formats ("New York," "new york," and "NY" all mean the same thing), or wildly different scales (one column holds ages in the range 0–100, another holds income in the range 0–200,000).

That scale problem matters more than it looks. Many learning algorithms compute distances or weighted sums. If income is 2,000 times larger than age by raw units, it will dominate every calculation — not because it's *more important*, but just because its numbers are bigger.

**The key rule: normalization and encoding**

Two transformations come up constantly:

- **Min-max normalization** rescales every value to the interval $[0, 1]$:
$$x' = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}$$
- **One-hot encoding** converts a category like `{red, green, blue}` into three binary columns: $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$, $(0,0,1)$. Numbers don't imply order; binary flags don't.

Together these two rules put every feature on equal footing before learning begins.

### data_analysis (17.7s)

## Data Analysis

You already know that data is just a collection of observations — temperatures recorded each day, test scores in a class, or the number of steps you walk. Data analysis is what happens next: turning that raw list of numbers into something you can *understand* and *use*.

The first move is almost always to summarize. Given a dataset $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$, two summaries immediately tell you a lot:

- **Mean** (the balance point): $\bar{x} = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$
- **Range** (the spread): $R = x_{\max} - x_{\min}$

These two numbers together already reveal whether values are tightly packed around a typical value or wildly scattered. A class where everyone scores between 78 and 82 is very different from one where scores run from 45 to 99 — even if the means are identical.

The next layer is **distribution**: how often does each value (or range of values) appear? A histogram groups data into bins and lets you see the shape — is it symmetric, skewed to one side, or does it have two humps? Shape matters because it tells you whether the mean is a fair representative or misleading.

A simple pattern to remember: **mean describes center; spread and shape describe context**. Never report one without at least hinting at the other.

### predictive_analytics (13.9s)

## Predictive Analytics

You already use prediction every day — checking the weather before a trip, guessing how long homework will take, or sensing a friend's mood. Predictive analytics is the formal version of that instinct: using patterns from past data to make informed guesses about the future.

**The core structure.** Every predictive model looks for a relationship between an *input* (called a **feature**, $x$) and an *output* (called a **target**, $y$). The simplest form is linear regression, which fits a straight line through your data:

$$\hat{y} = mx + b$$

Here $\hat{y}$ (read "y-hat") is the *predicted* value, $m$ is the slope (how strongly $x$ influences $y$), and $b$ is the intercept. Once you've estimated $m$ and $b$ from historical data, you can plug in a new $x$ you've never seen before and get a reasonable $\hat{y}$.

**The key idea.** A model is never perfectly right — there's always a gap between the prediction and reality called the **residual**, $e = y - \hat{y}$. Good predictive analytics minimizes those residuals without *memorizing* the training data (that's called overfitting). The goal is a model that generalizes: it works on new examples, not just the ones it learned from.

**Simple pattern to remember.** More data $+$ the right features $=$ better predictions. Raw data alone tells you nothing; structure (a model) is what turns it into foresight.
