### neuron (0.1s)

## Neuron

**Definition.** A *neuron* (or *unit*) in a neural network is a parameterized scalar function $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ of the form

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b\right), \quad \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n,\ b \in \mathbb{R},$$

where $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a fixed nonlinearity (activation function) and the pair $(\mathbf{w}, b)$ constitutes the learnable parameters. The one additional primitive introduced here is the *layer*: a vector-valued composition of $m$ neurons, $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$, with $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$. Depth, expressivity hierarchies, and the tradeoffs that arise from stacking layers are deferred to subsequent sections.

**Theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991).** *Let $\sigma$ be any continuous sigmoidal function. Then finite sums of the form $\sum_{k=1}^N c_k \sigma(\mathbf{w}_k^\top \mathbf{x} + b_k)$ are dense in $C(K)$ for any compact $K \subset \mathbb{R}^n$.*

The proof proceeds by a Hahn–Banach separation argument: suppose some bounded linear functional on $C(K)$ annihilates all such sums. By the Riesz representation theorem, this functional corresponds to a signed measure $\mu$ on $K$. One then shows that sigmoidal activations are *discriminatory* — a signed measure that annihilates $\sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b)$ for every $(\mathbf{w}, b)$ must be the zero measure — so the span is dense in $C(K)$. The result extends to ReLU activations ($\sigma(t) = \max(0,t)$) via different arguments (Leshno et al. 1993), which require the activation to be non-polynomial almost everywhere rather than sigmoidal.

**What is known and what remains open.** Universal approximation establishes *existence* of parameters achieving arbitrary accuracy for a single hidden layer of sufficient width, but says nothing about *learnability* — whether gradient-based optimization finds those parameters — or about the rate at which the approximation error decreases with $m$. For Sobolev and Hölder function classes, quantitative rates are available; see the survey by DeVore et al. (2021) for a systematic treatment. A subtler open question concerns the role of the individual neuron as an atomic unit: in the overparameterized regime, neurons collectively behave as a continuous field, and it is unclear how much of a network's representational capacity is attributable to individual units versus emergent interaction structure. The question of which activation functions $\sigma$ maximize approximation efficiency for a given parameter budget remains incompletely answered.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the approximation rate for a single hidden layer of $m$ ReLU neurons applied to $f(x) = \sin(2\pi k x)$ on $[0,1]$, varying $m \in \{4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}$ and frequency $k \in \{2, 4, 8\}$. For each configuration, train to convergence with gradient descent and record the $L^2$ approximation error. The discriminatory-function argument predicts that for any fixed $k$, error should decrease with $m$; the interesting empirical question is the *rate*: does it match the theoretical $O(m^{-2/n})$ rate expected for Sobolev functions, and how does this rate degrade as $k$ increases? A natural secondary measurement is the distribution of learned weight norms $\|\mathbf{w}_k\|$, which reveals whether neurons specialize to different frequency components or collapse to near-identical copies.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should specify the function class being approximated and the norm used to measure error, give precise network architectures and optimization details (optimizer, learning rate schedule, initialization distribution), and report error as a function of $m$ for each value of $k$ in a controlled table. If the empirical rate differs from the theoretical prediction, the write-up should diagnose whether the gap is attributable to optimization failure (the loss did not converge) or a genuine approximation-theoretic effect. Both outcomes are scientifically informative and should be reported with equal care.

### bias (0.2s)

## Bias

**Definition.** Let $\hat{\theta}_n$ be an estimator of a parameter $\theta \in \Theta$ based on $n$ i.i.d. observations $X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim P_\theta$. The *bias* of $\hat{\theta}_n$ is

$$
\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n) = \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}[\hat{\theta}_n] - \theta,
$$

where the expectation is taken under the true data-generating measure $P_\theta$. An estimator with $\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n) = 0$ for all $\theta \in \Theta$ is called *unbiased*; one for which $\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ is called *asymptotically unbiased*. The mean squared error decomposes as

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_n) = \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) + \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n)^2,
$$

a decomposition that immediately frames bias not as an isolated defect but as one term in a fundamental trade-off.

**Theorem (Cramér–Rao lower bound for biased estimators).** Suppose $P_\theta$ satisfies standard regularity conditions (Fisher information $I(\theta)$ finite and positive, interchange of differentiation and integration). Let $b(\theta) = \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n)$. Then

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) \geq \frac{(1 + b'(\theta))^2}{n\, I(\theta)}.
$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\theta}_n,\, \partial_\theta \log p(X;\theta))$, noting that $\mathbb{E}[\partial_\theta \log p] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_n \cdot \partial_\theta \log p] = 1 + b'(\theta)$ by differentiation under the integral sign. $\square$

The bound reveals that a *carefully chosen* bias — one with $|b'(\theta)| < 0$ — can shrink the lower bound on variance, permitting a biased estimator to achieve smaller MSE than any unbiased competitor. The James–Stein estimator (1961) makes this precise: for estimating a mean vector $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^p$ with $p \geq 3$ under squared-error loss, the shrinkage estimator $\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{JS}} = (1 - (p-2)/\|\bar{X}\|^2)\bar{X}$ dominates the MLE uniformly, despite being biased toward the origin.

**Literature context.** Stein's inadmissibility result overturned the century-old presumption that unbiasedness was a design virtue rather than an incidental property. It spawned the Stein phenomenon literature and, more recently, motivated regularization in high-dimensional statistics: ridge regression introduces bias $-(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}\lambda\beta$ to reduce variance when $p/n$ is large (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970). Contemporary open questions include the optimal bias schedule in neural network training (implicit regularization via SGD; Gunasekar et al., 2017) and the formal characterization of *algorithmic bias* — systematic deviation arising from training data distributions rather than estimator design — a setting where the parameter space $\Theta$ itself may be ill-defined.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the bias–variance trade-off empirically on synthetic Gaussian data ($p = 50$, $n = 100$): compare the MLE, ridge, and James–Stein estimators over a grid of signal strengths $\|\mu\|$. Measure MSE by Monte Carlo averaging over $10^4$ trials. Identify the crossover $\|\mu\|^*$ at which shrinkage loses its MSE advantage. Then derive $\|\mu\|^*$ analytically from the Cramér–Rao bound and compare to your simulation.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should state the estimand and loss function precisely, reproduce the Cramér–Rao derivation for the biased case, report Monte Carlo standard errors alongside MSE estimates, and locate the analytic $\|\mu\|^*$ relative to the empirical crossover. Discussion should address whether the qualitative finding extends to non-Gaussian families and what it implies for the default choice of regularization strength in practice.

### weight (0.2s)

## Weight

**Definition.** Let $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \ldots, w_n)^\top$ with each $w_i > 0$. The *weight matrix* $W = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w})$ induces a **weighted inner product** on $\mathbb{R}^n$:

$$\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle_W := \mathbf{u}^\top W \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \, u_i v_i,$$

with associated *weighted norm* $\|\mathbf{u}\|_W = \sqrt{\mathbf{u}^\top W \mathbf{u}}$. Because $W$ is symmetric positive definite, $(\mathbb{R}^n, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_W)$ is a valid inner product space, and all Hilbert-space geometry — projection, orthogonality, Gram–Schmidt — carries over in this weighted metric. This single change of geometry is the primitive that underlies weighted least squares, iteratively reweighted algorithms, and kernel-weighted regression; the present section develops only the first of these.

**Theorem (Gauss–Markov, weighted form).** Consider the linear model $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}] = \mathbf{0}$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \sigma^2 W^{-1}$ for a known positive-definite $W$. The *weighted least squares* (WLS) estimator,

$$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{WLS}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \|\mathbf{y} - A\mathbf{x}\|_W^2 = (A^\top W A)^{-1} A^\top W \mathbf{y},$$

is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) of $\mathbf{x}$.

*Proof sketch.* Let $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = L\mathbf{y}$ be any linear unbiased estimator, so $LA = I$. Write $L = (A^\top W A)^{-1} A^\top W + D$ with $DA = 0$. Then $\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \sigma^2 L W^{-1} L^\top = \sigma^2 [(A^\top W A)^{-1} + DW^{-1}D^\top]$. Since $DW^{-1}D^\top \succeq 0$, the WLS covariance is minimal among all linear unbiased estimators. $\square$

The key step to internalize is that the normal equations for $\|\mathbf{y} - A\mathbf{x}\|_W^2$ are identical in form to those for ordinary least squares once the ambient inner product is replaced by $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_W$. OLS is the special case $W = I$.

**Literature context.** Aitken (1935) established the generalized Gauss–Markov theorem in this form. The central open problem is that $W$ is rarely known in practice: it must be estimated or modeled. White (1980) introduced heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC) covariance estimators that remain valid without specifying $W$ exactly. Goldberger (1962) formalized feasible generalized least squares (FGLS), in which a pilot OLS fit yields residuals that are used to estimate $W$, and the procedure is iterated. A foundational question that remains incompletely resolved is how much efficiency is lost at each FGLS iteration when the pilot estimate of $W$ is noisy, particularly in finite samples. Recent work connecting weighted projections to attention mechanisms in neural networks (Tsai et al., 2019) suggests that the geometry introduced here has implications well beyond classical statistics, though making that connection rigorous is an open research direction.

**Suggested investigation.** Generate a synthetic regression dataset with known heteroskedastic noise: $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i^2)$ where $\sigma_i = f(x_i)$ for some chosen function $f$. Fit the model using (1) OLS, (2) oracle WLS with true $W = \operatorname{diag}(1/\sigma_i^2)$, and (3) feasible GLS with $W$ estimated from OLS residuals. Compare the sampling distributions of estimator variance across 1,000 Monte Carlo trials. Derive analytically the efficiency loss of OLS relative to oracle WLS using $\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathrm{OLS}}) / \operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathrm{WLS}})$ as a scalar summary. A natural extension is to vary the severity of heteroskedasticity and plot how this efficiency gap scales with the spread of the $\sigma_i$.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish three things explicitly: the algebraic derivation of the WLS normal equations from the weighted inner product, making clear that the geometry is the primitive and WLS is its immediate consequence; a proof or citation of the BLUE property with the unbiasedness constraint stated precisely; and an empirical section that reports estimator variance — not merely point estimates — across simulation trials, with Monte Carlo standard errors stated. The identification of $W$ with the inverse noise covariance should be stated as a theorem, not an analogy, so that the scope and assumptions of the result are unambiguous.

### activation_function (0.2s)

## Activation Function

**Definition.** Let $f : \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}}$ be a layer of a feedforward neural network parameterized by weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$ and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}}$. An *activation function* is a (typically nonlinear) map $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ applied elementwise, so that the layer computes $x \mapsto \sigma(Wx + b)$. Without $\sigma$, arbitrary depth collapses to a single affine map; the activation breaks this degeneracy. Classical choices include the sigmoid $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$, hyperbolic tangent $\tanh$, and the rectified linear unit $\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$. A prominent modern alternative is the Gaussian Error Linear Unit, $\text{GELU}(z) = z\,\Phi(z)$, where $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal CDF; unlike ReLU it is smooth and stochastically interpretable as a gate that drops units proportionally to how small they are relative to the input distribution (Hendrycks & Gimpel 2016).

**Theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik, Stinchcombe, White 1989).** Let $\sigma$ be any continuous, bounded, and non-constant function. Then finite sums of the form $\sum_{k=1}^{N} c_k \,\sigma(w_k^\top x + b_k)$ are dense in $C([0,1]^d)$ with respect to the uniform norm.

*Proof sketch.* The argument proceeds by contradiction via the Hahn–Banach theorem. Suppose the closure of the function class is a proper subspace $S \subsetneq C([0,1]^d)$. By Hahn–Banach there exists a nonzero bounded linear functional $\mu$ (representable by a signed Borel measure) annihilating $S$. One then shows that $\int \sigma(w^\top x + b)\,d\mu(x) = 0$ for all $w, b$ forces $\mu = 0$, a contradiction, using the fact that the Fourier transform of $\mu$ must vanish on a dense set when $\sigma$ is non-polynomial. Full technical details require the Riesz representation theorem and a careful application of the Stone–Weierstrass theorem; see Cybenko (1989) and the corrected version in Hornik (1991). $\square$

**Literature context.** Universal approximation is an *existence* result: it guarantees a network exists but says nothing about the width-depth tradeoff, the optimization landscape, or whether gradient methods find such a network. The depth-separation literature (Eldan & Shamir 2016; Telgarsky 2016) shows that depth-$2$ networks require exponentially more neurons than depth-$3$ to represent certain functions, motivating deep architectures. The choice of $\sigma$ is not neutral: ReLU induces piecewise-linear activations with countable breakpoints and enables sparse gradient flow, but suffers dying-neuron pathology when pre-activations remain negative throughout training. GELU introduces smooth, input-dependent gating and empirically outperforms ReLU at transformer scale (Hendrycks & Gimpel 2016). Open questions remain: (1) Can the optimal activation be *learned* jointly with weights — e.g., via KAN-style basis functions (Liu et al. 2024) — without destabilizing optimization? (2) What is the right inductive bias for $\sigma$ in physics-informed networks where the target has known periodicity or positivity constraints?

**Suggested investigation.** Fix a target function $g(x) = \sin(5x) + x^2$ on $[-1,1]$. Train shallow ($L=1$) and deep ($L=4$) networks with identical parameter counts under ReLU and GELU, recording mean-squared error and gradient norm at convergence across five random seeds. Plot the piecewise structure of the fitted ReLU network against the smooth GELU fit to make the approximation-theoretic distinction concrete. Vary width $N \in \{8, 16, 32, 64\}$ to trace empirical scaling curves for each activation and depth combination.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (a) empirical width-scaling curves for each $\sigma$ and depth; (b) a comparison of test error against the approximation-theoretic bound implied by the Jackson-type theorem for the target smoothness class; (c) a discussion of whether observed differences in convergence speed are attributable to curvature of the loss landscape (gradient flow analysis) or to the intrinsic function class (approximation theory). Null results — cases where both activations perform identically — are as informative as positive findings and should be reported with the same rigor.

### layers (0.2s)

## Layers

**Definition.** A *layer* in a feedforward neural network is a parametric map $\ell_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ of the form

$$\ell_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}),$$

where $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$, and $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is applied elementwise. A *depth-$L$ network* is the composition $f = \ell_{\theta_L} \circ \cdots \circ \ell_{\theta_1}$, fully specified by the width sequence $(d_0, d_1, \ldots, d_L)$ and the nonlinearity $\sigma$. Replacing $\sigma$ with the identity collapses the entire composition to a single affine map regardless of depth, which makes the nonlinearity structurally non-negotiable rather than cosmetic.

**Theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik, Stinchcombe & White 1989).** Let $\sigma$ be a continuous, non-constant sigmoidal function. Then the class of single-hidden-layer networks of arbitrary width is dense in $C(K)$ for every compact $K \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ under the sup-norm.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose, for contradiction, that the closure $\mathcal{F}$ of this class is a proper subspace of $C(K)$. By the Hahn–Banach theorem, there exists a bounded linear functional $\mu \in C(K)^*$ — identified with a signed Borel measure — such that $\mu$ annihilates $\mathcal{F}$ but is not identically zero. Annihilation requires

$$\int_K \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b)\, d\mu(\mathbf{x}) = 0 \quad \forall\, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n,\, b \in \mathbb{R}.$$

The *discriminatory* property of sigmoidal $\sigma$ — established via the Riemann–Stieltjes representation of the Fourier transform of $\mu$ — forces $\mu = 0$, a contradiction. $\square$

**Literature context.** The classical theorem establishes existence of a shallow approximator but says nothing about efficiency. The depth-efficiency literature — Eldan & Shamir (2016), Telgarsky (2016), Montufar et al. (2014) — shows that depth-$L$ ReLU networks can represent functions requiring exponentially large width at depth $L-1$, quantified via the number of linear regions: a depth-$L$ width-$w$ ReLU network achieves $O\!\left(w^{d_0}\right)^{L-1}$ linear regions, whereas a depth-2 network achieves only $O(w^{d_0})$. Yarotsky (2017) gives sharp rates for Sobolev balls $W^{p,\infty}([0,1]^d)$: ReLU networks of depth $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ achieve $L^\infty$ error $\varepsilon$ with $O(\varepsilon^{-d/p}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ parameters, matching the classical nonparametric lower bound. Open questions include the analogous tight rates for attention-based layers (transformers), the minimum depth required to represent solutions of specific PDE families, and whether implicit regularization of gradient descent selects parsimonious depth automatically.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the depth-separation construction of Telgarsky (2016, §4). Define the tent map $t(x) = \min(2x, 2-2x)$ on $[0,1]$ and let $t_k = t^{\circ k}$ be its $k$-fold composition. Fix $k \in \{4, 6, 8\}$. Train: (a) a depth-$\lceil k/2 \rceil$ ReLU network of moderate width, and (b) a depth-2 ReLU network whose width grows from $2^k$ to $4 \cdot 2^k$. Optimize both with Adam at learning rate $10^{-3}$ for 10,000 steps. Plot final $L^2$ test error against total parameter count. The theory predicts that network (a) achieves near-zero error with polynomial parameters while (b) fails to close below a constant-order gap at matched parameter budgets.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should state the closed form of $t_k$ explicitly (it is a piecewise-linear function with $2^k$ equal-length segments alternating between 0 and 1), report depth and width *separately* rather than aggregated parameter count (because the theoretical bound is width-conditional on depth), include an error table indexed by $k$ and architecture class, and explicitly compare the empirically observed crossover width against the theoretical $\Omega(2^k)$ lower bound to confirm or quantify deviation from the asymptotic prediction.

### neural_network (0.2s)

## Neural Network

**Definition.** A *feedforward neural network* of depth $L$ is a parameterized function $f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{d_0} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$ defined by the composition

$$f_\theta(x) = W_L \sigma(W_{L-1} \sigma(\cdots \sigma(W_1 x + b_1) \cdots) + b_{L-1}) + b_L,$$

where $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a nonlinear activation applied elementwise, $W_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}}$ and $b_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$ are learnable weight matrices and bias vectors, and $\theta = \{W_\ell, b_\ell\}_{\ell=1}^L$ is the full parameter vector. The integer $d_\ell$ is the *width* at layer $\ell$; $L$ is the *depth*. Recurrent and convolutional variants introduce parameter sharing across positions, but the compositional structure and gradient-based training remain the same.

**Theorem (Universal Approximation, Cybenko 1989; Hornik et al. 1989).** Let $\sigma$ be a continuous, bounded, non-constant function. For every $\varepsilon > 0$ and every continuous $g : [0,1]^d \to \mathbb{R}$, there exists a single-hidden-layer network $f_\theta$ such that $\sup_{x \in [0,1]^d} |f_\theta(x) - g(x)| < \varepsilon$.

*Proof sketch.* The set of finite linear combinations of compositions of the form $\sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top x + b)$ is dense in $C([0,1]^d)$ equipped with the uniform norm. Density follows from the fact that this set is not contained in any closed proper subspace—verified by a measure-theoretic argument invoking the Hahn–Banach theorem in the dual of $C([0,1]^d)$. Because $\sigma$ is non-polynomial, no such proper subspace contains it, completing the argument. $\square$

This result is existential, not constructive: it guarantees that a representation exists but provides no bound on the required width, no learning algorithm, and no generalization guarantee.

**Literature context.** The classical universal approximation theorem leaves several fundamental questions open, each the subject of active research. The theorem as stated is silent on *how large* the network must be: for smooth target functions in $d$ dimensions, width requirements can scale exponentially in $d$, a manifestation of the curse of dimensionality. Barron (1993) shows that functions whose Fourier transform has finite first-moment norm admit shallow approximations with width $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ independent of $d$, identifying a function class where the theorem becomes quantitatively useful. The question of whether *depth* can substitute for width in a non-trivial sense is settled in one direction—Telgarsky (2016) and Eldan & Shamir (2016) exhibit functions that depth-$k$ networks represent efficiently but depth-$(k-1)$ networks require exponential width to approximate—yet a complete characterization of the depth–width tradeoff for natural function classes remains open. The leap from approximation to *learning*—whether gradient descent on a finite sample finds the guaranteed representation—is an entirely separate problem not addressed by these theorems, and remains the central open question in the theory of deep learning.

**Suggested investigation.** Study the empirical width requirement of the universal approximation theorem directly. Fix target functions of increasing complexity: $g_1(x) = \sin(2\pi x)$, $g_2(x) = \sin(2\pi x) + \sin(6\pi x)$, and $g_3(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{5} k^{-1}\sin(2\pi k x)$ on $[0,1]$. For each target, train single-hidden-layer networks with widths $n \in \{8, 32, 128, 512\}$ until convergence (or a fixed epoch budget) and record the uniform approximation error $\sup_x |f_\theta(x) - g(x)|$ on a held-out grid. Plot error as a function of $n$ on a log–log scale and test whether the observed scaling matches the $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ Barron rate or degrades faster with spectral complexity of the target. Repeat with ReLU and $\tanh$ activations to assess sensitivity to $\sigma$.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a precise statement of the universal approximation theorem and the Barron class condition, with an explicit derivation of the $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ width bound for functions in that class; (2) empirical approximation-error curves as a function of width and target complexity, with error bars over random initializations; (3) a comparison of the observed scaling exponent against the theoretical prediction, noting where the bound is tight and where it is loose; and (4) a discussion of what the gap between the existential guarantee and the empirical result implies—specifically, whether the shortfall is attributable to optimization failure or to the function lying outside the Barron class, and how one might distinguish these explanations experimentally.

### perceptron (0.1s)

## Perceptron

**Definition.** A *perceptron* is a parameterized binary classifier $f_{\mathbf{w}, b} : \mathbb{R}^d \to \{-1, +1\}$ defined by

$$f_{\mathbf{w},b}(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b),$$

where $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a weight vector and $b \in \mathbb{R}$ is a bias. Without loss of generality one absorbs the bias into an augmented weight $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (b, \mathbf{w})^\top$ with $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (1, \mathbf{x})^\top$, yielding $f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{x}})$. The Rosenblatt (1958) update rule, given a misclassified example $(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t)$ with $y_t \in \{-1,+1\}$, is

$$\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1} \leftarrow \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_t + y_t \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t.$$

**Theorem (Perceptron Convergence, Novikoff 1962).** *Let $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n \subset \mathbb{R}^d \times \{-1,+1\}$ be linearly separable with margin $\gamma = \min_i y_i (\mathbf{w}^* \cdot \mathbf{x}_i) > 0$ for some unit-norm separator $\mathbf{w}^*$, and $R = \max_i \|\mathbf{x}_i\|$. Then the online perceptron algorithm makes at most $\lfloor R^2/\gamma^2 \rfloor$ mistakes.*

*Proof sketch.* Track two quantities across mistake steps $t$: the inner product $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_t \cdot \mathbf{w}^*$ grows by at least $\gamma$ per update (since $y_t(\mathbf{w}^* \cdot \mathbf{x}_t) \geq \gamma$), giving $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_t \cdot \mathbf{w}^* \geq M\gamma$ after $M$ mistakes; meanwhile $\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_t\|^2$ grows by at most $R^2$ per update (since $y_t(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_t \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t) \leq 0$ at a mistake), giving $\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_t\|^2 \leq MR^2$. Applying Cauchy–Schwarz, $M\gamma \leq \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_t \cdot \mathbf{w}^* \leq \|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_t\| \leq \sqrt{MR^2}$, which yields $M \leq R^2/\gamma^2$. $\square$

**Literature context.** The bound is tight up to constants: Blum & Warmuth (1992) construct adversarial sequences achieving $\Theta(R^2/\gamma^2)$ mistakes, so no algorithm in the online mistake-bound model can improve the exponent. The connection to support vector machines is structural — the SVM margin $\gamma_{\text{SVM}}$ maximizes the Novikoff denominator, making SVMs the "best-case" input for perceptron-style analysis (Vapnik 1995) — but this connection is taken up in a later section. In the non-separable regime the perceptron does not converge; the voted perceptron of Freund & Schapire (1999) addresses this by maintaining a weighted ensemble of all intermediate hypotheses, and its analysis introduces one additional mechanism best treated separately. Modern interest resurfaces in the context of neural networks: a single perceptron is a one-neuron network, and gradient descent on the squared loss with a sigmoid activation recovers Rosenblatt's rule in the limit of hard thresholding. Whether the discrete mistake-bound model and continuous SGD convergence rates can be unified under a single framework remains an active research question (Shalev-Shwartz 2011).

**Suggested investigation.** Implement the Rosenblatt perceptron on a synthetic 2D dataset with controlled margin $\gamma$. Vary $\gamma \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5\}$ and sample size $n \in \{100, 500, 2000\}$; record mistake counts $M$ across multiple random draws and compare to the Novikoff bound $\lfloor R^2/\gamma^2 \rfloor$. The central empirical question is whether $M$ saturates the theoretical bound or falls well below it, and how the ratio $M_{\text{emp}}/\lfloor R^2/\gamma^2 \rfloor$ behaves as a function of $\gamma$ and $R$. A natural extension is to vary the ambient dimension $d$ while holding $\gamma$ and $R$ fixed, probing whether the bound's dimension-independence is reflected in practice.

**Report-writing note.** A rigorous write-up should reproduce the Novikoff proof with explicit constants, present the empirical mistake curves alongside the theoretical bound (as separate panels for each $\gamma$ value), and quantify the tightness ratio $M_{\text{emp}}/\lfloor R^2/\gamma^2 \rfloor$ as a function of $\gamma$, $R$, and $n$. The discussion should explain why the bound is pessimistic in practice — typically because adversarial orderings are not realized by random draws — and identify which geometric parameter ($\gamma$ versus $R$) dominates empirical convergence speed. The write-up should conclude with a precise statement of what the experiment does and does not establish about the tightness of the bound.

### depth (0.1s)

## Depth

**Definition.** Let $G = (V, E)$ be a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where each vertex $v \in V$ represents a concept and each directed edge $(u, v) \in E$ encodes the prerequisite relation "$u$ must precede $v$." The *depth* of a vertex $v$, written $d(v)$, is the length (number of edges) of the longest directed path from any source vertex (in-degree zero) to $v$:

$$d(v) = \max\bigl\{\,|P| : P \text{ is a directed path terminating at } v\bigr\},$$

where $|P|$ denotes edge-count and $d(v) = 0$ for all sources. Depth is the minimal number of prerequisite steps that must be traversed sequentially before $v$ is reachable, regardless of how other parts of the graph are studied in parallel.

---

**Theorem (Topological Depth Computation).** *Let $G = (V, E)$ be a DAG with $|V| = n$, $|E| = m$. The depth function $d : V \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ satisfies*

$$d(v) = \begin{cases} 0 & \mathrm{in\text{-}deg}(v) = 0, \\ 1 + \max_{(u,v) \in E}\, d(u) & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}$$

*and is computable in $O(n + m)$ time.*

*Proof.* Produce a topological ordering $\sigma = (v_1, \ldots, v_n)$ of $G$ via Kahn's algorithm or DFS postorder reversal (existence guaranteed by acyclicity; see Cormen et al.\ [CLRS], Ch.\ 22). By the topological property, every predecessor $u$ with $(u, v_i) \in E$ satisfies $u = v_j$ for some $j < i$, so $d(u)$ has already been assigned when $v_i$ is processed. A single forward pass over $\sigma$ evaluates the recurrence in $O(n + m)$ total work. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary (Critical Path).** The *diameter* $\Delta(G) = \max_{v \in V} d(v)$ is the length of the *critical path* — the minimum number of sequential learning steps required to reach the deepest concept in $G$, irrespective of any parallelism permitted by the graph structure. No curriculum respecting the prerequisite ordering can achieve the deepest concept in fewer than $\Delta(G)$ sequential steps.

---

**Literature context.** The critical-path interpretation is the direct knowledge-graph analogue of the *Critical Path Method* (Kelley & Walker, 1959) and longest-path scheduling (Garey & Johnson, 1979, problem SS14). In *knowledge space theory* (Doignon & Falmagne, 1999; Falmagne et al., 2013), the closest relative is the notion of a *fringe* — concepts on the boundary of a learner's current knowledge state — but per-concept depth is not explicitly derived from structural graph properties there. Applied learning-analytics work (Manouselis et al., 2011; Nabizadeh et al., 2020) uses depth as a scalar feature in recommender pipelines without establishing its distributional properties. Several questions remain open: (1) Given a learner knowledge state $K \subsetneq V$ and target concept $t$, what is the minimum-depth completion path from $K$ to $\{t\}$, and what structural properties of $G$ (tree-width, planarity, bounded degree) make this tractable in practice? (2) Is the depth distribution of real-world knowledge DAGs log-normal, as informally asserted in the learning-analytics literature, or does it depend systematically on domain?

---

**Suggested investigation.** Construct or extract a prerequisite DAG for a moderately complex domain — undergraduate linear algebra or real analysis provides a workable target of roughly 50–200 concepts. Compute $d(v)$ for every vertex and plot the full depth distribution. Then test two falsifiable claims: first, that depth predicts student time-to-mastery better than in-degree alone, measurable against a public dataset such as ASSISTments or OLI; second, that expert-authored graphs and NLP-extracted graphs for the same domain yield significantly different $\Delta(G)$ values and median depths, implying that structural properties are sensitive to graph provenance. As an extension, perturb the graph by removing or adding single prerequisite edges and measure the resulting change in $\Delta(G)$: this quantifies the *fragility* of the critical path to editorial choices.

---

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should establish: the formal depth definition and algorithm with stated complexity; graph provenance and any preprocessing decisions (cycle resolution, concept merging); the depth distribution as a histogram with mean, median, and maximum; regression results for student performance with depth and competing predictors; and an explicit verdict on each stated hypothesis. Attach the graph as a reproducible artifact in GraphML or JSON-LD so that results can be replicated or contested against alternative domain graphs.

### multilayer_perceptron (0.2s)

## Multilayer Perceptron

**Definition.** A *multilayer perceptron* (MLP) with $L$ hidden layers is a parametric function $f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{d_0} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}}$ defined by the composition

$$f_\theta(x) = W^{(L+1)} \sigma\!\left(W^{(L)} \sigma\!\left(\cdots \sigma\!\left(W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)}\right)\cdots\right) + b^{(L)}\right) + b^{(L+1)},$$

where $W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}}$ and $b^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$ are learnable parameters and $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a fixed nonlinearity applied elementwise. The total parameter count is $|\theta| = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L+1} d_\ell(d_{\ell-1} + 1)$. The model is trained by minimizing an empirical risk $\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f_\theta(x_i), y_i)$ via stochastic gradient descent or one of its adaptive variants.

**Theorem (Universal Approximation, Hornik–Stinchcombe–White 1989).** *Let $\sigma$ be a continuous, bounded, and non-constant activation. For any $\varepsilon > 0$ and any $g \in C(K)$ on a compact $K \subset \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$, there exist $N \in \mathbb{N}$ and parameters $\{v_j, w_j, b_j\}_{j=1}^N$ such that*

$$\sup_{x \in K} \left| g(x) - \sum_{j=1}^{N} v_j\, \sigma(w_j^\top x + b_j) \right| < \varepsilon.$$

*Proof sketch.* The set of finite linear combinations of shifts and scalings of $\sigma$ forms a subspace $\mathcal{H}_\sigma \subset C(K)$. Because $\sigma$ is non-constant, discriminatory functions in $\mathcal{H}_\sigma$ separate points; the Stone–Weierstrass theorem then implies $\mathcal{H}_\sigma$ is dense in $C(K)$ under the sup-norm. $\square$

This theorem is existential: it guarantees width $N$ may grow arbitrarily with $\varepsilon$ and $d_0$, but says nothing about the required $N$ for a given function class, about generalization, or about whether gradient descent finds the approximating weights.

**Literature context.** The depth-width tradeoff is an active frontier. Barron (1993) showed that single-hidden-layer networks approximate functions with finite first-moment spectra at rate $O(1/N)$ in $L^2$, avoiding the curse of dimensionality for that function class. Depth-separation results (Telgarsky 2016; Eldan–Shamir 2016) prove that certain functions require exponentially wide shallow networks but are efficiently realized by deep ones, motivating the practical shift toward depth. The loss landscape of overparameterized MLPs — why gradient descent finds global minima despite non-convexity — remains incompletely understood; the neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime (Jacot et al. 2018) provides a linearization in the infinite-width limit, but its applicability to practical finite-width, finite-step training is contested.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the depth-separation experiment of Telgarsky (2016, §4): construct the piecewise-linear "triangle wave" function $t_k : [0,1] \to [0,1]$ with $2^k$ oscillations and train MLPs of depths $L \in \{1, 2, k\}$ with matched parameter budgets (ReLU activations) to approximate $t_k$ in $L^2$. Record approximation error versus $k$ for each depth. Then vary the width at fixed depth to locate the crossover point where width can compensate for depth. This isolates the combinatorial mechanism underlying depth separation and builds intuition for when architectural depth is irreducible.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish three things cleanly: (1) a precise statement of the function family and the parameter-matching protocol used, so the comparison is not confounded by total parameter count; (2) empirical loss curves alongside the theoretical bound on oscillation count as a function of depth, cited against Telgarsky (2016, Theorem 1.1); (3) a discussion of whether the NTK linearization predicts the observed training dynamics, and where it fails—this bridges the experiment to the open question of when the NTK regime is a faithful proxy for real networks.

### artificial_intelligence (0.2s)

## Artificial Intelligence

**Definition.** Artificial intelligence (AI) denotes the study and construction of computational agents whose behavior optimizes some performance criterion over an environment, formally captured by the tuple $\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, T, R, \gamma \rangle$ — a Markov Decision Process where $T : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \to \Delta(\mathcal{S})$ is the transition kernel, $R : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \to \mathbb{R}$ the reward signal, and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ the discount factor. An agent policy $\pi : \mathcal{S} \to \Delta(\mathcal{A})$ is optimal if it maximizes the expected discounted return $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t R(s_t, a_t)\mid s_0 = s\right]$. This formalism, rooted in Bellman (1957) and formalized through the RL literature (Sutton & Barto, 2018), is deliberately agnostic to the computational substrate — it encompasses symbolic planners, neural networks, and hybrid systems equally.

**Theorem (Bellman Optimality).** There exists a unique fixed point $V^* \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S})$ of the Bellman operator $(\mathcal{T}V)(s) = \max_{a}\!\left[R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} T(s'|s,a)\,V(s')\right]$, and $V^* = V^{\pi^*}$ for the greedy policy $\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a [R(s,a) + \gamma \mathbb{E}_{s'}V^*(s')]$.

*Proof sketch.* $\mathcal{T}$ is a $\gamma$-contraction on the Banach space $(C_b(\mathcal{S}), \|\cdot\|_\infty)$ under the sup norm; the Banach fixed-point theorem guarantees existence, uniqueness, and convergence of value iteration $V_{k+1} = \mathcal{T}V_k$ at rate $\gamma^k$. $\square$

**Literature context.** The MDP framework crystallizes a century-long project — from Turing's (1950) imitation game to McCarthy's 1956 Dartmouth proposal — into a tractable mathematical object. Contemporary AI has bifurcated: *learning-based* approaches approximate $\pi^*$ from data (deep RL, large language models), while *symbolic* approaches exploit the structure of $\mathcal{S}$ (SAT solvers, knowledge graphs). The central unresolved tension is *sample efficiency vs. generalization*: empirical scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) suggest that language model performance obeys $L(N,D) \sim N^{-\alpha} + D^{-\beta}$ with $\alpha \approx \beta \approx 0.07$, yet theoretical understanding of why over-parameterized networks generalize remains incomplete (Bartlett et al., 2021; Belkin, 2021). Whether the MDP is the right unit of analysis for agents that reason over language and knowledge graphs — rather than numeric state spaces — is itself an open question.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the tabular value-iteration convergence result on a small gridworld, then replace the tabular representation with a neural approximator and observe the failure modes (oscillation, overestimation bias). Formally, investigate how the contraction property degrades when $\mathcal{T}$ is approximated by $\hat{\mathcal{T}}_\theta$: derive the error bound $\|V^* - V^\pi_\theta\|_\infty \leq \frac{\epsilon}{1-\gamma}$ and identify the conditions under which $\epsilon$ remains bounded during gradient descent. Extend the investigation to a language-model agent acting on a text environment (e.g., AlfWorld), and measure how the scaling law exponent shifts with context length.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should (i) state the MDP formulation precisely, naming every tuple element; (ii) prove or cite the contraction argument; (iii) report the empirical convergence curve alongside the theoretical $\gamma^k$ envelope; (iv) characterize the approximation error as a function of network width and training steps; and (v) position the findings relative to at least one contemporary benchmark, explaining what the gap between theory and observation implies for future work.

### deep_learning (0.2s)

## Deep Learning

**Definition.** A deep neural network is a parametric function $f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ defined by the composition $f_\theta = \ell_L \circ \ell_{L-1} \circ \cdots \circ \ell_1$, where each layer $\ell_k(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma(W_k \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_k)$ applies an affine map followed by a pointwise nonlinearity $\sigma$. The parameter set $\theta = \{W_k, \mathbf{b}_k\}_{k=1}^L$ is optimized by minimizing an empirical risk $\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i)$ via stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or its adaptive variants (Adam, AdaGrad). Deep learning is not, however, a settled technology — its theoretical foundations remain substantively incomplete, and every practitioner encounters regimes where received intuitions fail.

**Theorem (Universal Approximation, Hornik–Stinchcombe–White, 1989).** Let $\sigma$ be a continuous, bounded, nonconstant function. For any $f \in C(\mathcal{K})$ on a compact $\mathcal{K} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ and any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a single-hidden-layer network $\hat{f}$ such that $\sup_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{K}} |f(\mathbf{x}) - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x})| < \varepsilon$.

**Proof sketch.** The proof uses the density of finite linear combinations of ridge functions $\sum_j c_j \sigma(\mathbf{w}_j^\top \mathbf{x} + b_j)$ in $C(\mathcal{K})$, established via the Stone–Weierstrass theorem and properties of the Fourier transform of $\sigma$. Width sufficient; depth not required. $\square$

This result is frequently misread as a justification for depth. It is not: it guarantees existence of a width-$N$ shallow network for some $N$ that may be exponentially large in dimension. The depth-separation literature (Eldan–Shamir 2016, Telgarsky 2016) shows that there exist functions computed by depth-$L$ networks that require exponential width in depth-$(L-1)$ networks, but these separations are worst-case and rarely characterize practical learning scenarios.

**What remains open.** The central mysteries are not architectural but optimization-theoretic and statistical. Why does SGD on nonconvex loss landscapes find solutions that generalize well? The double-descent phenomenon (Belkin et al. 2019, Hastie et al. 2022) shows that interpolating models — trained to zero training loss — can achieve low test error, violating classical bias–variance tradeoffs. The implicit regularization of SGD (Gunasekar et al. 2018) and the edge-of-stability phenomenon (Cohen et al. 2021), wherein gradient descent operates stably above the theoretical stability threshold of $2/\eta_{\max}$, remain active research areas. Neural tangent kernel (NTK) theory (Jacot et al. 2018) provides a tractable infinite-width limit but does not explain finite-width generalization or feature learning.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the double-descent curve on a benchmark dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10 with a ResNet family). Vary model size from underparameterized ($d \ll n$) through the interpolation threshold to overparameterized ($d \gg n$). Plot test risk as a function of parameter count. Then perturb the training procedure — replace SGD with full-batch gradient descent, or change the learning rate schedule — and measure how the location and amplitude of the double-descent peak shift. This isolates the role of the optimizer versus the model class in producing the phenomenon.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) precise definitions of the underparameterized and interpolation regimes in terms of effective degrees of freedom; (2) a theoretical prediction for peak location derived from the random matrix theory literature (e.g., Mei–Montanari 2022 for random features); (3) quantitative comparison between prediction and experiment; and (4) a clear statement of which aspects of the observed behavior are explained by existing theory and which constitute open problems. Do not treat generalization as explained by any single existing framework — that claim would be false.

### natural_language_processing (0.2s)

## Natural Language Processing

**Definition.** Natural language processing (NLP) is the subfield of artificial intelligence concerned with computational models of human language — its syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and discourse structure. Formally, let $\Sigma^*$ denote the Kleene closure of a finite vocabulary $\Sigma$. A language model is a probability distribution $P_\theta : \Sigma^* \to [0,1]$ satisfying $\sum_{w \in \Sigma^*} P_\theta(w) = 1$, parameterized by $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Modern NLP is largely the study of how to define, learn, and exploit such distributions at scale, and what structures emerge from doing so.

**Core result: the self-attention mechanism as a sequence transducer.** The Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) defines a sequence-to-sequence map via multi-head scaled dot-product attention. Given query, key, and value matrices $Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}$, the attention output is

$$\operatorname{Attn}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.$$

Stacking $h$ such heads with learned projections $W_i^Q, W_i^K, W_i^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_k}$ and composing with position-wise feedforward layers yields the full encoder block. A foundational result (Yun et al., 2020) shows that Transformers are universal approximators of continuous sequence-to-sequence functions on compact domains — a universality theorem analogous to those for MLPs, but operating over permutation-variant inputs. The proof constructs an explicit depth-width trade-off: approximation error $\epsilon$ is achievable with $O(\epsilon^{-1/p})$ heads given $L^p$-norm targets.

**Literature context and open questions.** The scaling hypothesis — that loss $\mathcal{L}$ obeys a power law $\mathcal{L}(N) \approx \alpha N^{-\beta}$ in parameter count $N$ (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) — has driven architectural choices and compute budgets. However, the precise functional form, the dependence on data distribution, and whether emergent capabilities (Wei et al., 2022) are genuine phase transitions or smooth threshold effects remain contested. Interpretability remains largely open: it is unknown whether polysemanticity of individual neurons (Elhage et al., 2022) is an artifact of superposition or reflects something fundamental about high-dimensional representation. Similarly, the relationship between perplexity and downstream task performance lacks a tight theoretical characterization.

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable graduate project is to replicate the scaling law fitting pipeline on a controlled corpus (e.g., a domain-specific subset of The Pile). Specifically: train a family of GPT-style autoregressive models spanning at least two decades in parameter count $N \in \{10^6, 10^7, 10^8\}$ and two decades in token budget $D$; fit the Chinchilla loss surface $\mathcal{L}(N,D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$ via nonlinear least squares; and test whether the optimal compute-matched pair $N^* \propto C^{0.5}$ (Hoffmann et al.) holds under domain shift. The experiment isolates whether scaling exponents are universal or corpus-dependent — a question with direct implications for how practitioners should allocate compute for specialized models.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should clearly state the training corpus statistics (token count, domain, deduplication strategy), the optimizer and learning rate schedule used across runs (to exclude confounds), the fitting procedure and confidence intervals on $\alpha, \beta$, and a comparison of the empirically optimal allocation against the Chinchilla prediction. It should conclude with a statement about generalizability: whether the fitted surface would recommend a different architecture or data mix than the standard recommendation, and what further ablation would be needed to confirm that conclusion.

### speech_recognition_tts (30.1s)

## Speech Recognition Tts

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}^{T \times F}$ denote the space of acoustic feature sequences of length $T$ with $F$ mel-filterbank coefficients per frame. Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) seeks a mapping $\Phi_{\text{ASR}} : \mathcal{X} \to \Sigma^*$, where $\Sigma^*$ is the set of token sequences over a vocabulary $\Sigma$. Text-to-Speech synthesis (TTS) seeks the inverse mapping $\Phi_{\text{TTS}} : \Sigma^* \to \mathcal{X}$. Jointly, the composition $\Phi_{\text{TTS}} \circ \Phi_{\text{ASR}}$ defines a *voice transformation* or *speech resynthesis* operator; its fixed points, cycles, and information-theoretic fidelity bounds constitute the central open problems of end-to-end speech processing.

**Theorem (Connectionist Temporal Classification lower bound).** Let $\pi : \{1,\ldots,T\} \to \Sigma \cup \{\epsilon\}$ be a path over the extended alphabet including the blank token $\epsilon$, and let $\mathcal{B}(\pi)$ denote the label sequence obtained by collapsing repeated symbols and removing blanks. The CTC objective maximizes $\log p(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \log \sum_{\pi : \mathcal{B}(\pi)=\mathbf{y}} \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\pi_t \mid \mathbf{x})$. By the data-processing inequality, for any deterministic decoder $d$,
$$
H(\mathbf{Y} \mid \hat{\mathbf{Y}}) \leq H(\mathbf{Y} \mid \mathbf{X}),
$$
so the irreducible acoustic ambiguity — homophones, co-articulation, prosodic neutralization — establishes a floor on word error rate that no architecture can breach without external linguistic priors.

**Proof sketch.** The mutual information $I(\mathbf{X}; \mathbf{Y}) = H(\mathbf{Y}) - H(\mathbf{Y} \mid \mathbf{X})$ is bounded by the channel capacity of the vocal tract viewed as a noisy channel. Since $H(\mathbf{Y} \mid \mathbf{X}) > 0$ for natural speech corpora (homophone pairs such as "bare"/"bear" contribute identically distributed acoustic realizations in context-free settings), $\Phi_{\text{ASR}}$ is necessarily lossy. The TTS inverse therefore cannot recover the original waveform in the $L^2$ sense without conditioning on speaker identity and prosodic annotations beyond the text transcript. $\square$

**Literature context.** The transformer-based ASR architecture of Whisper (Radford et al., 2022) achieves near-human word error rates on clean speech by training on $680{,}000$ hours of weakly supervised data, yet degrades measurably on accented or noisy speech — a distribution shift problem still unresolved for low-resource languages. On the TTS side, neural vocoders (HiFi-GAN, Voicebox, Matcha-TTS) demonstrate high perceptual naturalness on constrained domains but exhibit brittleness under out-of-distribution phoneme sequences and zero-shot speaker cloning remains an active challenge (Casanova et al., 2022; Le Paine et al., 2023). The compositionality of prosody — how intonation, rhythm, and stress interact with semantics — is not captured by current end-to-end models (Shen et al., 2023).

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a round-trip pipeline $\Phi_{\text{TTS}} \circ \Phi_{\text{ASR}}$ using Whisper (medium) and a publicly available TTS model (e.g., Coqui XTTS or Matcha-TTS). Measure round-trip Word Error Rate (rt-WER) and Mel Cepstral Distortion (MCD) as a function of speaker accent (VCTK corpus), noise SNR, and text register (formal vs. spontaneous). Fit a linear model $\text{rt-WER} \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{SNR} + \beta_2 \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{accented}] + \epsilon$ and test whether $\beta_2$ is significantly nonzero under heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.

**Report-writing note.** A satisfactory write-up should establish: (i) a clear definition of round-trip fidelity and its relationship to the information-theoretic bound derived above; (ii) a reproducible experimental protocol with exact model versions and corpus splits; (iii) error analysis distinguishing acoustic errors from language model interpolation errors; and (iv) a discussion of whether the observed failure modes are addressable by fine-tuning, data augmentation, or require architectural changes — grounding each claim in a specific citation.
