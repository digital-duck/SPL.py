### sequence (9.1s)

## Sequence

A **sequence** is an ordered list of numbers, measurements, or other data values, where each value is called a **term**. Order matters: the sequence $2, 4, 6, 8$ is not the same object as $8, 6, 4, 2$, even though both contain identical values. Sequences are typically written as $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots, a_n, \ldots$, where the subscript indicates the term's position, and $a_n$ denotes the general (or $n$-th) term. A sequence can be finite, like a list of monthly sales figures for one year, or infinite, like the sequence of prime numbers.

Two patterns appear constantly in practice. An **arithmetic sequence** adds a constant difference $d$ at each step: $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d$. A **geometric sequence** multiplies by a constant ratio $r$ at each step: $a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{n-1}$.

**Worked example.** A server logs response times (in milliseconds) for five consecutive requests: $120, 135, 150, 165, 180$. Checking consecutive differences — $135-120=15$, $150-135=15$, $165-150=15$, $180-165=15$ — confirms this is arithmetic with $a_1 = 120$ and $d = 15$. The formula predicts the 10th request's time without listing all prior terms: $a_{10} = 120 + (10-1)(15) = 255$ ms.

**Problem-solving application.** Sequences let you compress a repeated pattern into a single formula, which is essential for forecasting and for writing efficient code. Suppose a bacterial culture doubles every hour, starting at 500 cells: $a_n = 500 \cdot 2^{n-1}$. Rather than doubling by hand 12 times, you compute $a_{12} = 500 \cdot 2^{11} = 1{,}024{,}000$ directly. This same skill applies to compound interest, radioactive decay, and loop iteration counts in programming.

To identify which formula applies to a new dataset, test it systematically: compute consecutive differences (constant $\Rightarrow$ arithmetic) and consecutive ratios (constant $\Rightarrow$ geometric). If neither is constant, the sequence may follow a different rule — such as a recursive definition, where each term depends on one or more previous terms, as in the Fibonacci sequence $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \ldots$, where $a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}$.

### seasonal_component (10.9s)

## Seasonal Component

The seasonal component is the part of a time series that repeats at fixed, known intervals tied to the calendar: retail sales spiking every December, electricity demand rising every summer afternoon, or airline bookings peaking every July. Unlike the trend, which reflects a long-run direction, or a business cycle, which has no fixed length, seasonality is defined by its predictable periodicity — it returns at the same phase of the same cycle every time.

**Definition.** In an additive decomposition, a time series $y_t$ is modeled as

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

where $T_t$ is the trend, $S_t$ is the seasonal component, and $R_t$ is the residual (irregular) component. The defining property of $S_t$ is periodicity: for a season length $m$ (e.g., $m = 12$ for monthly data with yearly seasonality),

$$S_t = S_{t+m}$$

and the seasonal effects for a full cycle sum to approximately zero, $\sum_{i=1}^{m} S_i \approx 0$, so that seasonality shifts values up or down around the trend without adding a net long-run drift. When the seasonal swings grow proportionally with the level of the series (e.g., holiday sales spikes get bigger as a store grows), a multiplicative model $y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t$ is more appropriate, with seasonal factors averaging to 1 rather than 0.

**Worked example.** Suppose a coffee shop's monthly revenue is trending upward by \$200/month, but July revenue is reliably \$800 above trend (iced drinks) and January is \$600 below trend (post-holiday slump). If July's trend value is \$5,000, actual July revenue is predicted at \$5,800; if January's trend value is \$5,200, actual January revenue is predicted at \$4,600. The seasonal component is what separates "July was a strong month" from "July is *always* strong" — a distinction critical for not overreacting to expected fluctuations.

**Application.** Estimating $S_t$ typically involves computing a moving average to isolate $T_t$, subtracting it from $y_t$ to get $S_t + R_t$, then averaging those detrended values by season (all Julys together, all Januaries together) to cancel out the noisy $R_t$. Once identified, seasonal adjustment — removing $S_t$ from the series — lets analysts compare, say, this March to last March on a level playing field, which is exactly what government agencies do when reporting "seasonally adjusted" unemployment or retail figures.

### time_series (17.1s)

## Time Series

A time series is a sequence of numerical measurements of the same variable, recorded at regular, ordered intervals — daily closing stock prices, monthly unemployment rates, hourly temperature readings. What distinguishes a time series from an ordinary list of numbers is that order matters: the value at time $t$ is understood to be related to the values that came before it. Formally, we write it as $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T$, where the subscript indexes time rather than an arbitrary label, and $T$ is the number of observations.

**Worked example.** Suppose a small bakery records weekly sales (in units) for eight weeks: 120, 135, 128, 150, 142, 160, 155, 170. Plotted against time, this sequence reveals two things a plain average would hide: an upward trend (sales are generally climbing) and short-term fluctuation (week-to-week ups and downs around that trend). A simple way to separate signal from noise is a moving average — replacing each point with the average of itself and its neighbors. A 3-week moving average centered on week 4, for instance, is $(128 + 150 + 142)/3 \approx 140$. Repeating this across the series smooths out the week-to-week bounce and makes the underlying trend easier to see.

**Problem-solving application.** Time series analysis is used whenever you need to forecast a future value, detect a change in pattern, or decide whether a fluctuation is "normal" or a genuine anomaly. The central question is always the same: how much of the movement in the data is trend, how much is a pattern that repeats at fixed intervals (seasonality, like higher retail sales every December), and how much is just random noise left over once both are accounted for? A data analyst forecasting next month's bakery sales would separate the trend and seasonal parts using tools like the moving average above, then look at how far the actual values deviate from that smoothed line — the size of those leftover deviations tells you how much confidence to place in the forecast. The same logic underlies far higher-stakes applications: epidemiologists tracking whether daily case counts are truly declining or just noisy, and central banks deciding whether an inflation uptick is a lasting trend or a temporary blip. In every case, the discipline is the same — don't react to a single data point; look at the ordered sequence, separate trend from noise, and only then draw a conclusion.

### period (18.7s)

## Period

The period of a seasonal pattern is the smallest time interval, $T$, after which the pattern repeats itself exactly. If sales spike every December and fall back to baseline by February, then rise again the following December, the period is 12 months — regardless of how large or small the spikes are. Period describes *timing*, not magnitude: it answers the question "how often?" rather than "how much?"

Formally, a time series component $S_t$ is periodic with period $T$ if $S_{t+T} = S_t$ for all $t$. The key word is *smallest*: a pattern that repeats every 12 months technically also "repeats" every 24 or 36 months, but its period is 12, because that is the shortest interval satisfying the condition.

**Worked example.** Suppose a company records ice cream sales monthly and observes this seasonal index pattern, repeating year after year:

$$
\text{Jan}=0.6,\ \text{Feb}=0.6,\ \text{Mar}=0.8,\ \text{Apr}=1.0,\ \text{May}=1.2,\ \text{Jun}=1.4,
$$
$$
\text{Jul}=1.5,\ \text{Aug}=1.4,\ \text{Sep}=1.0,\ \text{Oct}=0.8,\ \text{Nov}=0.6,\ \text{Dec}=0.6
$$

After December, January's value returns to 0.6, matching the value 12 months earlier. No shorter sub-cycle (say, every 6 months) reproduces the same sequence — June (1.4) doesn't match December (0.6). So the period is confirmed as 12 months, consistent with an annual demand cycle driven by summer heat.

**Problem-solving application.** Identifying the correct period is the first step in almost any seasonal analysis, because it determines the "lag" you use in decomposition or seasonal-differencing models like SARIMA. If you mistakenly assume a period of 4 (quarterly) for data that actually cycles every 12 months (monthly-annual), your seasonal adjustment will be wrong, and residual seasonality will leak into your "deseasonalized" series, distorting trend estimates.

A practical diagnostic: plot the series and scan for the shortest interval at which the pattern visibly repeats, then confirm it by checking that the values line up across cycles (as in the worked example above). For daily retail data, you might spot two overlapping periods: a 7-day (weekly) cycle from weekday/weekend shopping habits, and a 365-day (annual) cycle from holidays. Real-world series often have multiple, nested periods, and part of the analyst's job is disentangling which period matters for the question being asked.
