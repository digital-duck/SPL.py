### point_estimate (16.9s)

## Point Estimate

You already have the right instinct: when you don't know something exactly, you use the data you *do* have to make a best guess. A **point estimate** turns that guess into a single number — one specific value that stands in for an unknown population quantity.

The unknown quantity is called a **parameter**. Parameters describe the whole population: the true average height of every 15-year-old in the country, or the true fraction of voters who support a candidate. You can't measure everyone, so you take a sample and compute a **statistic** from it. That statistic *is* the point estimate.

The two most common point estimates are:

- **Sample mean** $\bar{x}$, used to estimate the population mean $\mu$:
$$\bar{x} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n}$$
- **Sample proportion** $\hat{p}$, used to estimate the true proportion $p$:
$$\hat{p} = \frac{\text{number of successes}}{n}$$

The hat symbol $\hat{\phantom{p}}$ is the universal signal that a value is an *estimate*, not the true parameter.

The key insight is that a point estimate is precise but uncertain — it gives one clean answer, but a different sample would give a slightly different number. That's not a flaw; it's honest math. Later you'll learn how to attach an *interval* around the estimate to show how uncertain you are.

### null_hypothesis (13.8s)

## Null Hypothesis

You have probably already sensed the core idea: when scientists run an experiment, they start by assuming *nothing interesting is happening* — no effect, no difference, no relationship. That assumption has a name: the **null hypothesis**, written $H_0$.

### The Structure

The null hypothesis is always a claim of equality or no change. If you want to test whether a new study app improves test scores, you write:

$$H_0: \mu_{\text{app}} = \mu_{\text{no app}}$$

This says the average score with the app equals the average score without it. The **alternative hypothesis** $H_1$ (or $H_a$) is what you are actually trying to show:

$$H_1: \mu_{\text{app}} > \mu_{\text{no app}}$$

You do not try to prove $H_1$ directly. Instead, you collect data and ask: *how likely is this data if $H_0$ were true?* If the answer is "very unlikely," you **reject** $H_0$. If the data are consistent with $H_0$, you **fail to reject** it — notice statisticians never say "prove $H_0$ is true," because absence of evidence is not the same as evidence of absence.

### The Pattern

| Decision | $H_0$ actually true | $H_0$ actually false |
|---|---|---|
| Reject $H_0$ | Error (false alarm) | Correct |
| Fail to reject $H_0$ | Correct | Missed effect |

The threshold for rejection is usually a **p-value** below $0.05$ — meaning the data would occur less than $5\%$ of the time by pure chance.

### alternative_hypothesis (11.9s)

## Alternative Hypothesis

You already know that a hypothesis is an educated guess — a statement you test with data. But in statistics, hypotheses come in pairs. The **null hypothesis** ($H_0$) is the boring, default claim: "nothing special is happening here." The **alternative hypothesis** ($H_1$ or $H_a$) is the interesting one — the claim you actually *want* to investigate.

Think of it like a courtroom. The null hypothesis is "innocent until proven guilty." The alternative hypothesis is the prosecutor's claim: "something is going on." Your job with data is to decide whether the evidence is strong enough to reject the default.

Here's the structure. Every statistical test sets up this pair:

$$H_0: \text{no effect (or no difference)}$$
$$H_1: \text{there is an effect (or a difference)}$$

For example, suppose a new study drug is being tested:

$$H_0: \mu_{\text{drug}} = \mu_{\text{placebo}}$$
$$H_1: \mu_{\text{drug}} \neq \mu_{\text{placebo}}$$

This is called a **two-sided** alternative — you're open to the drug being either better *or* worse. If you had a specific direction in mind (the drug only helps, never harms), you'd write a **one-sided** alternative: $H_1: \mu_{\text{drug}} > \mu_{\text{placebo}}$.

The key rule: you never "prove" $H_1$ directly. You only collect enough evidence to *reject* $H_0$. The alternative hypothesis is the conclusion you reach by elimination, not by direct proof.

### critical_value (14.8s)

## Critical Value

You already have a sense that some outcomes are "too extreme to be coincidence." A critical value is the precise number on a distribution where you draw that line — the exact boundary between "plausible by chance" and "statistically surprising."

**Structure of the idea.** Imagine the standard normal distribution, that symmetric bell curve centered at zero. Most outcomes cluster near 0; outcomes far in the tails are rare. When you choose a significance level $\alpha$ (say, $\alpha = 0.05$, meaning you accept a 5% chance of a false alarm), you are carving off that percentage from one or both tails. The critical value $z^*$ is the $z$-score at exactly that cutoff. For a two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$, the cutoffs land at $z^* = \pm 1.96$, because the two tails together contain exactly 5% of the area under the curve.

**The decision rule.** You compute a test statistic $z$ from your data. Then:

$$|z| > z^* \implies \text{reject } H_0$$
$$|z| \leq z^* \implies \text{fail to reject } H_0$$

Everything hinges on whether your result clears that bar. The critical value does not change with your data — it depends only on your chosen $\alpha$ and which distribution you use.

**Simple rule.** A smaller $\alpha$ pushes $z^*$ further into the tail, making it harder to reject $H_0$. Stricter standard, higher bar.

### level_of_significance (14.3s)

## Level Of Significance

You have probably heard the phrase "statistically significant" thrown around in science news. But what actually makes a result *significant*? That threshold has a name: the **level of significance**, written as $\alpha$ (alpha).

**The core idea.** When you run a hypothesis test, you are asking: "Could this result have happened by pure chance?" You calculate a **p-value** — the probability of seeing data at least as extreme as yours, *assuming the null hypothesis is true*. The level of significance is the cut-off you set in advance. If the p-value falls below $\alpha$, the result is considered statistically significant and you reject the null hypothesis.

$$\text{Reject } H_0 \quad \text{if} \quad p\text{-value} < \alpha$$

**Choosing $\alpha$.** The most common choices are $\alpha = 0.05$ (5%) and $\alpha = 0.01$ (1%). A smaller $\alpha$ demands stronger evidence before you reject $H_0$. Think of it like a courtroom: setting $\alpha = 0.01$ is like requiring a very high standard of proof, while $\alpha = 0.10$ lets you convict on weaker evidence — you will be right more often on real effects, but you will also make more false accusations.

**The key pattern.** $\alpha$ is always chosen *before* looking at the data. Picking it afterward — once you already know the p-value — is like moving the goal post after the penalty kick. Scientists set $\alpha$ first to keep the test honest.

---

### scatterplot (18.0s)

## Scatterplot

You already know that two things can be related — taller people often have longer legs, more study hours often lead to better scores. A scatterplot is the tool that lets you *see* that relationship by plotting two numerical variables at once, one per axis.

**Structure of a scatterplot.** Each data point is an ordered pair $(x, y)$, where $x$ is called the **explanatory variable** (the one you think might cause or predict the other) and $y$ is the **response variable** (the outcome you're watching). Plot enough of these pairs and a shape starts to emerge from the cloud of dots.

That shape tells a story:

- **Positive association** — as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to increase. The cloud tilts up-right.
- **Negative association** — as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to decrease. The cloud tilts down-right.
- **No association** — the dots look like scattered noise with no tilt.

You can also describe **strength** (how tightly the dots hug a line) and **form** (linear vs. curved). A tight, straight upward cloud signals a strong positive linear relationship; a wide, formless blob signals weak or no relationship.

One important rule: a pattern in a scatterplot shows **association, not causation**. Two variables can move together for many reasons — including a hidden third variable driving both.

### test_statistic (13.3s)

## Test Statistic

You already have an intuition that a single number can summarize an experiment — like averaging your quiz scores to get one grade. A **test statistic** does exactly that for a hypothesis test: it collapses all your data into one number that you can compare against what randomness alone would produce.

### The Structure

Suppose you flip a coin 50 times and get 31 heads. Is the coin fair? Your intuition says "probably, but maybe not." A test statistic gives that gut feeling a precise form.

For a coin-flip test, a common test statistic is:

$$z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}}$$

Here $\hat{p}$ is your observed proportion (heads), $p_0$ is the proportion you'd expect under the null hypothesis (0.5 for a fair coin), and $n$ is the number of trials.

With 31 heads out of 50:

$$z = \frac{0.62 - 0.50}{\sqrt{\dfrac{0.5 \times 0.5}{50}}} = \frac{0.12}{0.0707} \approx 1.70$$

### The Pattern

The test statistic always has the same skeleton:

$$\text{test statistic} = \frac{\text{observed} - \text{expected}}{\text{typical variation}}$$

A value near 0 means your data look ordinary. A large value (positive or negative) means your data would be surprising under the null hypothesis.

### correlation_coefficient (14.2s)

## Correlation Coefficient

You already know that scatter plots can show a relationship between two variables — points that slope upward suggest that as one value grows, so does the other. But "it looks like they're related" isn't precise enough for math. The **correlation coefficient** gives that relationship a number.

The most common version is called **Pearson's $r$**, and it always lands between $-1$ and $1$:

$$r \in [-1,\; 1]$$

Here is how to read it:

| Value of $r$ | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Close to $1$ | Strong positive relationship (both go up together) |
| Close to $-1$ | Strong negative relationship (one goes up, other goes down) |
| Near $0$ | Little to no linear relationship |

The formula behind $r$ compares how much each variable wanders from its own mean. For $n$ data pairs $(x_i, y_i)$:

$$r = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2}\;\cdot\;\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}$$

The numerator asks: when $x$ is above its mean, is $y$ also above its mean? If yes consistently, the product is positive and $r$ climbs toward $1$.

**Key rule:** $r$ measures *linear* association only. A perfectly curved relationship can still give $r \approx 0$, so always look at the scatter plot too.

### f_distribution (21.7s)

## F Distribution

You already know that variance measures how spread out a dataset is. But what happens when you want to *compare* the spreads of two different groups — say, test scores from two different classrooms? The F distribution is the tool built for exactly that question.

**The structure behind the ratio**

The F statistic is a ratio of two variances:

$$F = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}$$

where $s_1^2$ and $s_2^2$ are the sample variances of two groups. When both groups are drawn from populations with *equal* variance, this ratio tends to hover near 1. If one group is far more spread out than the other, $F$ climbs well above 1.

Formally, the F distribution arises as the ratio of two scaled chi-squared variables:

$$F = \frac{\chi^2_{d_1}/d_1}{\chi^2_{d_2}/d_2}$$

It is controlled by two parameters: $d_1$ (degrees of freedom in the numerator) and $d_2$ (denominator). Both must be positive, so $F \geq 0$ always — and the distribution is right-skewed, with a long tail to the right.

**A useful pattern**

As both $d_1$ and $d_2$ grow large, the F distribution flattens and becomes more symmetric, approaching a bell shape. With small degrees of freedom, the curve peaks near 1 but has a heavy right tail.

### hypothesis_testing (13.4s)

## Hypothesis Testing

You already know the spirit of hypothesis testing: make a claim, collect data, see if the evidence supports it. But scientists need something more precise than "it seems like it worked." They need a rule for deciding when data is *surprising enough* to overturn a default assumption.

**The setup.** Every hypothesis test starts with two competing statements. The **null hypothesis** $H_0$ is the "nothing special is happening" baseline — for example, *the coin is fair*. The **alternative hypothesis** $H_a$ is what you suspect is true instead — *the coin is biased toward heads*.

You then collect data and ask: *if $H_0$ were true, how likely is this result?* That probability is called the **$p$-value**. A small $p$-value means your data would be very rare under the null hypothesis — so rare that it's more reasonable to reject $H_0$.

**The decision rule.** Researchers set a threshold called the **significance level**, usually $\alpha = 0.05$. The rule is:

$$\text{if } p \leq \alpha, \text{ reject } H_0; \quad \text{if } p > \alpha, \text{ fail to reject } H_0.$$

"Fail to reject" is not the same as "prove $H_0$ is true" — absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. You're just saying the data didn't surprise you enough.

**Simple pattern to remember:** small $p$ → surprising data → reject the default.

### margin_of_error (11.5s)

## Margin Of Error

**Recap:** You already know that surveys and polls can't ask everyone — they ask a sample and use it to estimate the whole population. But no sample is perfect, so any estimate carries a little uncertainty. The *margin of error* is how we put a number on that uncertainty.

**The actual structure:** When a poll reports "48% support the new policy, with a margin of error of ±3%," it means the true percentage is likely somewhere between 45% and 51%. Formally, the result is written as:

$$\hat{p} \pm E$$

where $\hat{p}$ (read "p-hat") is the sample proportion and $E$ is the margin of error.

Where does $E$ come from? The most common formula for a 95% confidence level is:

$$E = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$$

where $n$ is the sample size. This is a useful rule of thumb — not the exact formula statisticians use, but it captures the key pattern: **a bigger sample gives a smaller margin of error.**

**The pattern to remember:** Doubling your sample size doesn't cut the margin of error in half — it cuts it by a factor of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. To cut $E$ in half, you need *four times* as many people. Shrinking uncertainty is expensive.

### anova (15.1s)

## Anova

You have probably compared two groups before — say, testing whether one study method produces higher quiz scores than another. But what if you have three groups, or five? Running separate comparisons for every pair is messy and quietly inflates your chances of finding a "difference" by accident. **ANOVA** (Analysis of Variance) solves this by asking a single unified question: *are all these group means really the same, or does at least one group stand apart?*

### The Core Idea

ANOVA splits the total variation in your data into two pieces:

- **Variation between groups** — how far each group's mean sits from the overall mean
- **Variation within groups** — how spread out the scores are inside each group

It then compares these two pieces using a ratio called the **F-statistic**:

$$F = \frac{\text{variance between groups}}{\text{variance within groups}}$$

If the group means are truly all equal, both numerator and denominator measure similar random noise, so $F \approx 1$. A large $F$ value signals that the between-group spread is too big to blame on chance alone — at least one group is genuinely different.

### The Key Rule

A larger $F$ is stronger evidence against "all groups are equal." You compare your $F$ against a critical value from an F-table (which depends on the number of groups and total sample size). If $F$ exceeds that threshold, you conclude that group membership *does* matter.

Note: ANOVA only tells you *that* a difference exists — a follow-up test (like Tukey's) identifies *which* groups differ.

### confidence_interval (13.2s)

## Confidence Interval

You already know that a sample mean is just an *estimate* — it lands somewhere near the true population mean, but not exactly on it. A confidence interval takes that idea one step further: instead of reporting a single number, you report a *range* that you're reasonably sure contains the true value.

**The structure.** Suppose you measure the heights of 30 students and find a sample mean $\bar{x} = 162\ \text{cm}$ with a standard error $SE = 2\ \text{cm}$. A 95% confidence interval is built by stepping out roughly 2 standard errors in each direction:

$$\bar{x} \pm 2 \cdot SE = 162 \pm 4 = [158,\ 166]\ \text{cm}$$

The multiplier 2 comes from the fact that, for a normal distribution, about 95% of values fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean. More precisely the multiplier is $z^* \approx 1.96$, but 2 is a perfectly good approximation at this level.

**The key idea.** A 95% confidence interval does *not* mean "there is a 95% chance the true mean is inside this interval." The true mean is fixed — it's either in there or it isn't. What it means is: if you repeated this sampling process many times, about 95% of the intervals you built would capture the true mean.

**Simple rule.** Wider interval → more confident but less precise. Narrower interval → more precise but riskier. Larger sample size shrinks $SE$, which shrinks the interval without sacrificing confidence.

---

### linear_regression (13.6s)

## Linear Regression

You already have the intuition: if you plot data points that roughly follow a line, you can draw a "best-fit" line through them to make predictions. Now let's look at what makes one line better than another.

**The structure behind the fit.**
Every straight line can be written as

$$\hat{y} = mx + b$$

where $m$ is the slope and $b$ is the y-intercept. For any data point $(x_i, y_i)$, the line predicts $\hat{y}_i$, but the actual value is $y_i$. The gap between them is called a **residual**:

$$e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$$

Some residuals are positive (the line guessed too low), some negative (too high). To find the *best* line, we want all residuals to be as small as possible at once. Linear regression does this by minimizing the **sum of squared residuals**:

$$\text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

Squaring ensures negatives don't cancel positives, and punishes large errors more heavily than small ones.

**The pattern to remember.**
The best-fit line always passes through the point $(\bar{x},\, \bar{y})$ — the mean of all $x$-values and the mean of all $y$-values. If your line misses that center point, it's not the best fit.

### python_statistical_computing (23.4s)

## Python Statistical Computing

You've probably calculated a mean or median by hand — now Python lets you apply those same ideas to thousands of data points in milliseconds. The key shift is thinking of data as a *collection* you transform, not a list of numbers you process one at a time.

**From formula to code**

Recall the mean of $n$ values:

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$$

In plain Python that is `sum(data) / len(data)`. The library NumPy brings you closer to the math:

```python
import numpy as np
data = np.array([4, 7, 13, 2, 1])
print(np.mean(data))    # 5.4
print(np.median(data))  # 4.0
```

NumPy arrays look like lists, but every operation works element-by-element across the whole collection at once — making them much faster on real datasets.

**The three-anchor summary**

For any dataset, three numbers tell most of the story:

| Statistic | Symbol | NumPy call |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | $\bar{x}$ | `np.mean(data)` |
| Median | $\tilde{x}$ | `np.median(data)` |
| Std. deviation | $\sigma$ | `np.std(data)` |

Standard deviation measures spread: $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}$. A large $\sigma$ means the values are far from the center.

**The pattern to remember**

When $\bar{x}$ and $\tilde{x}$ differ noticeably, your data is *skewed* — a few extreme values are pulling the mean away from the middle. The median is the more honest center in that case.
