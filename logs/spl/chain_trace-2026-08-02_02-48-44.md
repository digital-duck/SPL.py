### point_estimate (30.4s)

## Point Estimate

**Definition.** Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F}, \{P_\theta\}_{\theta \in \Theta})$ be a statistical model parameterized by $\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$. A *point estimator* of $\theta$ (or of a functional $g(\theta)$) is a measurable function $\hat{\theta} : \mathcal{X}^n \to \Theta$ mapping an observed sample $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ to a single element of the parameter space. The estimator is distinct from the *estimate*, which is the realized value $\hat{\theta}(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ for a specific data realization. This distinction is not merely pedantic: all finite-sample and asymptotic guarantees are properties of the *random variable* $\hat{\theta}(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$, not of any single observed number.

**Optimality and the Cramér–Rao Lower Bound.** The central question in point estimation is not merely *what* to estimate, but *how well* any estimator can perform. Suppose $P_\theta$ admits a density $p(x;\theta)$ satisfying standard regularity conditions (differentiability of the log-likelihood, dominated convergence permitting interchange of derivative and integral). Define the *Fisher information*:
$$\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p(X;\theta)\right)^2\right] = -\mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \log p(X;\theta)\right].$$

**Theorem (Cramér–Rao, 1945–1946).** For any unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta$, the variance satisfies
$$\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\hat{\theta}) \geq \frac{1}{n\,\mathcal{I}(\theta)}.$$

**Proof sketch.** Apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the covariance $\operatorname{Cov}_\theta(\hat{\theta}, \partial_\theta \log p(X;\theta))$, use the unbiasedness condition $\partial_\theta \mathbb{E}_\theta[\hat{\theta}] = 1$, and identify the Fisher information as the variance of the score. The full proof appears in Lehmann and Casella (1998, §2.5).

An unbiased estimator achieving this bound uniformly in $\theta$ is called a *uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator* (UMVUE). The Rao–Blackwell theorem provides a constructive route: conditioning any unbiased estimator on a *sufficient statistic* $T(X)$ — defined by the factorization $p(x;\theta) = g(T(x),\theta)\,h(x)$ — yields an estimator with variance no greater than the original. Combined with Lehmann–Scheffé's theorem (completeness of $T$ implies uniqueness of the UMVUE), this gives a complete theory for the exponential family.

**What is known and what remains open.** For exponential families, the MLE coincides with the UMVUE when a complete sufficient statistic exists, and asymptotic efficiency is characterized by Le Cam's local asymptotic normality (LAN) framework. Beyond exponential families, the picture is substantially more involved: the Cramér–Rao bound may be unachievable (e.g., in curved exponential families), the MLE can be inadmissible under squared-error loss (Stein's phenomenon for $d \geq 3$), and minimax-optimal estimators (James–Stein, ridge, LASSO in regression contexts) can dominate. In semiparametric and nonparametric models, efficiency theory requires Hilbert-space arguments (Bickel et al., 1993). Modern work on high-dimensional and robust estimation continues to revisit these foundations.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the James–Stein domination result numerically: generate $X \sim N(\theta, I_d)$ for $d = 1, 2, 5, 10, 50$ and compare the MSE of the MLE $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = X$ against the James–Stein estimator $\hat{\theta}_{\text{JS}} = (1 - (d-2)/\|X\|^2)\,X$. Verify that domination fails for $d \leq 2$ and holds uniformly in $\theta$ for $d \geq 3$. Extend by examining the positive-part James–Stein estimator and plotting the risk function as a function of $\|\theta\|^2$. This is a self-contained weekend computation that reveals the geometry of inadmissibility far more viscerally than any theorem statement alone.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) the formal definition of admissibility and the precise sense in which MLE is dominated; (2) the derivation or careful citation of the James–Stein risk formula; (3) empirical risk curves with confidence bands from simulation; (4) a discussion of what the result implies for practice — when shrinkage toward a chosen point meaningfully reduces expected loss, and when the choice of shrinkage target matters.

### null_hypothesis (28.2s)

## Null Hypothesis

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P_\theta)$ be a statistical model indexed by $\theta \in \Theta$. A *null hypothesis* is a measurable restriction $H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0 \subsetneq \Theta$, which partitions the parameter space so that $\Theta = \Theta_0 \cup \Theta_1$ with $\Theta_0 \cap \Theta_1 = \emptyset$. A test is a measurable function $\phi : \mathcal{X}^n \to \{0,1\}$; we reject $H_0$ when $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = 1$. The *size* of $\phi$ is $\alpha = \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} \mathbb{E}_\theta[\phi(\mathbf{X})]$, and its *power* against a specific $\theta_1 \in \Theta_1$ is $\beta(\theta_1) = \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\phi(\mathbf{X})]$.

**Theorem (Neyman–Pearson Lemma).** Let $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ and $H_1 : \theta = \theta_1$ be simple hypotheses with densities $f_0, f_1$ with respect to a dominating measure $\mu$. Define the likelihood ratio $\Lambda(\mathbf{x}) = f_1(\mathbf{x}) / f_0(\mathbf{x})$. For a fixed level $\alpha \in (0,1)$, the most powerful test rejects $H_0$ whenever $\Lambda(\mathbf{x}) > k_\alpha$, where $k_\alpha$ is the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of $\Lambda$ under $P_{\theta_0}$. Formally, no other size-$\alpha$ test $\tilde\phi$ can satisfy $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\tilde\phi] > \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\phi^*]$.

*Proof sketch.* For any competing test $\tilde\phi$ of size $\leq \alpha$:
$$\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\phi^* - \tilde\phi] = \int (\phi^* - \tilde\phi)(f_1 - k_\alpha f_0)\,d\mu + k_\alpha\underbrace{\int(\phi^* - \tilde\phi)f_0\,d\mu}_{\geq 0}.$$
The integrand of the first term is $\geq 0$ pointwise by construction of the rejection region, so the whole expression is non-negative. $\square$

**Literature context.** The Neyman–Pearson framework, introduced in their 1933 *Philosophical Transactions* paper, remains the foundational architecture for frequentist inference. Wald (1950) extended it to sequential and decision-theoretic settings; Lehmann and Romano (2005, *Testing Statistical Hypotheses*) provide the canonical modern treatment. Composite nulls ($|\Theta_0| > 1$) are substantially harder: uniformly most powerful (UMP) tests exist only under special structure (e.g., one-parameter exponential families with monotone likelihood ratios). In high dimensions, recent work by Candès et al. (2018) on model-X knockoffs and Bates et al. (2023) on e-values revisits what "valid" null rejection means when $n \ll p$ or the model is misspecified. A live open question is how to maintain valid Type-I error control in adaptive or data-dependent hypothesis selection pipelines—the multiple-testing problem as encountered in modern ML model selection.

**Suggested investigation.** Simulate a one-sample $z$-test under varying degrees of model misspecification: generate data from a heavy-tailed distribution (e.g., $t_3$) while testing $H_0 : \mu = 0$ with the Gaussian likelihood ratio. Plot realized size (empirical rejection rate under $H_0$) as a function of $n$ and tail index. Then implement the same experiment using a permutation test; verify that the permutation test maintains exact size regardless of the generating distribution. Extend the comparison to composite $H_0$ (testing $\mu \leq 0$) and assess power curves numerically, checking whether the UMP test from Lehmann–Romano §3.4 is recovered.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should formally state the null and alternative as parameter-space subsets, specify the test statistic and its null distribution (exact or asymptotic), report both the empirical size at the nominal $\alpha$ and the power curve over a grid of $\theta_1$ values, and conclude with a discussion of which regularity conditions were violated in the misspecified setting and how the permutation test circumvents them. Figures should include overlaid power curves for parametric versus permutation tests, annotated with the theoretically predicted power derived from the Neyman–Pearson envelope.

### alternative_hypothesis (28.0s)

## Alternative Hypothesis

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P_\theta)$ be a statistical model indexed by parameter $\theta \in \Theta$. A hypothesis is a restriction on $\theta$; a *null hypothesis* $H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0$ is the claim to be falsified, while the *alternative hypothesis* $H_1 : \theta \in \Theta_1 = \Theta \setminus \Theta_0$ is the complement that a test seeks evidence for. The alternative is not merely the logical negation of $H_0$: it encodes the *direction* of departure the investigator considers scientifically meaningful, and this choice structurally constrains every downstream operating characteristic of the test.

The alternative may be *simple* ($|\Theta_1| = 1$), *composite* (a proper subset of $\Theta$), *one-sided* ($\theta > \theta_0$), or *two-sided* ($\theta \neq \theta_0$). These are not equivalent, and conflating them is a persistent source of inferential error in applied work.

**Theorem (Neyman–Pearson Lemma).** Suppose $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ and $H_1 : \theta = \theta_1$ are both simple hypotheses and observations $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are i.i.d. with densities $f_{\theta_0}$ and $f_{\theta_1}$, respectively. Among all tests of size at most $\alpha$, the likelihood ratio test

$$\phi^*(x) = \mathbf{1}\!\left\{\frac{\prod_{i=1}^n f_{\theta_1}(x_i)}{\prod_{i=1}^n f_{\theta_0}(x_i)} > k_\alpha\right\}$$

is uniformly most powerful (UMP): it maximises power $\beta(\theta_1) = E_{\theta_1}[\phi^*(X)]$ subject to $E_{\theta_0}[\phi^*(X)] \leq \alpha$.

*Proof sketch.* The Neyman–Pearson fundamental lemma establishes that any test $\phi$ with $E_{\theta_0}[\phi] \leq \alpha$ satisfies $E_{\theta_1}[\phi^* - \phi](f_{\theta_1} - k_\alpha f_{\theta_0}) \geq 0$, which immediately yields $E_{\theta_1}[\phi^*] \geq E_{\theta_1}[\phi]$. See Lehmann and Romano (2005, Ch. 3) for the complete argument including the randomised boundary case. $\square$

**Literature context.** The Neyman–Pearson framework is foundational but exhibits a fundamental asymmetry: the alternative enters only through the likelihood ratio, so the test is constructed *around* the null. This asymmetry becomes consequential when $\Theta_1$ is composite: UMP tests generally do not exist, and one must appeal to uniformly most powerful unbiased (UMPU) tests, invariance arguments, or Bayesian alternatives (Berger, 1985). The choice between one-sided and two-sided alternatives is a research design decision with direct power implications—a one-sided test against $\theta > \theta_0$ achieves higher power there but has zero formal power against $\theta < \theta_0$. In high-dimensional settings ($p \gg n$), the classical theory breaks down further; minimax testing rates over composite alternatives become the operative criterion (Ingster and Suslina, 2003; Collier et al., 2017).

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a simulation study in which the true data-generating process lies at varying distances from $\Theta_0$ along a one-dimensional parameter path. Compute empirical power curves for a one-sided $z$-test, a two-sided $z$-test, and the Bayesian posterior probability $P(\theta > \theta_0 \mid X)$ as a function of effect size $\delta = (\theta_1 - \theta_0)/\sigma$ and sample size $n$. Vary the prior on $\theta$ (flat, weakly informative, spiked at $\theta_0$) and record how the alternative specification interacts with prior choice. Extend by replacing the Gaussian model with a heavy-tailed distribution and observe whether the Neyman–Pearson optimality is preserved empirically.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should (i) state the parameter space and both hypotheses with full mathematical notation before any simulation detail; (ii) report power as a function of $\delta$ and $n$ in a single well-labelled figure with confidence bands; (iii) articulate precisely what "choosing the alternative" implies for the experiment's inferential scope; and (iv) discuss one open question—for instance, whether the observed power ordering between frequentist and Bayesian procedures is robust to misspecification of the likelihood family.

### critical_value (105.9s)

## Critical Value

**Definition.** Let $T$ be a test statistic with sampling distribution $F_T(\,\cdot\,;\theta_0)$ under the null hypothesis $H_0:\theta=\theta_0$. For a pre-specified significance level $\alpha\in(0,1)$, the *critical value* $c_\alpha$ is defined as the quantile satisfying
$$P_{\theta_0}(T > c_\alpha) = \alpha,$$
for a one-sided upper-tail test, or more generally as the boundary of a rejection region $\mathcal{R}_\alpha$ such that $P_{\theta_0}(T\in\mathcal{R}_\alpha)\leq\alpha$. For two-sided tests, $\mathcal{R}_\alpha=\{\lvert T\rvert > c_{\alpha/2}\}$. The null hypothesis is rejected when the observed statistic falls beyond $c_\alpha$, partitioning the sample space into the rejection region and its complement.

**Theorem (Neyman–Pearson, 1933).** Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n\sim f(\,\cdot\,;\theta)$ with $\theta\in\{\theta_0,\theta_1\}$. Among all level-$\alpha$ tests of $H_0:\theta=\theta_0$ against $H_1:\theta=\theta_1$, the likelihood-ratio test with rejection region
$$\mathcal{R}^* = \left\{\Lambda(\mathbf{X}) = \frac{f(\mathbf{X};\theta_1)}{f(\mathbf{X};\theta_0)} > k_\alpha\right\},$$
where $k_\alpha$ satisfies $P_{\theta_0}(\Lambda > k_\alpha)=\alpha$, is uniformly most powerful.

*Proof sketch.* Let $\phi^*$ be the likelihood-ratio test and $\phi$ any competing level-$\alpha$ test. Since $\phi^*(\mathbf{x})=1$ whenever $\Lambda(\mathbf{x})>k_\alpha$ and $0$ otherwise,
$$\bigl(\phi^*(\mathbf{x})-\phi(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\bigl(f(\mathbf{x};\theta_1)-k_\alpha f(\mathbf{x};\theta_0)\bigr)\geq 0 \quad\text{a.e.}$$
Integrating and applying the level constraint $E_{\theta_0}[\phi]\leq\alpha$ yields $E_{\theta_1}[\phi^*]\geq E_{\theta_1}[\phi]$. $\square$

The critical value $k_\alpha$ here is not a table look-up but the solution to an integral equation that may have no closed form; finding or approximating it is already a nontrivial numerical task.

**Literature context.** The Neyman–Pearson framework gives critical values a decision-theoretic interpretation, but in practice $c_\alpha$ is almost always obtained asymptotically: by Slutsky's theorem, many statistics converge in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ or $\chi^2_k$ under $H_0$, so $c_\alpha=z_\alpha$ or $\chi^2_{k,\alpha}$ from standard tables. The open frontier concerns finite-sample validity. The *bootstrap critical value* $\hat{c}_\alpha^B$ is the one new primitive introduced here: one resamples $B$ datasets under the null (either by recentering draws around $\hat{\theta}_0$ or by exact null-resampling for permutation-invariant statistics), computes $T^{(b)}$ for each, and sets $\hat{c}_\alpha^B$ as the $(1-\alpha)$ empirical quantile of $\{T^{(1)},\ldots,T^{(B)}\}$. Hall (1992) and Efron and Tibshirani (1993) show this achieves higher-order accuracy via implicit Edgeworth correction, reducing size distortion by $O(n^{-1})$ relative to the normal critical value under i.i.d. sampling. The caveat is sharp: coverage guarantees degrade under heavy tails or dependence because the resampling scheme must correctly mimic the null distribution, and it does not when serial correlation invalidates the i.i.d. bootstrap. Extensions to block bootstrap and subsampling (Politis, Romano & Wolf 1999; Lahiri 2003) address the dependent-data case but introduce new tuning choices. The composite-null extension — requiring $\sup_{\theta\in H_0}P_\theta(T\in\mathcal{R}_\alpha)\leq\alpha$ — introduces further difficulty addressed by Lehmann and Romano (2005), and distribution-free alternatives (Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer 2005) recasting $c_\alpha$ as a nonconformity quantile are active research directions beyond the scope of this entry.

**Suggested investigation.** Simulate data from three generative models: $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ (baseline), $t_3$ (heavy tails), and $\mathrm{AR}(1)$ with $\rho=0.5$ (dependence). For each setting and $n\in\{20,50,200\}$, compute the empirical null-rejection rate at $\alpha=0.05$ under three critical-value schemes: (1) the asymptotic normal value $z_\alpha$; (2) the bootstrap critical value $\hat{c}_\alpha^B$ obtained by resampling under the null; (3) a permutation critical value $\hat{c}_\alpha^P$. Then sweep a local-alternatives sequence $\theta_1=\delta/\sqrt{n}$ for $\delta\in[0,4]$ to produce empirical power curves, comparing against the Neyman–Pearson power envelope where computable. Expected pattern: the asymptotic critical value over-rejects under $t_3$ at small $n$; the bootstrap corrects this for i.i.d. data but degrades under $\mathrm{AR}(1)$; the permutation test holds exact level under exchangeability but loses power when exchangeability fails.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should establish: (i) null rejection rates as a joint function of $(n, \alpha, \text{DGP})$ with Monte Carlo standard errors; (ii) the power envelope comparison with explicit statement of which optimality conditions hold in each setting; (iii) a theoretical argument — at minimum via first-order Edgeworth expansion — explaining why $\hat{c}_\alpha^B$ reduces size distortion relative to $z_\alpha$; and (iv) a discussion of when the observed size distortions are practically consequential. Every assumption underlying each method must be stated explicitly and cross-referenced against each simulation setting.

### level_of_significance (29.1s)

## Level Of Significance

**Definition.** Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F}, \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\})$ be a statistical model with null hypothesis $H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0 \subseteq \Theta$. A test is a measurable function $\phi : \mathcal{X} \to [0,1]$, where $\phi(x)$ denotes the probability of rejecting $H_0$ upon observing $x$. The *size* of the test is $\sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} \mathbb{E}_\theta[\phi(X)]$, and the test is said to have *level of significance* $\alpha \in (0,1)$ if its size does not exceed $\alpha$. In the classical Neyman–Pearson framework, $\alpha$ is fixed prior to data collection and represents the maximum tolerable probability of a Type I error — rejecting $H_0$ when it is true.

**Theorem (Neyman–Pearson Lemma).** Suppose $\Theta_0 = \{\theta_0\}$ and $\Theta_1 = \{\theta_1\}$ are simple hypotheses with densities $p_0, p_1$ with respect to a dominating measure $\mu$. For a fixed $\alpha \in (0,1)$, the most powerful level-$\alpha$ test takes the form

$$\phi^*(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \Lambda(x) > k, \\ \gamma & \text{if } \Lambda(x) = k, \\ 0 & \text{if } \Lambda(x) < k, \end{cases}$$

where $\Lambda(x) = p_1(x)/p_0(x)$ is the likelihood ratio, and the constants $k \geq 0$, $\gamma \in [0,1]$ are chosen so that $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[\phi^*(X)] = \alpha$ exactly. The proof proceeds by showing that any competing test $\phi$ with $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[\phi(X)] \leq \alpha$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\phi^*(X)] \geq \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\phi(X)]$ via the inequality $(\phi^* - \phi)(p_1 - k p_0) \geq 0$ $\mu$-almost everywhere.

**Literature context.** The conventional choice $\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.05\}$ has no theoretical justification beyond historical convention traceable to Fisher (1925) and is the subject of ongoing debate. Benjamin et al. (2018) proposed redefining significance at $\alpha = 0.005$ to address replication failures, while Lakens et al. (2018) countered that the threshold should be calibrated per-domain. The relationship between $\alpha$ and false discovery rate (FDR) control — studied by Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) in the multiple-testing setting — reveals that naïve application of a fixed $\alpha$ to a family of $m$ simultaneous tests inflates the familywise error rate to $1 - (1-\alpha)^m$. In high-dimensional regimes (genomics, neuroimaging), $m$ can reach $10^5$–$10^6$, rendering per-test significance levels nearly meaningless without correction. Open questions persist around optimal $\alpha$ selection under composite nulls, adaptive procedures that tune $\alpha$ to observed data without invalidating Type I error guarantees, and the reconciliation of significance with Bayesian evidence thresholds (Sellke, Bayarri, and Berger, 2001).

**Suggested investigation.** A tractable graduate project is to replicate the power–size tradeoff curve for the likelihood ratio test under a specific parametric family (e.g., $\mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)$ with $H_0 : \mu = 0$) as $\alpha$ varies from $10^{-4}$ to $0.10$, then extend to the two-sample $t$-test with unequal variances. Compute empirical Type I and Type II error rates via Monte Carlo simulation at each $\alpha$ level, and overlay the theoretical Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve $(\alpha, \beta(\alpha))$ where $\beta(\alpha) = 1 - \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\phi^*(X)]$ is the Type II error. A natural extension is to examine Bonferroni-corrected $\alpha' = \alpha/m$ versus BH-corrected thresholds across a grid of $m$ simultaneous tests, measuring empirical FDR and power at each correction scheme.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should clearly distinguish size from level (a test can have level $\alpha$ but size strictly less than $\alpha$, a distinction that matters for discrete distributions), state the simulation parameters and random seed for reproducibility, and provide a figure showing the empirical versus theoretical power function. The discussion should address whether the chosen $\alpha$ is appropriate given the cost asymmetry between Type I and Type II errors in the application domain — this cost analysis, often omitted, is what transforms a rote significance report into a scientifically defensible inference.

### scatterplot (29.2s)

## Scatterplot

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ be a bivariate sample drawn from a joint distribution $P_{XY}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$. The *scatterplot* of $\mathcal{D}$ is the point set $S(\mathcal{D}) = \{(x_i, y_i) : i = 1, \ldots, n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, rendered as a map $\phi: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2_{\text{display}}$ that preserves relative order in each coordinate margin. This definition extends naturally to $p$-dimensional data via the *scatterplot matrix* (SPLOM), which tiles the $\binom{p}{2}$ bivariate projections, and to three or more variables by encoding additional dimensions as aesthetic channels (color, size, shape).

**Theorem (Perception–Accuracy Correspondence, Cleveland & McGill 1984).** Among elementary graphical tasks — position along a common scale, position along non-aligned scales, length, angle, area, volume, color saturation — position along a common scale achieves the lowest mean absolute judgment error. Because the scatterplot encodes both $x$ and $y$ as Cartesian positions, it is, among the standard two-variable displays, the most accurate channel for joint value estimation.

*Proof sketch.* Cleveland and McGill formalized the task as: given two marked magnitudes on a shared or separated axis, estimate the ratio $r = a/b$. Fitting a power-law model $\log |\hat{r} - r| = \alpha + \beta \log r$ across graphical primitives, they showed that the exponent $\beta$ is smallest for positional encodings. A tighter theoretical grounding comes from the signal-detection framework of Macmillan & Creelman (2004): the discriminability $d'$ for positional judgments exceeds that for area or angle under matched signal-to-noise conditions, because the visual system's position channel has higher spatial bandwidth.

**Literature context.** The scatterplot is the empirical substrate for the two foundational inferential tasks of bivariate analysis: (i) estimating the dependence structure $\rho = \text{Corr}(X, Y)$ or, more generally, a copula $C_{XY}$, and (ii) detecting departures from a posited regression model $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x] = f(x; \theta)$ via residual patterns. Anscombe's quartet (1973) remains the canonical demonstration that $\hat{\rho}$ is a statistically sufficient but perceptually insufficient summary: four datasets sharing identical means, variances, and Pearson correlation exhibit visually distinct scatterplots, motivating the principle that no single statistic substitutes for the display. More recently, the *datasaurus dozen* (Matejka & Fitzmaurice, 2017) extends this to 12 datasets with identical summary statistics but radically different visual configurations, constructed via simulated annealing on a target point cloud.

Open questions persist at the boundary of perception and inference. The relationship between human detection of non-linearity in scatterplots and formal statistical power against alternatives to linearity has not been fully characterized for small $n$. Work by Majumder et al. (2013) on the *lineup protocol* formalizes scatterplot reading as a hypothesis test — observers identify a target plot among $m - 1$ null plots — enabling $p$-value calibration for visual inference, but optimal $m$ and plot-size choices remain empirically underdetermined.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the Anscombe quartet experiment computationally: (1) generate four datasets with matched first- and second-order moments but distinct structural forms (linear, quadratic, outlier-contaminated, leverage-point); (2) fit $\hat{\rho}$, $\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}$, and a kernel density estimate of $P_{XY}$; (3) compare diagnostic power of the scatterplot against residual-vs-fitted plots and the Q–Q plot of residuals for detecting each departure. Extend by constructing a lineup ($m = 20$) and measuring human detection rates to estimate visual power.

**Report note.** A rigorous write-up should state the data-generating process and sample size, report all matched summary statistics to confirm the construction, display the four scatterplots at equal scale with identical axis limits, and interpret each visual pattern in terms of the corresponding regression diagnostic. The lineup experiment, if conducted, should report detection frequency, visual $p$-value, and confidence interval using the binomial model $k \sim \text{Binomial}(N, 1/m)$ under the null.

### test_statistic (50.9s)

## Test Statistic

**Definition.** Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F}, \{P_\theta\}_{\theta \in \Theta})$ be a statistical model. A *test statistic* is a measurable function $T : \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ whose sampling distribution under the null hypothesis $H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0$ is either known exactly or approximable to sufficient precision that critical values can be computed. The statistic summarizes the data into a scalar whose extremity, relative to the null distribution, quantifies evidence against $H_0$. Formally, the rejection region at level $\alpha$ is $\mathcal{R}_\alpha = \{x : T(x) > c_\alpha\}$, where $c_\alpha = \inf\{c : \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} P_\theta(T > c) \leq \alpha\}$.

**Theorem (Neyman–Pearson lemma).** Suppose $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ and $H_1 : \theta = \theta_1$ are both simple. Among all tests of level $\alpha$, the likelihood ratio statistic

$$\Lambda(x) = \frac{p_{\theta_1}(x)}{p_{\theta_0}(x)}$$

maximizes power $\beta(\theta_1) = P_{\theta_1}(T \in \mathcal{R})$. That is, no level-$\alpha$ test has strictly greater power against $\theta_1$ than the threshold rule $\phi^*(x) = \mathbf{1}[\Lambda(x) > k_\alpha]$.

*Proof sketch.* Let $\phi$ be any level-$\alpha$ test. Then $\int (\phi^* - \phi)(p_{\theta_1} - k_\alpha p_{\theta_0})\,d\mu \geq 0$ follows because $\phi^* - \phi$ and $p_{\theta_1} - k_\alpha p_{\theta_0}$ share the same sign by construction. Integrating and rearranging yields $E_{\theta_1}[\phi^*] \geq E_{\theta_1}[\phi]$. $\square$

The two-sample $t$-statistic, the score statistic, and chi-squared goodness-of-fit statistics all arise as locally most powerful or asymptotically optimal variants of this fundamental construction under regularity conditions (see Lehmann and Romano, *Testing Statistical Hypotheses*, 3rd ed., Ch. 3–5).

**Literature context.** The optimality theory for composite hypotheses remains substantially harder. Uniformly most powerful (UMP) tests exist only under special structure — monotone likelihood ratio families, one-sided alternatives — and fail in the general multiparameter case, motivating UMP unbiased (UMPU) and locally most powerful tests. Contemporary work extends the framework to high-dimensional settings ($p \gg n$): classical chi-squared approximations break down, and corrected statistics (Bai–Saranadasa, Chen–Qin) or selective inference procedures (Berk et al. 2013; Taylor and Tibshirani 2015) become necessary. An active frontier concerns valid post-selection inference, where the test statistic's null distribution must be conditioned on the selection event.

**Suggested investigation.** Construct the likelihood ratio statistic $\Lambda$ for testing $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ in a one-parameter exponential family — the Poisson family, $X_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Poisson}(\theta)$, is a natural choice. Derive the exact null distribution of $-2\log\Lambda$ and prove analytically that it converges to $\chi^2_1$ as $n \to \infty$, identifying the rate of the remainder term. Then simulate: for $n \in \{20, 50, 200\}$, draw $B = 10{,}000$ samples and compare empirical rejection rates at $\alpha = 0.05$ to the nominal level using both the exact null distribution and the $\chi^2_1$ approximation. Examine how quickly the $\chi^2_1$ approximation becomes adequate, and relate any observed discrepancy to the skewness of the Poisson distribution at small $\theta_0$.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should state the null and alternative precisely in parameter space, derive the likelihood ratio statistic from first principles, prove or cite the $\chi^2_1$ asymptotic result with explicit rate, and present empirical size tables for both the exact and approximate critical values, each accompanied by Monte Carlo standard errors. The write-up must specify which null distribution underlies each test decision — exact or asymptotic — and explain why the two procedures diverge at small $n$, connecting the observed finite-sample distortion to a computable correction term rather than dismissing it as numerical noise.

### correlation_coefficient (25.5s)

## Correlation Coefficient

**Definition.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and let $X, Y \in L^2(\Omega)$ be square-integrable random variables with $\operatorname{Var}(X) > 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) > 0$. The *Pearson correlation coefficient* is

$$\rho(X, Y) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\,\operatorname{Var}(Y)}} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y},$$

where $\mu_X = \mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\sigma_X = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$. The empirical counterpart for a sample $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ is

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}.$$

**Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz bound).** For any $X, Y \in L^2(\Omega)$, $-1 \leq \rho(X,Y) \leq 1$. Moreover, $|\rho(X,Y)| = 1$ if and only if there exist constants $a \neq 0$ and $b$ such that $Y = aX + b$ almost surely.

*Proof sketch.* Consider the quadratic form $q(t) = \operatorname{Var}(X + tY) \geq 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Expanding, $q(t) = \sigma_X^2 + 2t\,\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) + t^2\sigma_Y^2$. Non-negativity of this quadratic requires its discriminant to be non-positive: $4\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)^2 - 4\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2 \leq 0$, which gives $|\rho| \leq 1$. Equality $|\rho| = 1$ forces the discriminant to vanish, meaning $q(t^*) = 0$ for some $t^* = -\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/\sigma_Y^2$, hence $\operatorname{Var}(X + t^* Y) = 0$ and $X + t^* Y = \mu_X + t^* \mu_Y$ a.s. $\square$

**Literature context.** Pearson introduced $r$ in 1896 as a measure of linear association; Galton's earlier work on regression provided the geometric intuition. The coefficient is the cosine of the angle between centered vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, making it a projection onto the unit sphere of the Hilbert space $L^2_0(\Omega)$. Its sampling distribution under bivariate normality is classical: Fisher showed that $\tanh^{-1}(r)$ (the Fisher $z$-transformation) is approximately $\mathcal{N}\!\left(\tanh^{-1}(\rho),\, (n-3)^{-1}\right)$, enabling confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. What remains actively studied is the behavior of $r$ under non-normality (robustness questions), its relationship to copula-based dependence measures (Spearman's $\rho_S$, Kendall's $\tau$), and its limitations as a dependence measure — notably, $\rho = 0$ does not imply independence unless joint normality holds. In high-dimensional settings, sparse estimation of correlation matrices (e.g., via thresholding or graphical lasso) is an active research frontier (Bickel and Levina, 2008; Cai, Liu, and Luo, 2011).

**Suggested investigation.** Design a simulation study in which $(X, Y)$ are drawn from a bivariate distribution with $\rho = 0$ but genuine statistical dependence — e.g., $Y = X^2 + \varepsilon$ with $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $\varepsilon \perp X$. Compute $r$, Spearman's $\rho_S$, Kendall's $\tau$, and a kernel-based dependence measure (HSIC) across sample sizes $n \in \{50, 200, 1000\}$. Investigate the power of each measure's independence test under this specific alternative.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (i) an analytic proof that $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] \not\equiv \text{const}$ yet $\rho = 0$ for the chosen model, (ii) empirical power curves with bootstrap confidence bands, and (iii) a discussion connecting the result to the sufficient condition for $\rho = 0 \Rightarrow$ independence (joint Gaussianity) and what this implies for practical use of correlation in exploratory data analysis.

### f_distribution (27.6s)

## F Distribution

**Definition.** Let $U \sim \chi^2(d_1)$ and $V \sim \chi^2(d_2)$ be independent chi-squared random variables with $d_1$ and $d_2$ degrees of freedom, respectively. The random variable

$$F = \frac{U/d_1}{V/d_2}$$

is said to follow an **F distribution** with parameters $d_1$ and $d_2$, written $F \sim F(d_1, d_2)$. Its probability density function on $(0, \infty)$ is

$$f(x; d_1, d_2) = \frac{1}{B(d_1/2,\, d_2/2)} \left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)^{d_1/2} \frac{x^{d_1/2 - 1}}{\left(1 + \frac{d_1}{d_2}x\right)^{(d_1+d_2)/2}},$$

where $B(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the beta function. The mean exists for $d_2 > 2$ and equals $d_2/(d_2-2)$; the variance exists for $d_2 > 4$.

**Theorem (Density derivation via ratio transformation).** If $U$ and $V$ are independent with $U \sim \chi^2(d_1)$ and $V \sim \chi^2(d_2)$, then $F = (U/d_1)/(V/d_2)$ has the density above.

*Proof sketch.* Write the joint density of $(U, V)$ as a product of gamma densities with shape parameters $d_1/2$ and $d_2/2$. Apply the change of variables $F = (U/d_1)/(V/d_2)$, $W = V$, with Jacobian $\partial(U,V)/\partial(F,W) = d_1 W / d_2$. Integrate out $W$ using the identity $\int_0^\infty w^{a-1} e^{-w(1 + d_1 F/d_2)/2}\,dw = 2^a \Gamma(a)(1 + d_1 F/d_2)^{-a}$ for $a = (d_1+d_2)/2$. The remaining constant assembles into $B(d_1/2, d_2/2)^{-1}$ via the standard beta–gamma relation $B(a,b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$. $\square$

A related identity of practical importance: $F(d_1, d_2) \stackrel{d}{=} 1/F(d_2, d_1)$, which implies $F_{1-\alpha}(d_1, d_2) = 1/F_\alpha(d_2, d_1)$, reducing the number of tabled critical values required.

**Literature context.** The F distribution was introduced by R.A. Fisher (1924) and named in his honor by Snedecor (1934); Lehmann and Romano's *Testing Statistical Hypotheses* (3rd ed., 2005) provides a rigorous treatment of the optimality of F-tests under normality. What remains less settled is the behavior under model misspecification: the classical ANOVA F-test is known to be robust to mild non-normality (Box, 1953), yet the precise breakdown point under heavy tails or heteroscedasticity remains an active area—see Wilcox (2012, *Introduction to Robust Estimation and Hypothesis Testing*) for a practical account and Romano & Wolf (2005, *JASA*) for permutation-based corrections that recalibrate the test without assuming $F(d_1, d_2)$ null distributions.

**Suggested investigation.** A productive graduate project is a simulation study of F-test size and power under departure from normality. Generate samples from $t_\nu$, $\text{Laplace}$, and skewed distributions; apply the standard two-sample variance-ratio test and one-way ANOVA F-test; record empirical Type I error rates against nominal $\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.10\}$ across a grid of sample sizes $n \in \{10, 30, 100\}$. Compare against Bartlett's test and Levene's test as competitors. A natural extension is to implement Welch's correction and verify that the approximate $F(d_1', d_2')$ distribution with non-integer degrees of freedom—derived via the Welch–Satterthwaite equation—recovers nominal size where the classical test fails.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (i) a precise statement of the null and its operating assumptions, (ii) the derivation linking the ratio of sample variances to $F(n_1-1, n_2-1)$ under normality, (iii) simulation methodology with seeds and code, (iv) a table of empirical rejection rates for each distribution–$n$ combination, and (v) a conclusion quantifying the practical cost of non-normality and recommending a robust alternative under specified conditions. Theoretical error bounds via Berry–Esseen would strengthen the argument.

### hypothesis_testing (27.6s)

## Hypothesis Testing

**Definition.** Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F}, \{P_\theta\}_{\theta \in \Theta})$ be a statistical model. A *hypothesis* is a restriction on $\theta$: the *null hypothesis* $H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0$ and the *alternative* $H_1 : \theta \in \Theta_1$, where $\Theta_0 \cap \Theta_1 = \emptyset$. A *test* is a measurable function $\phi : \mathcal{X} \to [0,1]$, where $\phi(x)$ is the probability of rejecting $H_0$ upon observing $x$. The *size* of $\phi$ is $\alpha = \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} \mathbb{E}_\theta[\phi(X)]$ and its *power function* is $\beta_\phi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[\phi(X)]$ for $\theta \in \Theta_1$.

**Theorem (Neyman–Pearson, 1933).** Suppose $\Theta_0 = \{\theta_0\}$ and $\Theta_1 = \{\theta_1\}$ are both simple, with $P_{\theta_i}$ admitting densities $f_i$ with respect to a common $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu$. Fix $\alpha \in (0,1)$. Then the *likelihood ratio test*

$$\phi^*(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & f_1(x) > k\, f_0(x), \\ \gamma & f_1(x) = k\, f_0(x), \\ 0 & f_1(x) < k\, f_0(x), \end{cases}$$

is *most powerful* at level $\alpha$ for every $\alpha$-level test, where $k \geq 0$ and $\gamma \in [0,1]$ are chosen to satisfy $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[\phi^*(X)] = \alpha$.

*Proof sketch.* Let $\phi$ be any other level-$\alpha$ test. Then $(\phi^* - \phi)(f_1 - kf_0) \geq 0$ pointwise by construction. Integrating against $\mu$: $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\phi^*] - \mathbb{E}_{\theta_1}[\phi] \geq k(\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[\phi^*] - \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[\phi]) \geq 0$, since $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[\phi^*] = \alpha \geq \mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}[\phi]$. $\square$

**Literature context.** The Neyman–Pearson framework formalizes the trade-off between Type I error ($P_{\theta_0}(\text{reject})$) and Type II error ($P_{\theta_1}(\text{accept})$), encoded in the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Extensions to composite hypotheses require UMP (uniformly most powerful) tests, which exist under monotone likelihood ratio families (Lehmann & Romano, *Testing Statistical Hypotheses*, 2005) but generically fail in multi-parameter settings — an open structural question in higher dimensions. The *p*-value $p(x) = \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} P_\theta(T(X) \geq T(x))$ is a summary statistic, not a posterior probability; conflation of these is the source of ongoing replication-crisis debates (Wasserstein & Lazar, *The American Statistician*, 2016). Modern extensions include e-values and e-processes (Grünwald et al., 2023), which admit sequential testing without inflating Type I error — a sharp open problem when composite nulls interact with adaptively collected data.

**Suggested investigation.** Fix a one-parameter exponential family (e.g., Poisson with rate $\lambda$). Derive the UMP test for $H_0 : \lambda \leq \lambda_0$ vs.\ $H_1 : \lambda > \lambda_0$ analytically. Then simulate: for varying sample sizes $n \in \{10, 50, 200\}$ and true $\lambda \in [\lambda_0, 2\lambda_0]$, estimate the power curve $\beta(\lambda)$ via Monte Carlo. Compare your empirical curve against the exact power derived from the Poisson CDF. Finally, implement a sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) for the same hypotheses and compare stopping times against the fixed-$n$ design at matched error rates.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should: (1) state the hypothesis pair and model assumptions precisely before invoking any theorem; (2) present the analytical power curve alongside the Monte Carlo estimate with confidence bands; (3) record whether the empirical size exceeded $\alpha$ and explain any discrepancy as finite-sample or implementation artifact; (4) discuss what properties of the exponential family guarantee UMP existence and why these fail outside it.

### margin_of_error (29.9s)

## Margin Of Error

**Definition.** Let $\hat{\theta}$ be an estimator of an unknown parameter $\theta \in \Theta$, constructed from $n$ independent and identically distributed observations $X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim P_\theta$. The *margin of error* at confidence level $1 - \alpha$ is the half-width $\varepsilon$ of a confidence interval such that

$$P_\theta\!\left(|\hat{\theta} - \theta| \leq \varepsilon\right) \geq 1 - \alpha, \quad \forall\, \theta \in \Theta.$$

For the canonical case of estimating a population proportion $p$ via the sample proportion $\hat{p} = \bar{X}$, the Central Limit Theorem gives

$$\hat{p} \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(p,\, \frac{p(1-p)}{n}\right),$$

yielding the textbook margin of error

$$\varepsilon = z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}},$$

where $z_{\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)$ is the standard normal quantile. Setting $\hat{p} = 0.5$ (the maximum-variance worst case) gives the survey-design formula $\varepsilon = z_{\alpha/2}/(2\sqrt{n})$, leading to the well-known $n \approx 1{,}068$ for $\varepsilon = 0.03$, $\alpha = 0.05$.

**Theorem (Clopper–Pearson exactness).** The Wald interval above is an *asymptotic* interval; its actual coverage can fall below $1 - \alpha$ for finite $n$ or extreme $p$. The Clopper–Pearson interval is *exact* — it guarantees $P(\theta \in \text{CI}) \geq 1 - \alpha$ for every $n$ — but is conservative (wider than necessary). Blyth and Still (1983) showed that no symmetric interval can be simultaneously exact and shortest-in-expectation for all $p$.

**Literature context.** The apparent simplicity of the margin of error conceals an active research landscape. Brown, Cai, and DasGupta (2001, *Statistical Science*) provided a comprehensive comparison of seven intervals for binomial proportions, demonstrating that the Wilson score interval achieves near-nominal coverage with shorter expected width than Clopper–Pearson. In complex survey settings — stratified sampling, cluster sampling, nonresponse weighting — the design effect $D_{\text{eff}} = \text{Var}_{\text{complex}} / \text{Var}_{\text{SRS}}$ inflates the naive margin of error; ignoring it is a frequent source of error in published polling analysis (Lohr, *Sampling: Design and Analysis*, 2nd ed.). Open questions remain in adaptive and sequential designs: how to report a margin of error when the sample size is itself a stopping-rule-dependent random variable (see Jennison and Turnbull, *Group Sequential Methods*, 2000).

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a simulation study in Python or R comparing Wald, Wilson, Agresti–Coull, and Clopper–Pearson intervals across a grid of $(n, p) \in \{10, 50, 200, 1{,}000\} \times \{0.01, 0.05, 0.5\}$. For each configuration, draw $10{,}000$ Monte Carlo samples and record empirical coverage and mean interval width. Examine where Wald coverage dips most severely below $1 - \alpha$. As an extension, introduce a two-stage adaptive design: after $n_1$ observations, stop early if $\hat{p}$ is extreme ($< 0.02$ or $> 0.98$), otherwise continue to $n_2$. Assess whether naively applying the Wald formula at the final sample size maintains nominal coverage.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a concise statement of the coverage guarantee each interval claims and the conditions under which the guarantee holds; (2) annotated simulation plots of coverage versus $p$ for each $n$; (3) a table of mean widths alongside empirical coverage, so the coverage–width trade-off is visible; and (4) a brief analysis of the adaptive-design scenario with a clear statement of where the standard margin-of-error formula breaks down and what correction (if any) restores nominal coverage.

### anova (28.3s)

## Anova

**Definition.** Let $y_{ij}$ denote the $j$-th observation in group $i$, for $i = 1, \ldots, k$ and $j = 1, \ldots, n_i$, with $N = \sum_i n_i$. The one-way fixed-effects model is

$$y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad \varepsilon_{ij} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),$$

subject to the identifiability constraint $\sum_i n_i \alpha_i = 0$. The null hypothesis of interest is $H_0 : \alpha_1 = \cdots = \alpha_k = 0$, i.e., group membership carries no information beyond the grand mean $\mu$.

**Theorem (Variance Decomposition).** Under the model above, the total sum of squares decomposes orthogonally:

$$\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^2}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Total}}} = \underbrace{\sum_i n_i (\bar{y}_{i\cdot} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^2}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Between}}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{i\cdot})^2}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Within}}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Write $y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot} = (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{i\cdot}) + (\bar{y}_{i\cdot} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot})$. Squaring and summing, the cross-product vanishes because $\sum_j (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{i\cdot}) = 0$ for each $i$. $\square$

Under $H_0$, $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Between}}/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{k-1}$ and $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Within}}/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{N-k}$, independently by Cochran's theorem. The $F$-statistic

$$F = \frac{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Between}}/(k-1)}{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Within}}/(N-k)} \sim F_{k-1,\, N-k}$$

forms the canonical test. Under the alternative, the numerator follows a noncentral $\chi^2$ with noncentrality $\lambda = \sum_i n_i \alpha_i^2 / \sigma^2$, which governs power calculations.

**Literature context.** The decomposition originates with Fisher (1925) and is treated rigorously in Scheffé (1959). The Gaussian assumption is load-bearing: under heteroscedasticity, Welch's correction to degrees of freedom restores approximate $F$ validity (Welch 1951; Brown & Forsythe 1974). For non-Gaussian errors the Kruskal–Wallis test offers a rank-based alternative, but loses power when normality holds. A deeper open question concerns the tradeoff between robustness and power in high-dimensional settings where $k$ grows with $N$: classical $F$ theory breaks down when $k = O(N)$, and recent work (Cai & Xia 2014; Fan et al. 2020) develops corrected statistics under sparsity assumptions on $\{\alpha_i\}$. Multiple-comparison corrections post-$F$ (Tukey HSD, Bonferroni, Benjamini–Hochberg) remain an active methodological area, particularly in genomics where $k$ can reach $10^4$.

**Suggested investigation.** Generate synthetic balanced data ($k = 5$, $n_i = 20$) at several signal-to-noise ratios $\delta = \max_i |\alpha_i| / \sigma \in \{0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$ and empirically trace the power curve of the $F$-test against the theoretical noncentral-$F$ prediction. Then deliberately violate the homoscedasticity assumption (e.g., $\sigma_i^2 = i \cdot \sigma^2$) and compare the empirical Type-I error of the classical $F$-test versus Welch's correction. A natural extension is to apply Levene's test for equality of variances as a diagnostic preceding the omnibus $F$ test, and to quantify how that sequential testing inflates overall $\alpha$.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (i) the exact form of the noncentrality parameter and how it connects observed effect sizes to power; (ii) empirical validation of the $\chi^2$ marginal distributions of $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Between}}$ and $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Within}}$ via Q–Q plots; (iii) a table comparing nominal versus empirical Type-I error rates under heteroscedasticity, with a clear recommendation for when Welch's correction is necessary; and (iv) a brief discussion of which post-hoc procedure is appropriate given the study design and the researcher's tolerance for false-discovery inflation.

### confidence_interval (25.8s)

## Confidence Interval

**Definition.** Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. random variables with distribution $P_\theta$ indexed by an unknown parameter $\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ *confidence interval* is a pair of statistics $(L_n, U_n)$, both functions of the data, satisfying

$$P_\theta\!\left(L_n(X_1,\ldots,X_n) \leq \theta \leq U_n(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\right) \geq 1 - \alpha \quad \forall\, \theta \in \Theta.$$

The probability statement is over the randomness in the sample, not over $\theta$; this frequentist interpretation is routinely misread even in published work. A Bayesian *credible interval* satisfies $P(\theta \in [L,U] \mid X) \geq 1-\alpha$ with $\theta$ treated as a random variable under a prior — the two objects are numerically identical only under flat priors and special model families.

**Theorem (Wald interval for the mean).** Under the assumptions above with $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty$, the interval

$$\left[\bar{X}_n - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X}_n + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right]$$

achieves exactly $1-\alpha$ asymptotic coverage, where $z_{\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$.

*Proof sketch.* By the Central Limit Theorem, $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/\sigma \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The event $|\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/\sigma| \leq z_{\alpha/2}$ has asymptotic probability $1-\alpha$; solving for $\mu$ yields the stated interval. When $\sigma$ is unknown, substituting the sample standard deviation $S_n$ preserves the limit by Slutsky's theorem, with finite-sample exactness recovered under Gaussianity via Student's $t$-distribution. $\square$

**Literature context.** The Wald interval is textbook (Casella and Berger, §9.2), but its finite-sample defects are well-documented: coverage can drop catastrophically for bounded parameters — e.g., a binomial proportion $p$ near 0 or 1. Brown, Cai, and DasGupta (2001, *Statistical Science*) demonstrate that the Wilson score interval and the Agresti-Coull interval achieve substantially better coverage uniformity; this is an open *engineering* problem wherever small-sample guarantees matter. For multivariate parameters, the natural extension is a confidence *region* based on Wald, likelihood-ratio, or score statistics, with asymptotic equivalence at order $O(n^{-1})$ but distinct higher-order behavior — a territory where Barndorff-Nielsen and Cox (1994) remain the primary reference. Non-parametric settings introduce additional subtlety: the bootstrap offers distribution-free intervals (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993), yet bias-corrected accelerated (BCa) variants are needed to match parametric rates in skewed distributions. Current research probes whether conformal prediction intervals (Venn, 2005; Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023) can be viewed as a unifying framework that yields finite-sample, distribution-free coverage without the large-$n$ assumption.

**Suggested investigation.** Implement a Monte Carlo coverage study: fix a Bernoulli$(p)$ model, vary $p \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$ and $n \in \{10, 30, 100\}$, and compute empirical coverage across $10^4$ replicates for the Wald, Wilson, Clopper-Pearson (exact), and BCa bootstrap intervals. Plot coverage as a function of $p$ for each $n$. Extend the comparison to a heavy-tailed Pareto distribution to stress-test the CLT-based intervals.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a clear definition distinguishing coverage probability from posterior probability; (2) the asymptotic derivation for at least one interval method; (3) a table reporting empirical versus nominal coverage, with standard errors on the coverage estimates themselves; and (4) a discussion of when finite-sample exactness is practically necessary versus when asymptotic approximation suffices.

### linear_regression (25.9s)

## Linear Regression

**Definition.** Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ be a design matrix of $n$ observations on $p$ predictors, and let $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a response vector. The ordinary least-squares (OLS) linear regression model posits

$$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}),$$

where $\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is the unknown coefficient vector. The OLS estimator minimizes the residual sum of squares: $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$.

**Theorem (Normal Equations).** If $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ is invertible, the unique minimizer is

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}.$$

*Proof sketch.* The objective $L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$ is strictly convex in $\boldsymbol{\beta}$. Setting $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} L = -2\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{0}$ yields the normal equations $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$. Invertibility of $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ (equivalently, $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p$) guarantees a unique solution. $\square$

**Theorem (Gauss–Markov).** Under the assumption $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}] = \mathbf{0}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$, the OLS estimator $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE): among all linear unbiased estimators $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}$, the variance $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{c}^\top \tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \geq \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{c}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})$ for every $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^p$.

The proof constructs $\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{C} - (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top$ and shows unbiasedness forces $\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0}$; the excess variance $\sigma^2 \mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^\top \mathbf{c} \geq 0$ then follows by positive semi-definiteness.

**Literature context.** The classical theory is settled in standard references (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, *ESL*, Ch. 3; Boyd & Vandenberghe, *Convex Optimization*, Ch. 4). Open questions concern the high-dimensional regime $p \gg n$: here $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is singular, and the minimum-norm pseudoinverse solution $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^+\mathbf{y}$ has become central to the *double descent* phenomenon (Belkin et al., 2019; Bartlett et al., 2020). Why interpolating estimators generalize is not fully explained by classical bias-variance decomposition, and the implicit regularization induced by gradient descent on overparameterized models remains an active research frontier.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the double descent curve for OLS on a synthetic Gaussian design: fix $n = 200$, vary $p$ from $10$ to $500$, record test MSE at the interpolation threshold $p = n$ versus the classical and overparameterized regimes. Compare the pseudoinverse solution to ridge regression $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_\lambda = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ across the same sweep. Vary the signal-to-noise ratio $\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2 / \sigma$ to probe when overparameterization helps or hurts.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up should derive the normal equations and Gauss–Markov bound from first principles, then present the empirical double descent curve with error bars over repeated random splits. The report should make explicit which assumptions (Gaussian noise, isotropic design) are load-bearing and conjecture how relaxing them would affect the interpolation threshold — this positions the investigation within the ongoing literature rather than as a textbook exercise.

### python_statistical_computing (30.3s)

## Python Statistical Computing

**Definition.** Python statistical computing refers to the implementation of probabilistic and statistical algorithms in the Python ecosystem—primarily via NumPy, SciPy, and statsmodels—where mathematical operations on random variables, estimators, and probability distributions are realized as vectorized array computations. The central object is the $n \times p$ data matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, and the fundamental computational primitive is the BLAS/LAPACK-backed matrix routine invoked through `numpy.linalg` or `scipy.linalg`. The discipline is not merely applied statistics with a Python flavor; it encompasses a nontrivial intersection of floating-point arithmetic, algorithmic complexity, and the asymptotic theory of estimators.

**Theorem and Derivation.** Consider the ordinary least-squares estimator $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$. In exact arithmetic this is a closed-form expression, but in finite-precision computation the numerical stability depends critically on the condition number $\kappa(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}) = \kappa(\mathbf{X})^2$. If $\mathbf{X}$ has singular values $\sigma_1 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_p > 0$, then the relative forward error in $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ satisfies

$$\frac{\|\delta\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|} \lesssim \kappa(\mathbf{X})^2 \cdot \epsilon_{\text{mach}},$$

where $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ for IEEE 754 double precision. The standard remedy is to compute $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ via the QR decomposition $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}$, reducing the effective condition number to $\kappa(\mathbf{X})$ rather than $\kappa(\mathbf{X})^2$. In Python, `numpy.linalg.lstsq` invokes the LAPACK routine `_gelsd`, which uses a divide-and-conquer SVD and therefore achieves the optimal error bound $O(\kappa(\mathbf{X})\epsilon_{\text{mach}})$ at cost $O(np^2)$. The distinction between these approaches is not academic: for design matrices with $\kappa(\mathbf{X}) \sim 10^8$, the naive normal-equation route loses all significance in double precision.

**Literature Context.** The foundational numerical analysis is laid out in Golub and Van Loan (*Matrix Computations*, 4th ed.) and Higham (*Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*). The statistical side—how numerical error propagates into confidence intervals and hypothesis tests—is less thoroughly treated. Longley (1967) gave an early empirical study of ill-conditioning in econometric data; more recent work by McCullough and Vinod (1999) benchmarked statistical packages against certified reference datasets, finding substantial variation. Open questions remain: the interaction between regularization (ridge, LASSO) and numerical precision for correlated predictors in high dimensions is not fully characterized, and the behavior of iterative solvers (conjugate gradient, LSQR) relative to direct factorization for large sparse statistical problems is an active area.

**Suggested Investigation.** Generate a family of design matrices $\mathbf{X}_k$ with condition numbers $\kappa_k = 10^k$ for $k = 1, \ldots, 14$ (using controlled SVD construction) and compare the forward error of four solvers: (1) explicit normal equations via `numpy.linalg.solve`, (2) `numpy.linalg.lstsq`, (3) `scipy.linalg.lstsq` with `lapack_driver='gelsy'`, and (4) iterative `scipy.sparse.linalg.lsqr`. Measure error against a reference solution computed in 128-bit arithmetic via Python's `mpmath`. Plot $\log_{10}(\text{relative error})$ against $\log_{10}(\kappa)$ and verify the theoretical slopes of $2$ and $1$ for the two regimes.
