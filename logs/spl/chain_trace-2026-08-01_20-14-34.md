### audience (36.1s)

## Audience

**Definition.** In adaptive knowledge systems, an *audience* is not a demographic category but a formal specification of epistemic state. Let $K$ denote the set of all concepts in a domain and let $2^K$ denote its power set. A *knowledge state* is a subset $q \subseteq K$ representing the concepts a learner has mastered. An *audience* $\mathcal{A} \subseteq 2^K$ is a collection of knowledge states — the population of learners a piece of content is designed to serve. This framing originates in Knowledge Space Theory (KST), introduced by Doignon and Falmagne (1985), where the central object is a *knowledge space*: a pair $(K, \mathcal{Q})$ with $\mathcal{Q} \subseteq 2^K$ closed under union, encoding the set of reachable knowledge states given a prerequisite structure on $K$.

**Theorem (Audience-Concept Compatibility).** Let $G = (K, E)$ be a directed acyclic prerequisite graph and let $\mathcal{Q}_G$ be the associated knowledge space (states closed under taking prerequisite-satisfying subsets). A concept $c \in K$ is *reachable* from audience $\mathcal{A}$ if and only if every knowledge state $q \in \mathcal{A}$ contains the predecessors $\mathrm{pred}_G(c) = \{k \in K : k \to^* c\}$ as a subset. Formally,
$$c \text{ is reachable from } \mathcal{A} \iff \forall q \in \mathcal{A},\; \mathrm{pred}_G(c) \subseteq q.$$

*Proof.* By definition of $\mathcal{Q}_G$, a learner in state $q$ can acquire $c$ only if all prerequisites of $c$ are already in $q$. The condition is necessary: missing any prerequisite makes $c$ unreachable for at least one member of $\mathcal{A}$. It is sufficient: if $\mathrm{pred}_G(c) \subseteq q$ for all $q \in \mathcal{A}$, then for each such $q$, the state $q \cup \{c\}$ is a valid successor state in $\mathcal{Q}_G$. $\square$

**Literature context.** Doignon and Falmagne's monograph (1999) establishes the combinatorial foundations; Heller et al. (2006) extend KST to probabilistic settings, giving rise to *Probabilistic Knowledge Structures* in which each state $q$ carries a probability $\pi(q)$ — precisely the distribution over an audience. Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (Corbett & Anderson, 1994) offers a sequential state-estimation model but lacks the global topological structure of KST. More recently, neural approaches to knowledge tracing (Piech et al., 2015, DKT) model audience state as a hidden vector, sacrificing interpretability for expressiveness. A central open question is whether the lattice structure of $\mathcal{Q}_G$ can be recovered from interaction data alone — an inverse problem connecting combinatorics to identifiability theory.

**Suggested investigation.** Take a small domain (e.g., undergraduate linear algebra: 15–20 concepts). Elicit a prerequisite DAG from a textbook or expert, construct $\mathcal{Q}_G$, and define three distinct audience distributions $\pi_1, \pi_2, \pi_3$ (novice-heavy, intermediate, expert-heavy). For a target concept $c$, compute the *expected instructional gap* $\mathbb{E}_{q \sim \pi}[|\mathrm{pred}_G(c) \setminus q|]$ for each audience. Verify that the minimal prerequisite cover differs across audiences and design three distinct learning paths accordingly. Then simulate a DKT model on synthetic response data and ask: does the learned hidden state recover the lattice boundaries of $\mathcal{Q}_G$?

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should formally define the prerequisite graph and knowledge space used, state the audience distributions and justify their construction, derive the expected instructional gap analytically, and provide a table comparing path lengths and required prerequisite remediation across the three audiences. It should conclude with a remark on whether interpretable (KST) and black-box (DKT) audience models agree on path recommendations — and where they diverge.

### data_visualization_choice (30.2s)

## Data Visualization Choice

**Definition.** A *visualization mapping* is a function $\phi: \mathcal{D} \times \mathcal{T} \to \mathcal{V}$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is the space of datasets (typed relational schemas), $\mathcal{T}$ is a space of analytical tasks (e.g., comparison, distribution estimation, correlation detection), and $\mathcal{V}$ is a space of visual encodings (mark types $\times$ retinal variable assignments). The *visualization choice problem* asks: among all $\phi$ consistent with data type and task constraints, which minimizes a cost functional that combines perceptual error, expressive completeness, and cognitive load?

**Formal framework.** Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a univariate sample drawn from an unknown distribution $F$. A visualization encodes a statistic $T(X)$ via a retinal channel $c$ (position, length, angle, area, hue, saturation). Cleveland and McGill (1984) experimentally established a partial order on perceptual accuracy:

$$\text{position} \succ \text{length} \succ \text{angle} \succ \text{area} \succ \text{color saturation} \succ \text{hue}$$

where $A \succ B$ means channel $A$ supports lower mean absolute error in magnitude judgment. Formally, if $\hat{T}_c$ denotes the viewer's decoded estimate of $T$ under channel $c$, then $\mathbb{E}[|\hat{T}_c - T|] \leq \mathbb{E}[|\hat{T}_{c'} - T|]$ for $c \succ c'$ across a wide range of stimuli. Mackinlay (1986) extended this into an automated presentation system (APT), defining *expressiveness* — an encoding expresses exactly the facts in the data — and *effectiveness* — ranked by the Cleveland–McGill order. APT selects $\phi^* = \arg\max_{\phi \in \Phi_{\text{expressive}}} \text{rank}_{\text{eff}}(\phi)$.

For multivariate data $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, channel assignment becomes a combinatorial problem. If $\pi: [p] \to \mathcal{C}$ denotes a channel allocation over a set $\mathcal{C}$ of available retinal variables, then selecting $\pi$ to maximize mutual information $I(X_j; \hat{X}_j)$ for each dimension $j$ subject to channel distinguishability constraints is NP-hard in general (Tableau Research, 2020; Moritz et al., 2019 — *Formalizing Visualization Design Knowledge as Constraints*).

**Literature context.** Munzner's nested model (2009) separates domain characterization, data/task abstraction, encoding/interaction idiom, and algorithm — providing a normative hierarchy that, while influential, does not yield a computable optimality criterion. Vega-Lite (Satyanarayan et al., 2017) operationalizes a fragment of Mackinlay's theory as a declarative grammar with partial automation. Recent work frames visualization recommendation as learning: VizML (Hu et al., 2019) trains a neural classifier on $10^5$ Plotly charts, outperforming rule-based systems on held-out pairs. Luo et al. (2021) formulate it as a multi-armed bandit where reward is task completion time. Open questions include: (1) whether the Cleveland–McGill order is invariant across data distributions and tasks beyond magnitude comparison; (2) whether learned recommendation systems generalize to novel domain schemas; (3) how to incorporate uncertainty visualization into the expressiveness criterion.

**Suggested investigation.** Replicate the Cleveland–McGill experiment on a modern crowdsourcing platform (Prolific or MTurk) with a controlled factorial design: five retinal channels $\times$ three distribution types (uniform, bimodal, heavy-tailed) $\times$ two tasks (point estimation, rank ordering). Compute $\text{RMSE}$ per cell and test whether the partial order $\succ$ is statistically stable via a Friedman test with post-hoc Nemenyi correction. As an extension, implement a greedy APT variant and measure its alignment with both the empirical order and VizML recommendations on a public corpus (e.g., Vega datasets repository).

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should: state the null hypothesis that channel order is distribution-invariant; present the RMSE matrix with confidence intervals; report the Friedman statistic and any violations of the canonical order; and discuss implications for automated recommendation when the data distribution is unknown at design time.

### executive_dashboard (29.8s)

## Executive Dashboard

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal{S} = \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the *organizational state space*, where each coordinate represents a time-indexed operational signal (revenue, latency, headcount, defect rate, etc.) observed over a horizon $T$. An *executive dashboard* is a measurable map

$$
\phi: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{T} \to \mathcal{D}
$$

where $\mathcal{D}$ is a low-dimensional *display space* (typically $|\mathcal{D}| \ll d$) and $\mathcal{T}$ is a time domain. The map $\phi$ is constructed to preserve decision-relevant structure while discarding operational detail. The central research question is not visual design but *epistemic compression*: under what conditions does $\phi$ induce a *sufficient reduction* of $\mathcal{S}$ for the executive's decision problem?

**Theorem (Sufficiency of Dashboard Reduction).** Let $\theta \in \Theta$ be a latent organizational parameter (strategy effectiveness, systemic risk, etc.) and let $X_{1:d}$ be observable signals with joint density $p(X_{1:d} \mid \theta)$. A statistic $T(X_{1:d}) = \phi(X_{1:d})$ is *sufficient* for $\theta$ if and only if the conditional distribution $p(X_{1:d} \mid T, \theta) = p(X_{1:d} \mid T)$ — that is, no residual information about $\theta$ remains in the raw signals once $T$ is known. By the Fisher–Neyman factorization theorem, this holds iff

$$
p(x_{1:d} \mid \theta) = h(x_{1:d}) \cdot g(T(x_{1:d}), \theta)
$$

for some non-negative functions $h, g$. The *information loss* of dashboard $\phi$ is then quantifiable as the mutual information gap $I(\theta; X_{1:d}) - I(\theta; \phi(X_{1:d})) \geq 0$, with equality characterizing a lossless (sufficient) dashboard.

**Literature context.** The sufficiency framing connects classical statistics (Fisher 1922; Lehmann & Scheffé 1950) to modern organizational theory. Galbraith's *information processing view of organizations* (1974) anticipated this formally: organizations are understood as bounded-information systems whose hierarchical aggregation must match the uncertainty of the task environment. More recent work in *management control systems* (Merchant & Van der Stede) and *balanced scorecard theory* (Kaplan & Norton 1996) offers normative aggregation heuristics but rarely derives optimality conditions. The algorithmic side intersects with sufficient dimensionality reduction (Cook 1998; Li 1991 via sliced inverse regression), principal component analysis under structured covariance, and causally-grounded aggregation (Peters, Mooij, Schölkopf 2017). An open question of practical importance: *when organizational signals exhibit non-stationary or heavy-tailed behavior, standard PCA-based aggregation systematically under-represents tail risk — is there a minimax-optimal dashboard under distributional shift?*

**Suggested investigation.** Select a publicly available organizational dataset (e.g., a multi-KPI time-series from the M-competitions, or SEC filings with quarterly operational metrics). Fix a proxy for $\theta$ (e.g., next-quarter earnings surprise as a latent organizational health signal). Fit a sufficient reduction $T$ using sliced inverse regression alongside a baseline PCA dashboard, and measure $I(\theta; T_{\text{SIR}}) - I(\theta; T_{\text{PCA}})$ using a $k$-nearest-neighbor mutual information estimator (Kraskov et al. 2004). Additionally, perturb the signal distribution (covariate shift) and record how dashboard sufficiency degrades. This constitutes a controlled ablation on the information-theoretic quality of competing aggregation strategies.

**Report-writing note.** A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a precise formalization of the decision problem and what $\theta$ represents, (2) the factorization check or MI estimate demonstrating sufficiency or its failure for each method, (3) the empirical degradation curve under distributional shift, and (4) a discussion of whether the sufficiency gap is practically significant relative to the executive's stated decision horizon — connecting the mathematical result back to organizational consequences.
